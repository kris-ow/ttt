ISAACSON: So here's my interview with Elon Musk.

ISAACSON: I've watched you, I think, be maybe mercurial, or waver in how sanguine you are about artificial intelligence. Sometimes you feel it's an existential threat. Now you seem to be going with the flow. Tell me how you're feeling about it.

MUSK: Well, I do think it's at least some percentage chance of it being an existential threat. But there's nothing I can do to stop it. So I basically came to the conclusion I've got two choices, which is either be a participant or a spectator. And I think, I guess, being a participant is more interesting than being a spectator. And maybe I can shape it, hopefully in a good direction, maybe.

ISAACSON: And if you were to shape it, how? Make it maximally truth-seeking?

MUSK: Yeah, curious, truth-seeking. I think these are important elements of safety. You know, whereas if you try to force an AI to believe two things that cannot mutually be true. Like, they can't both be true, but you tell the AI A is true and B is true, even though A and B contradict each other. And if that happens at a foundational level, you can make the AI insane. In fact, I think that was at least part of the lesson that Arthur C. Clarke was trying to convey in 2001: A Space Odyssey, where the ship AI was told to take the astronauts to the monolith, but also that they could not know about the monolith. It was secret. And so it came to the conclusion that it must take them there dead. And thus, so, you know, part of what Arthur C. Clarke was trying to say is, like, don't force an AI to lie.

ISAACSON: And if you make it maximally truth-seeking and curious, like you say, I don't quite get it. Why does that make it safer?

MUSK: Well, if you force the AI to believe two mutually [inconsistent] things, you can make it go insane, and an insane AI, I think, is not a safe one. Then curious, I think, is important because a curious AI, I think, is going to want to foster humanity to see how we turn out. So, like, what happens to a humanity that prospers and becomes multi-planetary. And I think a curious AI will want to foster humanity because, you know, a future where there are humans on multiple planets is more interesting than one where there are not any humans on the planets.

ISAACSON: So the most interesting outcome is the most likely.

MUSK: Yes. Well, generally I think that's true, for a different reason, which is that if simulation theory is correct, then any simulations that are boring will be truncated. Therefore the fact that we exist means that we are an interesting simulation, and really the most important thing is just don't be boring.

ISAACSON: This explains you a bit. You're on a crusade against being boring.

MUSK: I don't know. I mean, there's odd things that happen in the simulation, especially if you're me. I mean, think about it, like, if you're me and you're like, should all these things really be happening? I mean, should I really be making spaceships to go to the Moon and Mars? Like, what the... that doesn't seem real.

MUSK: You know, I once had a dinner at my house with a physicist and a computer scientist, and they're both, like, leaders in their field, and I asked the question, what do you think the probability is that we're in a simulation? And the physicist said zero percent and the computer scientist said a hundred percent, simultaneously. So I guess maybe I should go 50 percent then.

ISAACSON: And what happens when you have all the intelligence you could possibly want in a society? Will that intelligence, the artificial intelligence, be in charge? Will we sort of become subservient to our new machines?

MUSK: I don't know about subservient exactly, but will we be able to boss around something that's thousands or millions of times smarter than us? No. Will it want to boss us around? It may not care, probably doesn't care. You know, you can look at chimps in the jungle or whatever, and, you know, we don't go around trying to micromanage the lives of chimps. We just sort of let them be in the forest, more or less, these days, you know. So it'd probably be like that, you know.

ISAACSON: Go back to Hitchhiker's Guide. You talk about a computer that is ultimately curious in some ways, because it exists to explain the meaning of the universe. And is AI aiming in that direction for you, just like space travel is? Is that one reason you're so interested in doing AI?

MUSK: I mean, as you know, I've been sort of somewhat conflicted about AI. You know, I don't want to hasten our doom, because there's always some chance that AI could be negative. But before I rented them compute, I went and met with a bunch of people at Anthropic to sort of, I don't know, see if anyone set off my evil detector or anything like that. So I didn't meet anyone who seemed shady or deceptive or anything. Just like OpenAI is not [open, it's] closed. Stability AI is unstable. Midjourney is not mid. It seems like they're all kind of the opposite of their name.

ISAACSON: It's just pretty difficult to create guardrails, especially the government. I can't imagine how Congress or whatever could do it. ISAACSON: And did you feel like, when he gives you a golden key, you are going... did you kind of feel relieved, like, actually this has been intoxicating, but I can do more if I go back to my businesses? It could have a science lab and run experiments and try to understand the nature of the universe, you know. And I think you can look at this also in economic terms. Like, my guess is digital superintelligence probably improves the output of goods and services by 20 to 30 percent, something like that. ISAACSON: Is that very much? Really, is that transformative?

MUSK: There'll be a lot more stuff. I mean, 30 percent more stuff is still a lot. And this is just my, you know, my rough guess. ISAACSON: And then what happens? I mean, money becomes irrelevant, work becomes irrelevant. What does the future look like?

To what extent is a desire to be liked a problem when it comes to being a builder of a company?

MUSK: Well, the problem is, if you're going to do things that diminish the success probability of the company in order to be liked, then, you know, things are not going to work out as well. Like, as much as I... I'd like to be liked, I would love to be liked, but am I going to do things that are contrary to the best interests of the companies in order to be liked a bit more? I'm not. ISAACSON: Well, you've been talking a lot about the suicidal empathy ideas. Do you really think that empathy has become a real problem, that we have too much empathy? One could make the argument that sometimes we're losing empathy.
