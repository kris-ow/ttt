Lars Moravy, Tesla's Vice President of Vehicle Engineering, sat down with Herbert and supply chain executive Jeff Lutz for the Brighter with Herbert platform in an in-person recorded interview on June 30, 2026. The conversation ranged across Cybercab production ramping, Optimus manufacturing buildout, Tesla's AI integration inside factories, competitive philosophy, and vertical integration strategy. The single most newsworthy disclosure was Moravy teasing a "major announcement" tied to the Giga Texas campus scheduled for Tuesday, July 7, 2026, which he said is part of Tesla's broader scaling effort for Cybercab — while declining to elaborate further.

## Cybercab Production Ramp

1. **Lines installed and running, majority of units going to training:** Moravy confirmed that Cybercab production lines at Giga Texas are installed and operational. He said the majority of Cybercabs produced so far have gone into the autonomous training fleet — vehicles fitted with steering wheels and pedals, which he noted with some irony carry the Cybertruck emblem. He described the current phase as a "burn-in process" on a dedicated burn-in track built on the edge of the Giga Texas campus across the highway, explicitly framing every unit built as simultaneously a production unit and a test unit.

2. **~150 units in a burst build; automation "ahead of schedule":** When the interviewer cited a figure of approximately 150 Cybercabs observed, Moravy said "We maybe made 150 — I'll stick with that number." He was careful to distinguish between burst-build quantities and continuous production pace. He added that the automation side is "ahead of schedule," noting he was surprised by the speeds already achievable, and that lines are "over 90% automated all in." He said the teams continue to speed up the automation and work through issues as they build more continuously.

3. **"Production hell" scars as a management advantage:** Moravy said the primary cultural difference in the Cybercab ramp versus earlier programs (Model 3, Cybertruck) is "fear" — deep organizational memory of production hell — combined with significantly more virtual commissioning. He described building everything virtually before machines go online, coding within the virtual environment, and not shipping machines without pre-testing, resulting in better first-time performance. He contrasted this with Model T/Model A and noted Tesla's Model Y global four-factory simultaneous changeover "last year" (i.e., 2025) took three to four weeks, which he called perhaps the largest automated production changeover since Ford's 1914 transition.

4. **Scaling ambition and platform status:** Moravy stated Tesla believes it will build more Cybercabs than anything else it has ever built, positioning it as its own platform. He used the analogy of hard disk drives being displaced by solid-state flash memory — everything about the new technology was better (cheaper, faster, more reliable, more durable) with only initial cost as a drawback, and hard disks largely disappeared within ten to fifteen years. He said Cybercab is in a similar position for transportation: cheaper, safer, more reliable, more efficient. He said whether other vehicles need to be built on the Cybercab platform will depend on adoption rates of autonomous vehicles.

5. **Teaser for July 7 announcement:** Moravy voluntarily disclosed — twice in the transcript — that "in a week from Tuesday, there'll be some cool news about things happening on the campus of Texas," referring to July 7, 2026. He linked this directly to the scaling effort for Cybercab and declined to give any further detail.

6. **Efficiency as the underestimated variable:** When asked what people are most underestimating about Cybercab, Moravy said it is efficiency — not in watt-hours per mile, but in the end-to-end cost from raw material in to vehicle out. He said published autonomous vehicle business forecasts from firms like ARK or Wells Fargo do not actually understand the operational cost structure that Tesla will achieve. He attributed this to Tesla's uniquely vertical position: sourcing metal from ingot and pellets, building compute in-house, writing its own software, owning charging infrastructure, and owning service. He also said he believes vehicle efficiency still has roughly 10–15% to cut, naming mass efficiency as a personal passion.

7. **Consumer sales bet reference:** The host raised Elon Musk's publicized bet with MKBHD that a Cybercab would be sold to a consumer by end of 2026. Moravy declined to engage with the specifics of the bet, saying "I don't know what Elon's doing these days in terms of bets," but affirmed: "we made a product, it's safe, it's functionally safe, it's built on there and the deployment will happen."

## Optimus Production Buildout (Fremont)

1. **First line landed and install underway:** Moravy confirmed that the first Optimus production line has "landed" at the Fremont factory, with installation already underway. He said the automation groups building the equipment are based in the Midwest and in Germany, and those machines are going through the final stages of FAT (Field Acceptance Testing) — getting parts to the automation groups to test machines before shipping to Fremont.

2. **Modular line design enabling rapid commissioning:** Because Tesla knew the Optimus line would require frequent changes, it was designed to be modular. Moravy said the smaller physical scale of Optimus (relative to a car) means commissioning can be very fast: theoretically, a line can be taken down, shipped, reassembled, powered on, and back running within two to three days, with full operation restored within about a week. He said this is "proving out" on the first line.

3. **"40 more to go":** Moravy said there are approximately 40 more Optimus production lines to install after the first. He contextualized this by noting the large number of subsystems — actuators, limbs, torso, battery — each requiring dedicated lines. He described the layout as tributary/unboxed in philosophy, feeding into a small final assembly, similar to Cybercab's architecture but adapted for a 3D multi-axis product.

4. **Manufacturing expertise fully transfers:** Moravy was emphatic that Tesla's automotive manufacturing expertise — supply chain management, multi-sourcing, vendor management, material flow, force majeure robustness — transfers entirely to Optimus. He noted the people designing the Optimus lines are the same individuals who designed Tesla's car lines, and the automation groups building the equipment are the same groups. He also stated that the bill-of-materials cost of a car is higher than a robot, meaning car manufacturing actually demands higher precision and uptime standards than Optimus, making Tesla's experience directly and favorably applicable.

5. **Three competitive advantages for Optimus:** Moravy reiterated (attributing the framing to Elon Musk) that Tesla has three things other humanoid robot companies lack: (1) scale manufacturing capability, (2) proprietary high-power-dense actuators and electric motors that no one else has at volume, and (3) real-world AI implementation. He said: "You only need three for a chair, so it's probably good enough."

6. **Humanoid form factor first, then expansion:** Moravy said Tesla's current focus for Optimus is on "limbic resonance" — making the robot feel like it belongs in human spaces so people become comfortable with it. He said once society accepts robots in workplaces and homes, expansion into other form factors (what he called the "R2-D2 world") will follow naturally. He said it is important to start with the C3PO humanoid form factor.

## AI Integration Inside Tesla's Operations

1. **Four key factory and engineering AI use cases:** Moravy outlined four main internal AI applications: (a) a proprietary internal agent aggregating decades of engineering lessons learned — failure data, test results, design history — so any engineer can search it instantly rather than hunting through people or physical archives; (b) anomaly detection and trending on manufacturing KPIs, particularly catching confluence-of-events failures that spec limits alone would not flag, led by a quality data team he called out specifically; (c) AI-linked manufacturing operating systems that now "think to each other" — for example, body-fit scan data from end-of-line immediately triggering upstream process changes, which Moravy connected to Tesla reaching number three in J.D. Power IQS (announced approximately two days before the interview); (d) service knowledge propagation — a fix discovered by one service technician in one city is now surfaced automatically to technicians everywhere, reducing service times.

2. **"Full Self-Hearing" as a concrete example:** Moravy shared that Tesla cars now drive themselves autonomously from the end of the production line to the logistics yard, and also drive themselves over the bump, squeak, and rattle test track, using cabin microphones to listen for noises and document findings for production teams. He called this internally "full self-hearing." He noted it requires training to map frequencies from locations to specific root causes (e.g., loose door panel clips), with human review still in the loop for edge cases.

3. **Software teams and code generation:** Moravy said Tesla's software teams have been "massively benefited" by AI code generation for some time, using it to produce base code before engineers tackle the hard differentiated work. He also mentioned a company-wide morning digest tool that prepares all 140,000 employees with their schedules and relevant pre-reading.

4. **Internal trials of Macrohard:** When asked whether Tesla is "eating its own dog food" with tools like Macrohard, Moravy said "we are trialing certain things within the company" and left it at that, declining to elaborate.

## Competitive Philosophy and Manufacturing Culture

1. **Waymo efficiency comparison:** Moravy said Waymo's approach — buying a car and adding compute on top — is "probably not efficient enough." He acknowledged that Zoox "gets it" because they also started from a clean sheet. He positioned Cybercab as the correct approach for long-term cost-per-mile competitiveness.

2. **Flat engineering leadership as execution engine:** Moravy emphasized that Tesla's speed and willingness to take calculated risks derives from engineers leading engineers, without layers of program managers or product managers. He gave the example of a vehicle dynamics engineer emailing him and the head of firmware with FSD UX ideas and receiving substantive replies within twenty minutes. He described this as "servant leadership" that is often misunderstood about Tesla.

3. **Rolling changes and risk framing:** On Tesla's practice of rolling changes into live Model 3 and Y production lines, Moravy reframed "risk" as probability of success or failure — arguing that when you understand the impact and can assess probability of improvement, better judgments follow. He said Tesla's leaders understand the engineering deeply enough to make those calls without MBA-style risk frameworks.

4. **Vertical integration and tariff resilience:** Moravy said Tesla's vertical integration — including designing its own PCBs — was decisive during COVID chip shortages (enabling real-time component substitutions over phone calls with suppliers). He noted Tesla placed number one and two on the American-Made Index, which he attributed to understanding supply chains down to tier-three and tier-four levels. He mentioned Tesla shuffled which factory served Canada three times in 2026 in response to tariff changes, calling localization an effective buffer.

5. **SpaceX collaboration:** Moravy said he communicates regularly with SpaceX counterparts (naming Bill, Mike, and John without surnames) and described the two companies as "one mindset." He confirmed Starlink is being explored as a connectivity option for Cybercab, particularly for rural areas. He mentioned SpaceX uses Tesla drive units on Starship, and referenced Terafab as a joint project that is "back together again." He also briefly mentioned the Roadster and ongoing manufacturing integration discussions.

## Five-Year Outlook

1. **Real-world AI everywhere:** When asked what people would be most surprised Tesla accomplished in five years, Moravy said: "The amount of real-world AI that's going to be around you because of us is going to be mind-blowing." He specified this means robotaxis, Cybercabs, Optimus in workplaces and homes, and "everything else we have planned that we aren't even talking about."
