This video is a deep-dive conversation between FutureAzA host Brian and Jordan from The Limiting Factor, focused on Tesla's 4680 battery cell — where they're made, whether production targets are achievable, energy density competitiveness, and how supply chains for cells and packs are evolving. The discussion covers the role of Kato Road, Giga Texas, Giga Berlin, and Giga Nevada in Tesla's battery strategy, and how 4680s fit into the broader product roadmap including the Cybercab, Semi, and Optimus. A secondary theme is the "visibility problem" that causes outside observers to underestimate both Tesla's battery progress and competitors like Panasonic.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **4680 Energy Density Is Globally Competitive, Not Best-in-Class:** Jordan clarifies that Tesla's current 4680 cell achieves approximately 270 watt-hours per kilogram, placing it between the last generation of Panasonic and LG Chem cells (250–270 Wh/kg) and the newest generation of those cells (280–290+ Wh/kg). At the pack level, the new Model Y in Germany equipped with 4680s delivers roughly 5% less range than vehicles using the newest competing packs, though Jordan frames this as a value-for-money trade-off since the 4680-equipped vehicle is cheaper and buyers have other options.

2. **Giga Texas 4680 Line Status:** Joe Tegtmeyer's recent observation of the Giga Texas facility suggested roughly four lines visible but only approximately one and a half actually running. Notably, battery line equipment that had previously been stored outside has since moved indoors, leading Brian and Jordan to conclude that additional lines are being installed in the northwest corner of Giga Texas. Jordan assesses that Giga Texas should have sufficient space and equipment to meet production needs for that facility and nearby buildings, including a new Optimus facility reportedly under construction on the north campus.

3. **Giga Berlin Cells Not Yet Produced Locally:** The battery facility at Giga Berlin is advancing but does not appear to be producing cells yet. Current production involves importing cells and assembling modules in Germany. Tesla is reportedly targeting cell production at Giga Berlin beginning next year (2027). Jordan notes this timeline illustrates the complexity of manufacturing 4680s at precision scale — a challenge that has also stymied other major battery manufacturers who announced 4680 programs but have not brought competing cells to market.

4. **Kato Road Remains an Advanced Engineering Testbed:** Tesla's original Kato Road pilot line has been converted into a testing and advanced engineering facility. Jordan confirms this is consistent with what Tesla has officially communicated — it serves as a proving ground for cell advancements before they are transferred to mass production lines in Austin and eventually Germany.

5. **2170 Cells Likely to Continue for Existing Models:** Jordan's working assumption is that 2170 cells will continue supplying current passenger vehicle platforms (Model Y, etc.), as re-engineering packs and supply chains for those vehicles would be costly and unnecessary. A source Jordan cited on X indicated Tesla plans to upgrade the 2170 cell within approximately 18 months to improve charging speed and achieve similar or slightly better energy density.

6. **Panasonic Opens New 2170 Facility in Kansas City:** Panasonic has brought online a new 2170 cell facility in Kansas City. Brian and Jordan interpret this as capacity primarily destined for Tesla, reinforcing the thesis that 2170s will continue serving passenger EVs while 4680s are directed toward the Semi, Cybercab, and Optimus.

## Robotaxi

1. **Cybercab Battery Pack Prototyping at Nevada:** During a tour of Giga Nevada, a tour guide explained the facility has three types of pack assembly lines — a high-speed automated line, a mid-speed line used as backup when the fast line goes down, and a fully manual line used for prototyping. As an example of the manual line's purpose, the guide specifically cited being able to produce 23 Cybercab battery packs on demand — indicating small-batch, custom pack work for programs still in development.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus Building Under Construction at Giga Texas North Campus:** A dedicated Optimus facility is reportedly being constructed on the north campus of Giga Texas. Jordan confirmed this when discussing whether Giga Texas's 4680 production capacity would be sufficient to supply both vehicle and robot needs at and near the site.

2. **Visibility Problem Distorts Optimus Competitive Comparisons:** Brian draws a parallel between public perception of Optimus versus competing humanoid robots, and the earlier perception gap around Dojo versus established chip designers. An IBM senior chip engineer Brian interviewed at CES noted that Tesla's practice is to show things 5–7 years ahead of current competitor roadmaps, while most chip and hardware companies do not publicize future work. The same dynamic applies to Optimus: absence of recent public demos does not mean the program is stagnant.

## Energy

1. **Tesla Likely Still Importing Most Megapack Cells:** Jordan states that to his knowledge, Tesla is still importing the majority of battery cells used in Megapack. He contrasts this with the vehicle cell situation, where he believes domestic supply is adequate. He notes that many EV cell production lines from manufacturers that have abandoned EV programs are being repurposed for energy storage cells, reflecting stronger demand in that segment.

2. **Megapack Factory Uses LFP Chemistry:** Brian recounts touring the Megapack factory and confirms through direct observation that the packs use LFP (lithium iron phosphate) chemistry. He notes Tesla has only approximately 10 gigawatt-hours of LFP capacity, while one supplier (unnamed in transcript) produces 30–40 gigawatt hours per year — indicating Tesla is dependent on external LFP supply and that 10 GWh is insufficient to meet Megapack demand internally.

3. **Battery Constraint Is at Pack Level, Not Cell Level:** At recent earnings calls, Tesla has cited battery constraints. Jordan analyzed this and determined the bottleneck is not cells (of which there is adequate supply) but battery packs. Tesla confirmed this in a follow-up on a recent earnings call. Jordan attributes the pack constraint to Tesla transitioning previously outsourced pack production in-house to reduce costs, and to ongoing upgrades of older, less efficient pack assembly lines at Giga Nevada — a process analogous to the production dip seen during the Model Y to Model Y Juniper transition in 2025.

4. **LFP and High-Nickel Cathode Manufacturing Can Share Equipment But Not Lines:** Jordan explains that LFP and NMC cells differ primarily in cathode chemistry, and that Tesla's cathode manufacturing equipment in Austin is technically capable of producing either. However, he expects Tesla will run completely separate dedicated lines for each chemistry rather than swapping between them, for the same efficiency reasons that separate gigacasting machines are used for front and rear castings.

## Financials

1. **IRA Tax Credits: $35/kWh for Cell Manufacturing:** Jordan confirms that by manufacturing 4680 cells domestically, Tesla qualifies for a $35 per kilowatt-hour production tax credit. An additional $10 per kilowatt-hour credit is available for domestic battery pack manufacturing. Brian calculates that the $10 pack credit equates to approximately $700 per vehicle, suggesting that if Tesla ships cells to Germany and packages them there, they forgo that $700 per-car benefit — implying there must be at least equivalent savings in the current arrangement to justify it.
