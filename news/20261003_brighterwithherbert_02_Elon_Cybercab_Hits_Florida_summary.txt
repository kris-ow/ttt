This is a weekly recap covering Tesla's Q3 delivery beat and inventory drawdown, Elon Musk naming new states for Cybercab expansion, European regulatory friction over FSD speed behavior, a new $30B Tesla credit facility and shareholder voting program, and SpaceX's first Starship orbital flight alongside competing valuation models for its AI-compute ambitions.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Q3 deliveries beat estimates while inventory fell:** Tesla produced 464,391 vehicles and delivered 486,532 in Q3, versus a Tesla-compiled analyst consensus of roughly 461,000–462,000 — about 24,500 ahead of forecast (~5%). Deliveries exceeded production by about 22,000 units, marking the second straight quarter of inventory decline (following a roughly 21,000–28,000 unit drop in Q2, ~50,000 over two quarters); one commentator (Jeff Lutz) estimated supply at about 11 days, though the host notes this is his estimate, not an official figure. Deliveries were still down 2.1% year-over-year against a prior-year quarter inflated by the expiring $7,500 federal EV tax credit; Sawyer Merritt noted this was Tesla's best quarter for deliveries without that credit, and Nick Cruz Batayne called it the third-best quarter in company history. Gary Black attributed strength partly to global gasoline prices and expects full-year estimates to rise after the earnings call.
2. **Energy storage missed expectations:** Tesla deployed 13.7 gigawatt-hours of storage in Q3, up about 10% year-over-year and the second-best quarter on record, but below a market expectation of roughly 15.9 GWh — a shortfall of a little over 2 GWh.

## Robotaxi
1. **Musk names next robotaxi states:** In a Chinese TV interview last Friday, Musk said Cybercab robotaxi service is already commercial in Texas and will soon expand to Florida and Nevada (Florida currently runs Model Y-based robotaxis, so "soon" refers to Cybercab specifically), with additional states to follow and likely commercial operation in California by mid-next year (pending a drone/permit application Tesla has not yet submitted). Nevada has held a permit since August 20 with a 120-day rule requiring service to start by roughly December 18. The host flags that Musk previously said Nevada and Florida launches would happen by the end of last year, so timing should be treated skeptically even as direction is usually credible.
2. **Hiring posted across 55 cities:** A review of Tesla's careers page found robotaxi-related operator job postings in 55 cities — 36 in the US (including Phoenix, five California cities, Denver, Washington D.C., three Florida cities, Atlanta, Chicago, Nashville, four Texas cities, Seattle area, Reno) plus international cities (London, Dublin, Prague, Warsaw, Brisbane, Melbourne, Auckland, Hong Kong, Singapore, Taipei, two in Korea, Tel Aviv, Doha, Mumbai, and cities in Colombia and Argentina). The host characterizes these largely as early data-collection roles rather than imminent launches, noting Tesla posted New York jobs a year before activity there.
3. **Texas fleet and mileage tracker update:** The Texas registry showed 578 total vehicles after 32 more Cybercabs were added, with 158 being Cybercab, versus roughly 1,150 Waymo units in Texas (about double Tesla's count, per the host). The cumulative mileage figure remained stuck at 1 million miles as of September 3, unchanged for four weeks, with an update expected around the October 21 earnings call.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **European regulators converge on speed-related FSD objections:** Croatia approved supervised FSD, bringing the European count to eight countries approved this month and 16 markets globally, per Tesla. However, a German Transport Ministry letter (obtained by a Tesla owner and translated) indicated EU-level discussions are set for October 6 (a discussion, not a vote) with a decision expected in December; the ministry raised concern about the system allowing speeds up to 50% above the limit. Separately, Reuters reported the Brussels-based European Transport Safety Council asked the EU to reject two FSD features — a user-set speed offset (allowing up to 50% over the detected limit) and a contextual max-speed feature that adjusts to traffic flow — arguing the offset violates UN rules; Poland's regulator reportedly raised similar questions. The host notes four separate parties (a Belgian test, the German ministry, the Brussels council, and reportedly Poland) have now flagged the same speed-related issue, and believes Tesla is likely to limit the speed-offset feature by December, though cautions the December date currently appears only in a citizen letter, not an official agenda.
2. **New collision-avoidance feature praised in dashcam clip:** A driver posted dashcam footage of a newer automatic collision-avoidance feature engaging to prevent a curb strike while driving manually; Tesla's safety page states one serious crash every 5.3 million miles with FSD versus a U.S. average of one per 660,000 miles (roughly 7x), and about 2.5x better than Autopilot-assisted manual driving.

## Financials
1. **Tesla secures $30 billion in new credit lines:** Per an 8-K filing, Tesla signed a $20 billion three-year term loan with Citi plus $8 billion five-year and $2 billion one-year revolving lines with Wells Fargo, all unsecured, replacing a prior $5 billion facility — six times larger. Tesla says it has no plans to draw on the funds in 2026. Alexandra Merz suggested the flexible structure implies Tesla won't need all the funds and can repay quickly; the host frames this as insurance given capex guidance above $25 billion this year, a newly opened Semi plant, and Cybertruck's first month on sale.
2. **New opt-in shareholder voting program approved by SEC:** The SEC issued Tesla a no-objection letter for a program letting retail investors opt in to having shares vote per board recommendations by default at each meeting, while retaining the ability to vote manually or opt out; the option excludes contested board seats and company mergers. Tesla's Chief Legal Officer Brandon Earhart and Robinhood's Vlad Tenev both commented supportively, and Musk responded "Cool." Tesla's own data shows retail investors voted only 28% of shares last year versus 77% for institutions, and Tesla spent over $2 million on voting intermediaries at each of the last two meetings; the program has not yet launched pending further filings. The host highlights that any Tesla–SpaceX merger would require a shareholder vote, and investors who opt into the new default-voting option would not retain a vote on that specific matter. Separately, one data provider (Alexandra Merz) pegged retail shareholder ownership at around 15.6% (down from 21.5% in July by the same methodology), while the host notes other providers estimate retail ownership closer to 40%, so the lower figure is not confirmed.

## Market & Competition
1. **Roadster unveiling postponed again:** Tesla cited severe forecasted weather and postponed the Roadster event, newly scheduled for October 15 — the sixth postponement this year. Musk said the delay was due to wind. The host notes the event can only be held outdoors, which he interprets as a hint that an engine demonstration is planned.
2. **NHTSA Cybertruck certification review ongoing:** Tesla's deadline to submit 21 responses to NHTSA regarding Cybertruck certification was Wednesday; as of Thursday neither Tesla nor NHTSA had publicly commented, which the host notes is expected since such responses aren't automatically public.

## SpaceX — Starship
1. **First Starship orbital flight achieves partial success:** Starship launched 26 Starlink V3 satellites on Monday; one of the booster's 33 engines shut down about a minute in but the booster still performed a landing burn in the Gulf (without being caught, as planned). About 7 minutes in, one of the ship's three vacuum engines shut down, but mission control continued the orbital maneuver on the remaining engine, and SpaceX confirmed Starship reached low-Earth orbit for the first time roughly 25 minutes after launch, deploying all 26 satellites successfully. Due to the engine loss, the planned ~10-hour, 6-orbit mission with splashdown near Chile was shortened to about 3 hours, with the ship splashing down north of Hawaii instead and breaking apart on water impact as expected; the FAA said no investigation was needed.
2. **Flight cadence targets and next launches:** Musk said Starship achieving hourly flight cadence is still two to three years away (a target the host notes has crept out from three years in January to three-to-four/five in March). Separately, Sawyer Merritt relayed that SpaceX expects to reach a weekly or twice-weekly Starship launch rate next year. Flight 15 is scheduled for October 19 and flight 16 for October 30; reports suggest flight 15 will attempt to catch the ship.

## SpaceX — Starlink
1. **New satellites add measurable constellation capacity:** Per Sawyer Merritt, each Starlink V3 satellite adds about 0.11% to total constellation power, meaning the 26 newly deployed V3 satellites added roughly 3% to total network capacity in one launch.
2. **Starlink positioned as future dominant internet carrier:** A SpaceX open-model contributor (Aaron Barnett) calculated that a base case of 151 Starship launches by 2028 would put enough capacity into orbit to cover 2025-level global internet traffic demand; Musk responded that it's increasingly likely Starlink will carry most of Earth's IP traffic long-term.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Analyst notes rising AI compute rental revenue — but it's ground-based, not orbital:** TD Cowen (analyst John Blackledge) rated SpaceX shares "buy" with a $200 price target, calling AI compute rentals its fastest-growing revenue source, projected to grow from about $14 billion this year to about $66 billion next year; the host clarifies this refers to ground data center leases (for Google and Anthropic), not orbital capacity, which SpaceX expects to become operational only in 2027. Needham also rated SpaceX a buy, calling shares a bargain at $250 and citing about $54 billion in announced annual AI-deal revenue.
2. **Competing cost models for orbital compute — Musk sides against the higher bank estimate:** Joe Bhakdi posted that Wall Street estimates place the cost of a gigawatt of orbital computing at $160–180 billion, while his own model shows $68 billion falling to $43 billion by 2035, arguing banks are mistakenly applying today's launch costs to 2028 construction. Musk responded that costs will be "much better" by 2028, effectively endorsing a lower-cost view than the bank estimates (though not explicitly confirming Bhakdi's $68B figure).
3. **Colossus ground compute buildout continuing in tranches:** Musk said another 220,000 Nvidia GB300 chips would go live "next week" (as of his Thursday post), another 220,000 in November, and potentially another 220,000 by the end of December — three tranches in Memphis, which the host notes is the one figure in this discussion backed by equipment already in place today. Musk reiterated at a Tuesday White House appearance that Starship will eventually launch "at least a few hundred gigawatts" of AI computing power per year.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Tesla halves Optimus chip memory to boost production volume:** Musk said Tesla cut RAM for the Tesla AI5 chip in half (now 72GB of LPDDR5) and by a third for AI6 (now 144GB of LPDDR6), stating this was necessary to secure enough chip supply volume to produce Optimus at scale and significantly cut costs; he said the change has little performance impact since memory bandwidth, which was left unchanged, is the real constraint. Tesla's news feed also indicated a goal of a production line capable of over 1,000 Optimus units per week by year-end, contingent on supplier readiness — Tesla has not otherwise commented. Separately, Tesla posted Optimus data-collection operator jobs (motion-capture suit/helmet required, height range 5'7"–5'11") across 13 cities in six states, paying $25–$34/hour.
