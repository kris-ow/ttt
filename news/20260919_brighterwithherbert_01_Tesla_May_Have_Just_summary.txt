This video (a discussion between the host and Cern Basher of Brilliant Advice) argues that Tesla's robotaxi business model is coming together via three simultaneous developments: a new NHTSA regulatory framework favoring performance-based (not design-based) safety standards, Tesla's emerging dynamic/network pricing strategy for Cybercab, and the potential for FSD and Starlink to become massive subscription businesses. The core thesis is that regulatory, pricing, and monetization pieces are converging to make robotaxi economics work at scale.

## Autonomous Driving / Robotaxi
1. **NHTSA moves toward performance-based AV safety standards:** NHTSA administrator Jonathan Morrison stated the agency is updating federal motor vehicle safety standards to focus on safety performance outcomes rather than mandating specific design features (e.g., steering wheels, mirrors), calling this a "scalpel" not a "hatchet" approach. He noted asking regulators for permission is "a bad business model" for AV companies, and highlighted AVs' potential to reduce the roughly 40,000 annual U.S. traffic fatalities.
2. **Five FMVSS amendments already proposed, more coming:** NHTSA has proposed amendments to five federal standards (covering transmission shift displays, windshield defrosting/wiping, tire/loading placards' "driver side" language, and manual brake controls) that are not applicable to driverless vehicles like Cybercab, with the administrator saying "many more" are in progress. NHTSA also launched a consortium called ASCEND with SAE to accelerate national AV performance standards.
3. **NHTSA audit query and Tesla's self-certification stance:** On the day Tesla launched Cybercab commercial operations, NHTSA issued a special order/audit query with 21 questions covering scaling plans, applicable safety standards, temporary human controls, and certification rationale. Cern Basher used Grok to draft sample answers, arguing most standards (steering wheel, pedals, mirrors, turn signals) are inapplicable to Cybercab; Tesla is self-certifying rather than seeking a Part 555 exemption, unlike Waymo, which reportedly tried to self-certify, was told it didn't meet standards, then obtained an exemption and later a commercial-service exemption.
4. **Regulatory outcome still uncertain but directionally positive:** Basher noted that even with Texas-level authorization, federal self-certification could still be challenged by NHTSA, so the ultimate outcome for nationwide, unrestricted Cybercab scaling is not yet guaranteed, though he characterized the direction as favorable.

## Robotaxi — Pricing & Business Model
5. **Dynamic pricing beyond surge pricing:** Basher distinguished Uber's reactive "surge pricing" (raising prices after demand exceeds supply, which Uber drivers reportedly game by avoiding zones until surge kicks in) from Tesla's broader "dynamic pricing," which can proactively reposition vehicles (e.g., toward a stadium before a game ends) and offer low prices to fill otherwise-empty repositioning trips.
6. **Network-level pricing optimization:** Basher described a "network pricing" concept where Tesla prices trips based on value to the whole fleet (vehicle positions, traffic, demand forecasts, battery state, cleaning needs) rather than per-mile alone, and argued Tesla's full control over its own fleet gives it an advantage over Uber, which relies on independent drivers who optimize individually.
7. **Revenue-per-hour vs. revenue-per-mile framing:** Basher suggested Tesla may need to think about Cybercab profitability in terms of time utilization (like an airline seat) rather than purely miles driven, since a 45-minute trip covering the same distance as a 12-minute trip ties up the vehicle far longer.
8. **Human drivers and privately-owned cars as surge capacity:** Basher laid out a phased model: currently Uber's human drivers absorb peak/surge demand since Tesla's fleet is small; later, privately-owned Tesla vehicles (Model Y, Model 3, or owner Cybercabs) could join the network with owner-set earning thresholds (e.g., "$15/hour minimum" or "$100/day"), and eventually third-party fleet operators (e.g., someone owning 100 Cybercabs) would also be integrated, all optimized by Tesla's AI.
9. **Supercharger pricing as a precedent:** Tesla already dynamically adjusts Supercharger pricing by demand (in tiers), which Basher cited as an early, less-sophisticated example of the dynamic pricing approach he expects for robotaxi.

## Financials
10. **Subscription business projections (speculative, illustrative):** Building on a third-party's ranking of global subscription services (Microsoft 365 ~450M subscribers, Google ~350M, Netflix ~301M), Basher argued Tesla FSD (~$99/month) and Starlink (~$792/year) already have among the highest per-subscriber annual revenue on such lists, calculating Microsoft's subscription revenue at roughly $99 billion/year versus Netflix ~$50B, AT&T ~$50B, and Verizon ~$69B. He then presented explicitly speculative, long-horizon scenarios — e.g., a billion Optimus robots at $1,000/month subscription implying $12 trillion in revenue, or 300 million robotaxi subscribers at $500/month implying nearly $2 trillion, and Starlink at 600 million subscribers and $67/month implying roughly half a trillion dollars — caveating repeatedly that these are illustrative, not forecasts with a defined timeline.
11. **Payment/credit-card fee rationale for subscriptions:** Basher noted Tesla incurs proportionally higher credit-card fees on small per-ride charges versus a larger monthly subscription charge, giving Tesla a financial incentive to shift toward subscription billing, and speculated that integration with X Money could eliminate credit card fees entirely.
