This video from brighterwithherbert examines why Tesla continues to delay showing Optimus V3 publicly, arguing that while the robot's official unveiling keeps slipping, a wide range of job postings, supplier filings, chip redesigns, and factory construction suggest production is genuinely approaching. The host frames the hand/arm as the central technical bottleneck and previews what he'll be watching for on Tesla's upcoming earnings call.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Hiring points to an imminent assembly ramp:** Tesla posted a new "Process Engineer, Optimus General Assembly" role in Austin (spotted by a Tesla job tracker) focused on developing and launching highly automated Optimus assembly lines with emphasis on large-scale production and capacity expansion. A separate "Optimus Park Coordinator" posting describes managing the full operations and maintenance cycle of an Optimus fleet across multiple sites, tracking usage, downtime, and inventory — which a commentator likened to how a robotaxi network is managed. Other long-open postings (since at least early September) cover Optimus arm production-line engineers in Palo Alto and Austin, a Palo Alto materials coordinator sourcing sensors/batteries/motion-capture gear, German engineers for gears and actuators, and data-collection operators across 13+ US cities paid $25–34/hour to generate motion-capture training data.
2. **Supplier IPO filing claims V3 hardware design is frozen:** Chinese supplier Sinjian Transmission, which makes planetary roller screws for Optimus joints, stated in ChiNext listing documentation (per a commentator's summary) that the Optimus V3 hardware design is essentially frozen — ready for demonstration and scheduled for mass production by the end of 2026. A separate Chinese report said supplier Sinpan Transmission announced completion of a Tesla robot design heading toward mass production. A September report from The Paper claimed Tesla was vetting three suppliers (Topu, Sanhua, Joyson) who had received orders differing only in volume, but two of those suppliers denied knowledge of any such inspection/order when asked by another outlet, so the host treats this with caution. A separate September report from GMN claimed an initial order of about 5,000 robots with supplier targets of 1,000 units/week in September rising to 2,000–2,500/week by year-end; the host says this is unconfirmed and exceeds what Elon has described.
3. **Elon details the hand as the hardest unsolved problem:** During a weekend X Takeover event at Giga Texas, Elon said fine motor skills in the hand are "shockingly difficult," using the example of driving a small screw into wood and demonstrating a jar-opening test live. He said he has not seen any hand design — even a demo one — that can perform such tasks, and argued that most competitor robot demo videos showing complex tasks are staged/pre-programmed rather than real AI, which he defined as handling something never seen before. Tesla's Ashok Elluswamy made a similar point in September about a viral dancing-robot video, saying such movements are essentially remote-controlled. The host theorizes this is why Tesla keeps postponing a public demo — having publicly discredited staged demos, Tesla can't show one itself and needs the hand doing something genuinely new, live.
4. **China's magnet export ban set the program back months:** Elon said there is no existing robotics supply chain and that China's ban on magnet exports for electric motors set the Optimus program back two to three months, leaving Tesla temporarily without functioning hands. His stated lesson is that Tesla needs to be able to produce robots domestically even if China cuts off supply. The host notes an unresolved question of whether Tesla has also removed rare-earth metals from Optimus joint motors, as it reportedly did for the Cybertruck's motor, but says this hasn't been confirmed.
5. **Production volume estimates vary widely and remain unofficial:** Tesla's own Q2 report said it halted Model S/X lines in Fremont to set up a first-generation Optimus line, with early robots going to an internal "Optimus Academy" for training and data collection; Elon said on July 1 that early production would be "extremely slow" and gave no 2026 production target, calling such predictions "impossible." A widely circulated figure of 50,000 robots for 2026 is explicitly not Tesla's own guidance, per the host. The Information (anonymous sources, no Tesla comment) reported production rising from a few dozen robots/week in Q2 to several hundred/week by August, with an internal goal of 1,000/week by year-end, and described the hand/forearm (100+ screws, hand-assembled parts) and touch-sensor reliability as the "narrow place" bottleneck; the same report said Tesla has over 500,000 hours of training data and aims to double that by year-end, with external customers initially receiving leased rather than purchased robots. JP Morgan's August notes from a Fremont meeting with Tesla IR indicated Optimus production start is expected in the coming months, with commercial sales targeted for the second half of 2027.
6. **AI chip memory halved specifically to prioritize Optimus volume:** Elon stated that Tesla halved RAM on the AI5 chip (now 72GB LPDDR5) and cut AI6 memory to a third (now 144GB LPDDR6), saying this was the only way to get enough chip volume to produce Optimus and significantly reduce its cost, while keeping memory bandwidth unchanged since bandwidth is a bigger limiting factor than total storage. The host frames this as evidence that Optimus production volumes are now directly shaping chip design decisions amid a broader memory shortage.
7. **New image and patent hints, with a "digital Optimus" distinction:** A Tesla Android app reportedly contained an image file (found by a third party) with filenames pointing to "Gen 3," two including the word "home," showing closed joints, thicker forearms, and thinner wrists suggesting finger motors relocated to the forearm — though the host is skeptical this depicts the actual V3. A separate Tesla patent application covers touch sensors for fingertips, palms, and other robot end-effector surfaces. The host also clarifies that "digital Optimus," a software/AI agent project Elon referenced (tied to an outside AI team), is separate from the physical Optimus robot.
8. **Giga Texas Optimus plant under rapid construction:** Texas filings put the new Optimus facility at just under 7 million square feet. A drone-footage tracker reports site prep began in late March, first steel structures appeared on May 27, construction is now about 40% complete, and main steel structures could be finished by the end of Q1 next year — roughly a year from groundbreaking to finished steel frame, which the tracker calls fast for this scale. He describes Texas as the plant for a later robot generation beyond the version being built in Fremont, with industry capacity targets cited around 1 million/year in Fremont and 10 million/year in Texas at full scale, and estimates limited Texas production beginning late next year. At Giga Berlin, a prototype was reportedly kept behind frosted glass, and since mid-August selected workers there have worn camera backpacks/helmets to record movements for Optimus training data.
9. **Unveiling repeatedly postponed; outside signals mixed:** Elon's public timeline for a V3 reveal has slipped multiple times — from a stated first-quarter target last October, to a March 31 video showing the robot walking but needing "finishing touches," to a mid-2026 estimate in April (when he first cited competitors copying frame-by-frame as the reason to delay). Oppenheimer's Colin Rush, after a September Tesla visit, wrote that he expects limited Optimus revenue until 2029 and anticipates likely production ramp delays, while calling the hand a bottleneck across every source the host has found. Separately, Jason Calacanis said in August that he had seen a recent Optimus demo he judged better than anything shown at China's World Humanoid Robot Games, and that Elon told him the clip was real, not CGI — though the host notes this is a secondhand account from someone close to Elon describing footage the public hasn't seen, and weighs it as suggestive but not conclusive.
10. **Robot economics framed via a viral labor-cost comparison:** A social media post (quoted approvingly by Elon) argued a human works roughly 2,000 hours/year versus a robot running continuously for about 8,700 hours/year — roughly four workers' worth of output — and that a robot priced a bit over $20,000 lasting five years would cost about 50 cents/hour before energy and maintenance. The host notes this makes energy and materials, not labor, the binding constraint once per-hour costs are this low, and cites Ark's view that humanoid robots could become a $20 trillion market, while cautioning that actual robots delivered to paying customers — starting with any first leased unit working for a third party — is the number that will really validate the thesis.
