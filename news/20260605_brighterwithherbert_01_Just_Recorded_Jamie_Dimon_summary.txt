This video covers a live Q&A session between Jamie Dimon and Elon Musk hosted at JP Morgan's headquarters in New York City, broadcast simultaneously to approximately 3,500 individual investors across 100 branches. The conversation is centered almost entirely on SpaceX's public offering, its growth roadmap, and Elon Musk's broader vision for space infrastructure — with no substantive discussion of Tesla, autonomous driving, robotaxi services, or Optimus. The event appears timed to coincide with SpaceX's IPO-related investor outreach.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX IPO Rationale — Capital for a Major Growth Phase:** Elon Musk explained that SpaceX has been cash-flow positive since approximately 2014–2015 and that prior private equity rounds were liquidity events for employees and investors rather than fundraising rounds. The reason for going public now, he said, is that SpaceX is entering a significant new capital growth phase, specifically to fund the buildout of the Starlink Version 3 satellite constellation (targeting over 100,000 satellites), space-based AI data centers, and chip fabrication infrastructure. He described revenue as now being more predictable, which added to the timing rationale.

2. **Starlink Version 3 — Generational Leap in Capability:** Musk described the V3 satellite as 10 to 20 times more capable than the current V2 satellite, delivering 100 times more bandwidth and half the latency of the existing Starlink system, due in part to a lower orbital altitude. V3 satellites are approximately 7 meters (roughly 20–23 feet) wide — too large to be launched on any rocket other than Starship — and incorporate custom chips designed by SpaceX's chip team, larger phased array antennas, more inter-satellite laser links, and W-band and E-band communications. Starship is expected to launch up to 50 V3 satellites per mission.

3. **Starship Full Reusability as the Core Breakthrough:** Musk identified full reusability as the fundamental technological breakthrough of Starship, noting that Falcon 9 achieved partial reusability but Starship will be fully reusable. Because Starship uses liquid oxygen and liquid methane — which Musk described as the cheapest propellant available — the marginal cost per launch once reusability is achieved becomes primarily the cost of propellant, which he said would be less than the cost of jet aviation fuel. Starship Version 3 is targeting 100 tons to orbit with full reusability; Version 4 is targeting over 200 tons per mission.

4. **Space-Based AI Data Centers — Simpler Than Communication Satellites:** Musk argued that space-based AI data centers are technically easier to build than Starlink communication satellites, because they essentially consist of solar power, radiators, basic satellite operations equipment, and laser links connecting to the existing Starlink constellation. He said connectivity to the ground would be weather-independent, as Starlink communicates to the ground on cloud-penetrating (and even roof-penetrating) frequencies.

5. **Terafab Chip Manufacturing — Filling a Critical Gap:** Musk stated that there is currently no high-volume computer memory fabrication facility in the United States, and that even under best-case assumptions from existing chip manufacturers, domestic supply will be insufficient to meet anticipated AI demand. He cited a Micron fab in Idaho not expected to reach volume production until approximately 2028, and other fabs in New York targeting 2029–2030, as insufficient to close the gap. He framed the Terafab initiative as essential for AI logic, memory, and packaging.

6. **Grok AI Integration into SpaceX Platform:** Musk indicated that SpaceX's AI satellites will be an open platform, allowing customers to run Nvidia GPUs, Google TPUs, Amazon Trainium, or other third-party chips. SpaceX/xAI intends to also offer its own chips and AI software (including Grok) on the platform in the future, but the architecture is designed to be hardware- and software-agnostic.

7. **Moon and Mars as Long-Term Infrastructure Vision:** Musk outlined a vision in which the moon serves as an intermediary step before Mars, noting that the moon's lack of atmosphere and low gravity (approximately 1/6th of Earth's) allows for electromagnetic mass drivers (rail guns) to launch AI data center hardware into deep space without rockets. He estimated approximately 1 terawatt per year of AI space compute capacity from Earth-based launches, scaling to 1,000 terawatts or more from the moon. He described Mars as a "fixer upper of a planet" with long-term terraforming potential.
