This video is a presentation by Brian from the Futurazza channel, delivered at an EV owners club event, comparing three fundamental design approaches to purpose-built robotaxi vehicles. The core thesis is that Tesla's Cybercab — engineered from first principles specifically for autonomous ride-hailing — holds a decisive structural cost and operational advantage over competitors like Waymo and Zoox, which either retrofit existing consumer vehicles or build highly complex custom platforms. The talk covers design philosophy, part counts, manufacturing methods, cost economics, and the open question of unsupervised FSD deployment timelines.

## Robotaxi

1. **Three Design Philosophies for Robotaxis:** Brian categorizes the current robotaxi landscape into three approaches: "a car" (an unmodified or lightly modified consumer vehicle used as-is, such as Tesla's Model Y or Xpeng's P7), "a car plus" (a consumer vehicle heavily upfitted with sensors, connectivity, and safety hardware, such as Waymo's Jaguar iPace), and "first principles designed" (a vehicle conceived entirely around the robotaxi use case, such as Tesla's Cybercab or Zoox's box vehicle). He argues the first-principles approach is superior for long-term economics.

2. **Waymo's Car-Plus Approach and Its Cost Burdens:** Waymo uses the Jaguar iPace, manufactured in Austria, ships it to Arizona for full sensor upfitting, and then deploys it to its operating markets. Brian estimates this approach costs a minimum of $50,000 for the base vehicle at good scale, plus roughly $20,000 to upfit and $5,000 in lost time and shipping, for a total baseline cost that analysts peg closer to $150,000 per vehicle. He further notes that at 150,000 miles and 50% utilization, the first dollar per mile of revenue goes entirely to covering vehicle cost — and he considers 150,000 miles a generous lifespan for a Jaguar. Waymo also planned to use the Zeekr (a Chinese-made vehicle), but the 100% tariff on Chinese imports undermines any cost savings.

3. **Zoox's Custom Box and Its Tradeoffs:** Zoox built a bespoke, fully symmetrical box vehicle that can drive in either direction and seats four passengers facing each other. Brian estimates it costs approximately $250,000 per unit because everything is custom and handbuilt in low volumes. He identifies several practical shortcomings: a very large step-up to enter, a large flat front window vulnerable to highway debris (addressed by shrinking it to a small slit at the top, reducing forward visibility), and no meaningful luggage space — forcing passengers to hold bags on their laps even on airport runs, which Zoox recently added in Las Vegas.

4. **Cybercab's First-Principles Advantages:** The Cybercab is designed around the fact that 87% of Uber trips involve two or fewer passengers and the U.S. average vehicle occupancy is 1.5 people, making a large four-seat box unnecessary for most trips. Key design choices include only three pieces of glass (versus 8–13 on a typical consumer vehicle), approximately 4,000 total parts (versus 10,000 for a Model Y and 30,000 for a luxury car), no paint shop required (saving hundreds of millions to a billion dollars in factory cost), giga-casting front and rear sub-assemblies that go together like Legos, and no wheel-well cutouts in the cargo area. The doors swing up and out for accessibility, the seat height matches a wheelchair, and interior surfaces are designed for fast, foolproof cleaning with no crevices.

5. **Cybercab Cost and Longevity Economics:** Brian estimates the Cybercab will cost under $30,000 to produce, and realistically closer to $25,000. At 150,000 miles and 50% utilization, this translates to roughly $0.67 less per mile in vehicle cost compared to a competitor approach. More importantly, because Tesla intends to own and operate the fleet rather than sell the vehicles, it is building the Cybercab to last 500,000 to one million miles, with replaceable pre-painted plastic panels. He uses Waymo's sensor stack as a scale reference: Waymo's add-on hardware alone accounts for over 400 parts, which is 10% of the Cybercab's entire part count.

6. **Fleet Ownership Model and Sales Uncertainty:** Brian is uncertain whether Tesla will sell Cybercabs to individuals or third-party fleet operators. He argues Tesla's own operated fleet will be so profitable that the incentive to sell or partner may be limited. He suggests Tesla might prioritize fleet operators over individuals (one contact point for 100 vehicles is more efficient than 100 individual owners). He uses his small hometown of Longview, Washington as an example of a market Tesla would likely not serve directly — where individual ownership might make sense as a way to extend coverage. He also raises that Tesla Insurance is not yet available in Washington State, and that insurance for autonomous vehicles would likely need to be embedded in the per-mile fare, similar to how Waymo presumably handles it under a fleet umbrella policy.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **Current Robotaxi Operations Are Not Truly Unsupervised:** Brian distinguishes between robotaxis operating today (which he describes as "more like puppets" with remote operators intervening from headquarters to an unknown degree) and truly unsupervised FSD — where no one is at the wheel and no one is actively monitoring, with only an on-call support model similar to OnStar. He is transparent that he does not know when the latter will be achieved, estimating it could be anywhere from one year to five years out and describing it as "over the horizon."

2. **Unsupervised FSD for Personal Vehicles Is Resource-Constrained:** Even setting aside regulatory questions, Brian notes that deploying unsupervised FSD for personal vehicles at unpredictable hours and locations would overwhelm current remote operations resources, since operators cannot be stationed to monitor every privately-owned car in the fleet at all times.

## Market & Competition

1. **Chinese Competitors Offer Dramatically Lower Base Costs:** Xpeng's P7 sedan, which the company is using to test robotaxis in Guangzhou, costs $32,000 and requires no additional sensors or hardware beyond what is already on the production vehicle. Brian presents this as evidence that the "plain car" approach can be highly competitive on initial vehicle cost, though he does not address operational or software economics.

2. **Tariffs Undercut Waymo's Cost Strategy on Chinese Vehicles:** Waymo's plan to adopt the Zeekr (manufactured in China) as a next-generation robotaxi platform is undermined by the 100% U.S. tariff on Chinese-made vehicles, which eliminates or reverses any cost advantage Zeekr might have offered over the iPace.
