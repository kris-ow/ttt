This is a two-host discussion (Brian and Herbert, referencing conversations with other analysts including "Larry" Goldberg and Alexandra Merz) debating whether, when, and on what terms a Tesla–SpaceX merger might happen, the shareholder-vote mechanics that make approval difficult, and competing views on whether SpaceX's valuation hinges on orbital data centers or is already supported by terrestrial AI compute demand.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation
1. **Merger probability and timing debated:** Herbert initially states he gives the merger "over 99%" odds of eventually happening, then revises down to roughly 70%, citing the risk that Tesla's and SpaceX's special merger committees (one on each side) fail to reach agreement on terms. He argues a deal requires pricing acceptable to both Tesla shareholders (who need a premium) and Elon Musk as SpaceX's controlling shareholder.
2. **Backward-dated timeline from a third-party analyst:** Analyst Alexandra Merz (per the hosts) argues a merger would need to be announced before around December of this year in order to close by around September next year — timed to coincide with potential S&P 500 inclusion of the combined entity and while the current U.S. administration remains in office. The hosts note Merz has previously made similarly timed predictions that did not materialize, and flag this as her opinion, not confirmed fact.
3. **Shareholder vote mechanics favor approval but face two obstacles:** Per Herbert, SpaceX reportedly indicated (via a released document, exact legal form not specified) that it does not need a shareholder vote, leaving Tesla's vote as the key hurdle. Two obstacles are cited: (1) the vote threshold requires 50% of all outstanding shares (not just those voting) to approve, so non-votes count as "no"; and (2) post-merger, Tesla shareholders lose their current voting rights since Elon Musk would hold special voting control, making it harder to convince large institutional holders (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street, Schwab) and retail holders, especially non-U.S. retail investors who may be unable to vote at all.
4. **Premium expectations discussed:** Brad Gerstner is cited as having suggested a premium of at least 30% to Tesla's existing stock price would be needed to win shareholder approval; Herbert says he personally believes an even larger premium — potentially 30% above Tesla's prior all-time high (which he estimates near $500, implying a price around $600) — may be necessary, framing this explicitly as his own expectation rather than a confirmed figure.
5. **Succession and voting-control concerns:** The hosts raise concern that Musk's special voting rights may pass to his family/children, whose management abilities and intentions are unknown, and that this could unsettle institutional investors; they note that control concentration (described as around 80% of the vote in a combined entity, which Herbert believes would stay above 70% even if some shareholders are unhappy) is a point of institutional risk alongside comparisons to other "Mag 7" companies' founder-control structures.

## Financials
1. **Bull case: merged entity outgrows standalone Tesla:** The hosts cite an analysis attributed to ARK Invest's Brett Winton suggesting a combined Tesla-SpaceX entity could see roughly five times the growth over five years compared to a standalone path, which is used as the basis for Larry Goldberg's argument (relayed by Herbert) that Tesla shareholders benefit long-term from a merger regardless of premium terms, since they become SpaceX shareholders through the stock-for-stock conversion.
2. **SpaceX terrestrial AI compute revenue forecast:** Elon Musk is cited as having forecast a roughly fivefold increase in SpaceX's terrestrial data-center annualized revenue, from about $100 billion to about $500 billion next year. Anthropic is referenced as having committed roughly $125 billion through 2030 in compute spending with SpaceX, which the hosts cite as evidence the revenue ramp is real and not merely a cancellable commitment.
3. **Debt required to fund data center buildout questioned:** Herbert relays a figure from "Larry" that reaching the $500 billion revenue target (at a stated 13–20 gigawatt buildout) would require roughly $400 billion in borrowing, versus only about $40 billion borrowed so far — flagged by both hosts as a major open question and a key risk to the bullish compute thesis.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Orbital data centers framed by one analyst as existential:** Larry Goldberg (described as a large shareholder in both SpaceX and Tesla) is said to have run roughly 20,000 Monte Carlo simulations concluding that SpaceX's stock price is currently undervalued on a median basis, but carries meaningful probability of falling to zero if orbital data centers prove infeasible — a conclusion he reportedly attributes primarily to the viability of orbital compute rather than terrestrial data centers. Herbert explicitly disagrees with this zero-probability conclusion, saying he has limited confidence in Monte Carlo-style modeling generally and believes SpaceX "will survive" even without orbital data centers working out, while still calling the "if it works in space, it works on Earth" framing reasonable, citing SpaceX's ground-based pilot project (using solar panels and Megapacks) as a proof-of-concept step.
2. **Spot pricing risk to the compute revenue forecast:** Musk is quoted (via the hosts) as saying on an earnings call that the bet on compute spot pricing is "paying off" so far, with spot prices rising rather than falling — but the hosts note this assumption underlying the $500 billion+ revenue forecast could reverse.
