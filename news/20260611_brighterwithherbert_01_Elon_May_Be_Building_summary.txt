This video features an interview between the host (brighterwithherbert) and Brian Wang, a futurist blogger and science writer at NextBigFuture.com, discussing a SpaceX IPO analysis titled "SpaceX IPO: AI in Space." The central thesis is that Elon Musk is building a vertically integrated AI civilization stack — spanning Tesla's physical hardware layer (energy, batteries, Megapacks, solar, vehicles, robots, real-world data) through SpaceX AI's software and infrastructure layer (models, digital workers, payments, Starlink connectivity, orbital compute, and eventually AI data centers in space) — and that whoever controls the entire stack will capture the majority of AI's economic value. The conversation covers SpaceX's revenue projections, Starship launch cadence, AI satellites, Starlink V5 terminal economics, and briefly touches on how Tesla fits into the same ecosystem.

## Market & Competition

1. **The Full AI Stack Thesis:** Brian Wang argues that the real winner in the AI era won't be the company with the best model (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) but the company that owns the entire vertical stack. He describes Elon's combined empire as covering physical infrastructure (Tesla energy, batteries, Megapacks, solar, chips, vehicles, Optimus robots, real-world data), AI software (Grok, digital workers, payments), and space infrastructure (Starlink, orbital compute, AI data centers in space via SpaceX). The argument is that network effects and pricing power will accumulate at the level of whoever owns the full value chain.

2. **XAI Already Leading in AI Cloud Revenue:** Wang argues that XAI's terrestrial Colossus data centers — having already secured a $26 billion annual contract with Anthropic and Google (described as $2.2 billion per month starting from October, growing from approximately $1.2 billion per month currently) — already exceed Amazon's AI cloud run rate of $15 billion per year and Google's entire cloud revenue of $20 billion per year. He notes that only OpenAI/Microsoft, at $37 billion AI ARR, currently exceeds XAI in AI-specific cloud revenue. Wang's framing: XAI has approximately 20% of all AI data center capacity that is actually operational, which is why hyperscalers are paying a premium to rent it.

3. **AI Demand Vastly Exceeds Supply:** Wang cites analyst Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis as estimating that AI demand is approximately five times current supply, and that if supply could be quintupled, the demand would be there to absorb it — implying potential AI cloud revenue in the hundreds of billions or more. He notes that Anthropic is deliberately throttling users and making models "dumber" to manage inference demand because it lacks sufficient data center capacity. Jensen Huang's framing — "the more chips, the more money you make" — is cited as a simple summary of the economics.

4. **Cursor Revenue Growth:** Wang states that Cursor (the AI coding tool, set to be acquired by SpaceX for 2–3% of SpaceX shares) has doubled revenue in the last four months and reached approximately $2 billion in just the last month. He extrapolates that if it keeps doubling every four months, it could reach approximately $32–36 billion per year within 12 months, though he acknowledges the speculative nature of this.

5. **Chinese Competition in Space AI:** Wang and the host debate whether China poses a competitive threat to SpaceX's space-based AI data center ambitions. Wang acknowledges China will try to copy the approach but argues it cannot match the scale rapidly because it lacks a fully reusable heavy-lift rocket. He notes that in the prior year, approximately 4,000 tons were launched to orbit globally, with SpaceX accounting for roughly 3,000 tons and China approximately 500 tons. Without a fully reusable rocket equivalent to Starship, China cannot scale from 500 tons to hundreds of thousands of tons within three years.

## Energy

1. **Starlink V5 Terminal Economics and Starlink Revenue TAM:** Wang describes the new Starlink Version 5 terminal as a consumer product comparable in manufacturing economics to a flat-screen monitor. Current production is approximately 1 million units per month, scaling to 1.5 million, then doubling to 3 million, then 6 million per month (72 million per year). Wang's TAM estimate for terminals is "a few hundred million," suggesting approximately 400 million terminals at $40–50 per month per subscriber, which he calculates could yield approximately $200 billion per year in high-speed internet revenue alone. Key features of the V5 dish: includes a lithium battery for 1–2 hours of untethered operation, USB-C power input, no GPS chip (Starlink positioning used instead), thinner form factor, and capability for military/government workloads. With new Echo spectrum, Wang says it can achieve symmetrical gigabit speeds (up and down) with 20-millisecond latency, making it competitive with fiber in urban markets.

2. **Space-Based AI Data Centers (AI1 Satellites):** Wang describes SpaceX's AI1 satellite — a large orbital AI compute platform with a wingspan comparable to a Boeing 787 (approximately 70 meters) — as the first version of what will become a series of increasingly large space-based AI data centers. The AI1 has a power density of approximately 70 kilowatts per ton. Elon's stated goal is to have 1 gigawatt of orbital AI compute deployed by 2027, which Wang calculates requires launching approximately 13,000 tons — equivalent to roughly 130 Starship launches at 100-ton payload, or fewer at the 200-ton Version 4 payload capacity. The plan is then to 10x every 18 months: 1 gigawatt in 2027, 10 gigawatts in 2028, 100 gigawatts in 2029.

3. **Space-Based AI Revenue Potential:** Wang estimates that 1 gigawatt of orbital AI compute equipped with Rubin (next-generation Nvidia) chips could contain approximately 550,000 chips. If rented at current B200 pricing, this would yield approximately $50 billion per year; if Rubin chips command double the rental rate of B200s, this yields approximately $100 billion per year — comparable to Tesla's entire annual revenue — from just the first gigawatt. Wang notes that XAI can keep adding gigawatts in space while also adding Colossus 3 and additional terrestrial data centers ("Macrohard") on land they already own, using the Colossus 2 buildout as a repeatable template.

4. **Tesla's Parallel AI Rental Opportunity:** Wang briefly previews a future discussion topic: Tesla can similarly rent out compute capacity embedded in Cortex (Tesla's AI training cluster), Superchargers, Powerwalls, and Mega Chargers — representing what he calls "a bunch of free money" for Tesla as a parallel AI infrastructure revenue stream, analogous to what SpaceX/XAI is doing at scale. He notes Tesla and SpaceX are "Siamese twins" in this ecosystem and raises the possibility the two companies may eventually merge.

## Financials

1. **SpaceX Revenue Projections — Aggressive Case:** Wang constructs a bottom-up model responding to Elon's complaint that analysts (specifically referencing Aaron Bernett of Mach 33) were "too conservative" and "discounting out to $240, nowhere near our goals." Wang's model, if Elon hits his stated goals (1/10/100 gigawatt in 2027/2028/2029), projects approximately $1 trillion in revenue by 2028 and approximately $7 trillion by 2029 — more revenue and profit than the top 20 companies today combined. He estimates 70% profitability on these revenue streams, yielding approximately $4 trillion in EBITDA by 2029 versus approximately $1.2 trillion EBITDA for all top 20 companies today. He explicitly frames this as answering Elon's challenge rather than his own base case.

2. **SpaceX Revenue Projections — Conservative Downgrade:** Wang's more "reasoned" scenario downgrades the aggressive case by approximately 80%, projecting approximately $1 trillion in revenue by 2029–2030 (versus $7 trillion in the aggressive case), reflecting slower orbital deployment (perhaps 4–5 gigawatts rather than 100 gigawatts by 2029), slower Grok/Cursor growth, and no upgrade on the physical AI rental line. Even in this conservative case, he projects SpaceX reaching Apple-scale revenue (~$451 billion) by approximately 2028 from Starlink and AI rental alone, excluding space-based AI entirely. He notes that even zeroing out space AI and Digital Optimus, just Starlink DTC and AI rental yields approximately $500 billion in revenue — larger than Apple today.

3. **Colossus Expansion Economics:** Wang states that Colossus 1 and Colossus 2 combined, if fully rented out, would yield approximately $64 billion per year in revenue. XAI is currently renting out 40% of Colossus 2 capacity (the $26 billion Anthropic/Google deal), retaining the remainder for Grok and Cursor inference. XAI is adding approximately 220,000 chips and 400 megawatts of capacity approximately every 6 months — effectively replicating the Colossus 2 buildout on a rolling basis. Wang calculates this could bring the total rentable run rate to approximately $60 billion per year within 6 months if they chose to rent it all out. XAI also has secured a 2-gigawatt natural gas power deal and a third data center location for further expansion.

4. **Golden Dome Defense Contracts:** The US government's Golden Dome missile defense program represents a major guaranteed revenue stream. SpaceX already has $6 billion in Golden Dome contracts in the current year (2026). The Congressional Budget Office estimates Golden Dome will cost $185 billion over the next three years and $1 trillion or more over 20 years. Wang argues SpaceX will capture at least one-third to one-half of all launch and space infrastructure contracts under Golden Dome, as no other provider can deliver at scale. He projects this could grow to $20–50 billion per year for SpaceX from government space-enabled solutions alone, noting that every other country will eventually need its own equivalent program.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Cybercab Integration with Starlink V5:** The host notes that Cybercabs have been observed with Starlink terminals being bolted into them, and speculates that the new miniaturized V5 dish could be integrated directly into the Cybercab roof — small enough to be invisible as part of the vehicle structure. Wang concurs that the miniaturized terminal form factor is ideal for vehicle integration, providing always-on high-bandwidth AI and connectivity for the robotaxi fleet without requiring ground-based cellular infrastructure. The implication is that Cybercabs could become nodes in Starlink's terrestrial coverage network.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus and Multi-Form-Factor AI Devices:** Wang argues that Tesla will manufacture many different form factors of robots beyond the humanoid Optimus — a broad portfolio of AI-enabled physical devices. He also discusses SpaceX/XAI producing an "AI device" (not a traditional phone, but slabs, necklaces, wrist devices, glasses) that will all be connected to Starlink for instant AI access everywhere. He draws a parallel to Steve Jobs's iPhone moment, arguing this ecosystem of always-connected AI devices is analogous. Wang speculates that Optimus units working in remote environments (farms, mountains, military applications) could carry a Starlink V5 mini terminal as a backpack with a small articulated arm to angle it skyward for connectivity.
