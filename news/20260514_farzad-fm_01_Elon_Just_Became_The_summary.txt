This video analyzes the partnership deal between SpaceX and Anthropic, in which SpaceX is providing Anthropic with 300 megawatts of compute capacity and access to over 220,000 Nvidia GPUs at the Colossus 1 data center in Memphis, Tennessee. The host's core thesis is that SpaceX has quietly assembled the only fully vertically integrated AI infrastructure stack on Earth — spanning rockets, chip fabrication, power, satellites, developer surfaces, and now frontier model tenants — repositioning itself not as a rocket company or even an AI lab, but as the "landlord" of the entire AI economy. The video argues this structural shift mirrors Tesla's vertical integration playbook in EVs and has profound consequences for the competitive dynamics among OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and Amazon.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX–Anthropic compute deal:** Anthropic announced a partnership with SpaceX granting access to all compute capacity at Colossus 1, the Memphis, Tennessee data center originally built to train Grok. This provides over 300 megawatts of additional capacity and more than 220,000 Nvidia GPUs — primarily H100s, H200s, and GB200 chips — expected to come online within a month of the announcement. The immediate effect is that rate limits for Claude Pro, Max, Code, Team, and Enterprise products will effectively double, peak-hour throttling on Pro and Max accounts is removed, and API limits on Opus models are raised.

2. **Background: Anthropic's demand-strangled growth:** Throughout the first half of 2026, Anthropic was severely compute-constrained despite having what many consider the best AI models available. Claude Code users were hitting rate limits in hours rather than days. The host cites Anthropic's annual revenue run rate climbing from roughly $9 billion at the end of 2025, to approximately $19 billion by March 2026, to approximately $30 billion by the first week of April 2026 — described as the fastest revenue ramp of any company in history. Anthropic's valuation at the time of recording is cited at approximately $1.2 trillion.

3. **SpaceX's transformation into an AI hyperscaler:** The host argues SpaceX is no longer meaningfully a rocket company. Following its absorption of XAI in an all-stock transaction on February 2nd, 2026 — described by Reuters as the largest corporate merger ever recorded, valuing the combined entity at roughly $1.25 trillion at announcement and closer to $2 trillion at current estimated valuations — SpaceX gained ownership of XAI, Grok, and the Colossus data center complex. SpaceX now owns Colossus 1 (220,000+ GPUs), Colossus 2 (a gigawatt-scale facility with 335,000+ GPUs), and a third site coming online in South Haven, Mississippi, with a roadmap to 1 million GPUs by late 2026 and approximately $18 billion invested in the Memphis complex alone.

4. **Terafab chip fabrication venture:** SpaceX, along with Tesla, is building Terafab, a 2nm chip fabrication facility in Austin, Texas. Originally announced as a $20–$22 billion project, it is now described as on pace to be closer to $200 billion. A report published three hours before the Anthropic announcement cited SpaceX proposing $55 billion in capital expenditure for Phase 1 of Terafab, potentially rising to $119 billion across all phases. Intel joined the project in April as a manufacturing partner. The roadmap targets 100,000 wafer starts per month ramping to 1 million per month, which the host notes would be roughly 70% of TSMC's entire global output.

5. **SpaceX's vertically integrated AI stack:** The host enumerates six layers of the AI infrastructure stack that SpaceX now owns: (1) launches via Falcon 9 and Starship, with Starship targeting $10–$100 per kilogram to orbit when flying at cadence, representing a 30x–300x reduction from current Falcon 9 prices and over 1,000x from industry prices a decade ago; (2) chips via Terafab; (3) power and compute via the Colossus data centers; (4) network via Starlink, with over 10,000 satellites in orbit; (5) models via Grok/XAI; and (6) a developer surface via a $60 billion option to acquire Cursor by end of 2026 with a $10 billion breakup fee. The host argues no competitor owns more than four of these layers.

6. **Winners and losers from the deal:** Anthropic wins by breaking its compute bottleneck and gaining the runway to deploy its next model, called Mythos, which reportedly features a 1 million token context window and was previewed to limited partners in April; the host describes benchmark reports as indicating it is highly capable. SpaceX wins by proving its thesis as an AI infrastructure company and gaining a recurring revenue stream that supports its targeted IPO valuation of $1.5–$2 trillion or more, per Bank of America projections, with the IPO targeted for sometime in mid-2026. The relative loser is Grok/XAI: Colossus 1, originally built for Grok's training, has been entirely rented to Anthropic, and the host notes that essentially every original XAI co-founder besides Elon Musk has left in 2026.

7. **OpenAI's structural squeeze:** The host argues OpenAI is under serious pressure. Its CFO recently capped the Microsoft revenue share. OpenAI pulled back from a Stargate Norway data center project, which Microsoft then picked up directly through a company called Nscale, contracting 30,000 Rubin GPUs itself. OpenAI has committed $250 billion to Microsoft Azure over six years. Microsoft's 2026 capex of $190 billion and a doubling of AI infrastructure in two years signals Microsoft has the upper hand. The host frames the SpaceX–Anthropic deal as Elon Musk and Dario Amodei — both former OpenAI colleagues who departed over safety and leadership concerns — effectively uniting against Sam Altman and OpenAI.

8. **The "landlord of AI" thesis:** The host's central argument is that the AI competitive landscape has fundamentally shifted. The moat used to be the model; now it is everything beneath the model. Models are commoditizing rapidly due to open-source releases from China, Mistral, and Meta compressing the gap from best-in-class to adequate from years to months. SpaceX's strategy, the host argues, is not to win the model race but to be the infrastructure landlord that all frontier labs — including competitors — must pay rent to. This is compared directly to Tesla's vertical integration of the EV supply chain, which left legacy automakers unable to compete because they did not own the underlying industrial layers.
