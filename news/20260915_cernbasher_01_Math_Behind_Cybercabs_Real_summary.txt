This video (a two-host discussion) unpacks Elon Musk's claim that Cybercab is optimized for the lowest possible cost per mile "fully loaded," arguing that Tesla's real objective isn't a cheap sticker price but the lowest lifetime cost per passenger mile — driven by vehicle durability, operating costs, and fleet efficiency versus Waymo. The hosts work through hypothetical fleet-sizing and cost-per-mile math to argue that spending more per vehicle at the factory to extend its useful life can dramatically lower long-run costs, even though none of the specific mileage or per-unit cost figures used are confirmed Tesla data.

## Robotaxi
1. **Cybercab specs cited from third-party posts:** Citing posts from Nick Cruz Patane and Sawyer Merritt (not confirmed Tesla figures per the hosts' framing), Cybercab has a wider front than rear, a drag coefficient under 0.2, is described as the most efficient production EV certified at 165 Wh/mile, is Tesla's first front-wheel-drive model, and has a 47.6 kWh battery yielding about 6.1 miles per kWh.
2. **Musk's "fully loaded" cost-per-mile comment:** Elon Musk commented that the degree to which Cybercab is optimized for lowest possible cost per mile "fully loaded" is understood by very few; one host says he wrote a post on this after reflecting further and replied to Musk's comment (unconfirmed whether Musk saw it).
3. **Lifetime-cost framing vs. sticker price:** The hosts argue Tesla's goal is not simply a $30,000 (or lower) build cost but the lowest cost per revenue-generating passenger mile over the vehicle's full economic life — implying Tesla might deliberately spend more per unit (e.g., a hypothetical $40,000 vehicle) if it yields much longer service life.
4. **Fleet-sizing math example (hypothetical, host-constructed):** For a hypothetical city needing 1 billion passenger miles, a fleet of 300,000-mile vehicles would need 3,333 units (~$100M at $30,000/vehicle); a 500,000-mile vehicle life cuts that to 2,000 units (~$66M total, including an assumed $3,000/vehicle cost increase) — a $34M savings; a 1-million-mile vehicle life would need roughly 1,000 units for about $33M, a 67% cost reduction versus the 300,000-mile baseline. These are illustrative host calculations, not disclosed Tesla figures.
5. **Cost-per-mile scaling (hypothetical):** On the same framework, a $30,000 vehicle lasting 300,000 miles implies 10 cents/mile in vehicle cost; spending $3,000 more per unit but reaching 500,000 miles brings this to roughly 6.6 cents/mile; a million-mile lifetime would bring it to roughly 3.3 cents/mile — with diminishing returns beyond that, per the hosts' own construction.
6. **Battery designed for longevity over speed:** One host states Cybercab's battery was reportedly designed to prioritize longevity over rapid acceleration, and that the vehicle's top speed is limited (cited around 85 mph) to support longer battery life, contrasted with performance-oriented consumer EVs.
7. **Speculation on million-mile+ battery life:** One host says he's heard speculation that Cybercab could reach a million miles of useful life, and that some people have even speculated up to a million and a half miles, though he's not personally willing to go that far; he says Tesla is "clearly very confident" about reaching half a million miles, and notes older Teslas have been observed reaching a million miles.
8. **Operating-cost advantages vs. Waymo (unquantified):** The hosts argue Tesla may have structural advantages over Waymo in charging cost, cleaning cost, and repair speed/cost (e.g., a damaged panel possibly being swapped in under an hour for Tesla versus roughly two days for Waymo), though no specific figures are given for these claims.
9. **Cybercab vs. Waymo unit-economics comparison (host-cited, approximate):** One host recalls (without certainty) a comparison suggesting $1 million could buy roughly 55 Cybercabs versus about nine Waymos, and argues each Cybercab may be equivalent to about three Waymos in effective service because it avoids frequent recharging downtime and could last roughly twice as long (e.g., 10 years vs. 5), meaning fewer replacement purchases over time versus Waymo's shorter vehicle life (compared to rental fleets like Hertz replacing cars every roughly 3 years).
