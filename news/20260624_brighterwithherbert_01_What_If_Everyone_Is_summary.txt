This video is a detailed discussion between host Herbert and guest Jo Bhakdi — economist, investor, and tech entrepreneur — about the mechanics and structure of a potential SpaceX-Tesla merger. The core thesis is that the standard debate over "merger of equals" vs. ownership ratios is missing the most critical issue: whether the deal will carry a fixed, dollar-denominated price floor (a "ratchet") for Tesla shareholders, or leave them exposed to unknown SpaceX market volatility between announcement and the shareholder vote. Jo Bhakdi argues that a pure merger of equals — with no price guarantee — is practically unworkable for a company of Tesla's size and complexity, and that a ratchet mechanism is essential to ensure Tesla shareholders can make an informed vote.

## Market & Competition

1. **Why a merger is likely and why it should happen soon:** Jo Bhakdi argues that the fundamental reason for a SpaceX-Tesla merger is Elon Musk's time and management attention — running two massive, complex public companies in parallel wastes resources. He also cites strategic synergies: a Tesla patent for distributed compute centers at Supercharger stations (described as a SpaceX-type business residing in a Tesla asset), and Tesla's solar production capabilities needed for SpaceX's AI satellite ambitions. Bhakdi contends that because Musk's broader civilizational goals (including what Bhakdi describes as a Kardashev Type 2 civilization strategy) require unified assets, the merger needs to happen sooner rather than later. The host speculates the announcement could come as early as end of July 2026, with a Tesla shareholder vote potentially in November 2026.

2. **Why SpaceX must be the surviving/acquiring entity:** Bhakdi explains that because Elon Musk holds supervoting shares on the SpaceX side, the surviving legal entity must be SpaceX — otherwise Musk would lose his super-majority voting control. He estimates Musk would retain roughly 78–80% voting power in the combined entity regardless of the ownership ratio chosen.

3. **The "merger of equals" debate and its critics:** The video references an ongoing debate in the Tesla investment community. Commentators Alexandra Merz and Larry Goldberg (per the host, unconfirmed) have argued the deal should be structured as a merger of equals — where Tesla shareholders simply receive SpaceX shares at whatever the market caps happen to be at closing, with no fixed price. Bhakdi's counterargument is that this structure is fundamentally flawed: it asks Tesla shareholders to vote on a deal where the payout could range anywhere from roughly $900 billion to $4 trillion in SpaceX shares, with no way to know in advance. He argues this creates unresolvable fiduciary duty problems, accounting problems, and institutional shareholder pushback — and draws an analogy to Nvidia attempting a no-price merger of equals with Google.

4. **The ratchet mechanism as Bhakdi's proposed solution:** Bhakdi proposes what he calls a "ratchet" structure. SpaceX would make a fixed-dollar offer for Tesla — he uses $600 per share as a working example (representing roughly a 50% premium to Tesla's then-current price of roughly $400), implying a Tesla acquisition value of approximately $1.88 trillion. The ratchet would guarantee Tesla shareholders receive at least $600 per share worth of SpaceX shares. If SpaceX's market cap rises above $1.88 trillion by the time of the vote, the deal adjusts upward for SpaceX holders (so they are not diluted beyond a merger-of-equals ratio). If SpaceX falls below $1.88 trillion, Tesla shareholders still receive their floor price — potentially owning more than 50% of the combined entity. This structure, Bhakdi argues, gives Tesla shareholders certainty to vote yes while still being defensible to SpaceX shareholders.

5. **SpaceX share float unlock as a compounding risk factor:** Bhakdi flags a specific near-term risk: he states that an announcement timed for end of July 2026 would coincide with a share float unlock for SpaceX representing approximately 400% of the current share float, entering the market for selling between August and mid-October 2026. He argues this creates massive selling pressure on SpaceX shares in exactly the window between a potential announcement and the shareholder vote — making the volatility risk of a no-floor merger of equals even more acute.

6. **Lock-step trading and its limits:** The discussion addresses whether Tesla and SpaceX shares would trade in lock-step once a merger is announced. Both host and guest agree they would tend to trade together — but Bhakdi qualifies this: lock-step trading only holds to the extent the market believes the Tesla shareholder vote will pass. If SpaceX drops sharply and Tesla shareholders look likely to vote no, lock-step breaks down, introducing further uncertainty. A ratchet/floor, Bhakdi argues, is the mechanism that makes the vote outcome more certain and therefore sustains lock-step trading.

7. **Historical precedent for mergers of equals:** The host notes that mergers of equals do have historical precedents — citing Dow Chemical and DuPont, Exxon and Mobil, AOL and Time Warner, Daimler-Benz and Chrysler, and Citicorp and Travelers Group. He also notes that such deals typically include "double-trigger walk-away provisions" that allow either party to exit if one company's value deviates more than approximately 20% from the announcement price. Bhakdi acknowledges these precedents but argues none of them combined the sheer scale of a Tesla-SpaceX deal with the level of volatility present in SpaceX's current share structure — and that walk-away provisions are not the same as a ratchet (walk-away kills the deal; a ratchet preserves it with adjusted terms).
