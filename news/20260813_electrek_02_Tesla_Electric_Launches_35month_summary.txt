Tesla Energy launched a "Powerwall Lease" in select Texas retail-choice areas that bundles two leased Powerwall units with enrollment in Tesla Electric's Backup plan, bringing the effective monthly cost down to about $35 plus tax in year one.

## Energy

1. **How the pricing works:** A standard lease for two Powerwalls costs about $122/month in year one, with a 3% annual escalator. Enrolling in Tesla Electric's Backup plan provides an $87 monthly credit, reducing the net cost to roughly $35 plus tax. There's a one-time $100 order fee, though Tesla markets the offer as "$0 installation." The credit only activates after installation, permission to operate, and successful enrollment, and it disappears if the customer drops Tesla Electric or fails to meet program requirements.

2. **Program restrictions:** The lease is limited to select Texas areas with retail electric choice; solar panels are not eligible, making this a battery-and-electricity bundle rather than a solar offering. Non-standard installations requiring electrical upgrades or special permitting can cost extra and may affect credit eligibility. Included features are Storm Watch (pre-storm battery charging) and app-based management. Tesla states it has installed more than one million Powerwall units worldwide.

3. **Business rationale:** The article explains that the $87 credit is funded by Tesla's ability to monetize customers' stored battery energy through its virtual power plant, dispatching it to the ERCOT grid during demand/price spikes — the same model behind a prior program in which Tesla paid Powerwall owners $10 million through virtual power plants, but now built into the lease rather than paid as a check. The lease also locks customers into Tesla Electric as their retail provider. This follows Tesla's revival of its solar/Powerwall leasing business in October (2025) and a Cybertruck vehicle-to-grid program launched in Texas in February (2026) that pays owners for grid feed-in.

## Editorial Stance
Electrek's Fred Lambert views the deal as potentially attractive for some Texans facing high electricity rates, since it turns a normally five-figure Powerwall purchase into a low monthly bill that beats a standby generator on cost and convenience. However, he cautions readers to read the fine print: the underlying lease is $122/month with a 3% annual escalator, the low rate depends on staying with Tesla Electric and granting Tesla dispatch rights over the batteries, switching providers makes backup much pricier, and the escalator widens the cost gap over time. He also notes mixed reviews of Tesla Electric to date. He frames this as a template for future expansion, noting Tesla is the only company that owns the battery, software, VPP, and electricity retailer simultaneously, and separately recommends pairing home batteries with solar and suggests using EnergySage for solar quotes (a sponsored recommendation).
