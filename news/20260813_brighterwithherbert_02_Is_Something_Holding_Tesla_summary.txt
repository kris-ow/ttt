This panel discussion (Jeff Lutz, Alexandra Merz, and James from Invest Answers) covers weak Tesla stock sentiment despite a rebound, works through several "conspiracy theories" circulating about Tesla and SpaceX, and debates the timing, structure, and shareholder implications of a potential Tesla-SpaceX merger. The panel's core thesis: none of the popular FUD narratives (compensation loophole, index-fund manipulation, robotaxi sandbagging, data-center prioritization) hold up under scrutiny, and both stocks remain undervalued relative to price targets, with a merger — whenever it happens — likely to be the dominant near-term catalyst.

## Financials
1. **TSLA underperformance vs. peers:** A panelist noted that while the NASDAQ and S&P are both up roughly 15% year-to-date and most Magnificent Seven stocks are up 12–25% through the first seven-eight months of the year, Tesla is down 27%, which he ties to unclear forward guidance from Tesla's earnings calls this year compared to SpaceX's clearer revenue-doubling/tripling framework.
2. **Wall Street price targets vs. current price:** James cited average Wall Street price targets of roughly $500 for Tesla and $231 for SpaceX (58% above SpaceX's last close), versus a Tesla price he pegs at $330–$340; he stated his own fair value estimate for Tesla is roughly $495–$499, rising to about $698 with a ~40% merger premium, or ~$798 if Cybercab ramps and an Optimus 3 unveil occurs.
3. **Elon's compensation package "loophole" claim addressed:** Alexandra Merz explained that under Tesla's 2025 proxy, a change-of-control merger scenario (Elon holding >50% of the combined voting rights) would cause operational milestones to fall away, leaving only market-cap milestones (up to $8.5T for the full trillion-dollar package, with smaller tranches at $2T/$2.5T/$3T market cap). If there's no change of control, all original milestones stay in place. She stressed this was already publicly known months ago and could still be renegotiated in any merger agreement.
4. **SpaceX options deadline flagged:** A newly filed SEC 13G showed Elon Musk holding roughly 6.44 million SpaceX shares (about 48% of ~13 billion outstanding), including 350 million exercisable Class B options (strike price around $8, from an approximate 2015 grant) expiring end of August; Alexandra Merz said exercise could be via a ~$3 billion cash payment or a net-settlement (yielding roughly 330 million shares), and flagged this as a possible internal deadline ahead of any merger announcement — explicitly caveated as unconfirmed since she lacks the original contract terms.
5. **Merger ratio and shareholder-vote mechanics debated:** Alexandra Merz estimated Tesla retail ownership at roughly 22% of shares, with institutions holding over 55%; she calculated Elon could reach a 50% "yes" vote via institutional support alone without retail, using an estimated ~3.5 million votable Tesla shares (excluding ~400 million unvested compensation shares) and Elon's ~20% (700 million shares). She said she'd like to see Elon set an exchange-ratio floor (she floated $600–$690 for Tesla under a merger) to protect shareholders from SpaceX share-price volatility before a vote, and estimated a fair merger multiplier around 4.0–4.2x to preserve Elon's ~50% combined voting stake — all framed as her own hopes/speculation, not confirmed terms.
6. **Kalshi prediction market on merger odds:** James cited a Kalshi market showing less than 50% odds of a Tesla-SpaceX merger announcement within a year; Alexandra Merz was skeptical of prediction-market framing, noting her own polls of followers showed people interpret such questions inconsistently.

## Robotaxi
1. **Cybercab sandbagging conspiracy rebutted:** Jeff Lutz argued Tesla has missed its own 2026 robotaxi milestones but attributed this to safety/quality diligence rather than intentional throttling, citing recurring reports of ~20-minute pickup/drop-off "looping" issues in Austin. He said unsupervised vehicle count grew from about 30 in April to an estimated 90–100 now, with mile-doubling occurring roughly every 5–8 weeks, approaching the previously stated goal of monthly doubling.
2. **Nevada permit clarified:** Tesla received Nevada Transportation Authority approval to operate; while headlines cited approval for up to 5,000 vehicles, the actual permit is limited to 10 vehicles initially, consistent with a typical phased rollout pattern.
3. **Cybercab fleet buildout observed:** James noted new sightings of Cybercabs without steering wheels/pedals (43 of 44 in one group), some bearing first-responder QR stickers, and estimated 1,500–2,000 Cybercabs already deployed and staged across the US awaiting wider rollout, though he does not know the exact timing.
4. **Capital allocation seen as evidence against a "hidden problem":** Jeff Lutz argued that if Tesla had a deeper unresolved issue, it would show up in reduced capex/requisitions or halted vehicle production; instead, requisitions and vehicle deployment continue, which he takes as a signal that any current issue is software-related and likely resolvable in a shorter timeframe.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Data-center buildout defended against FUD:** Panelists rejected comparisons between SpaceX's data center buildout timeline and Tesla's FSD delays, arguing data center construction (sourcing, permitting, labor) is operationally simpler and more predictable than solving autonomy, and citing Neocloud companies (CoreWeave, Nebius) reportedly having sold out or nearly sold out 2027 data-center capacity as evidence of real demand.
2. **Colossus/XAI rationale explained:** Alexandra Merz argued Tesla built its own Colossus GPU clusters primarily for FSD development, and that any "why didn't Tesla rent out compute" critique misunderstands that GPUs were repurposed for Grok/XAI only after Tesla's own FSD needs were met — though she and Jeff Lutz both said they still wish XAI had been built inside Tesla rather than as a separate entity.
3. **SpaceX compute economics cited:** James referenced a two-day-old Elon Musk statement claiming a path to roughly 10 gigawatts of compute, charged at $30–$50 per watt, implying potential annual recurring revenue of $300–$500 billion by the end of 2027 — a scenario James flagged as producing very large valuations under a price-to-sales approach, without disclosing specific multiples.

## Market & Competition
1. **Index inclusion "conspiracy" rebutted:** A FTSE Russell letter to New York pension officials reportedly confirmed the firm never coordinated with Elon Musk or SpaceX before its rule change on fast-track index entry, and that the change was driven by index users/advisory committees throughout the prior year. Alexandra Merz added that SpaceX's initial index inclusions only satisfied about a third of target weighting (currently ~1% of NASDAQ, versus an implied ~3%), with further rebalancing events expected in September and December, and a potential future move from the consumer-discretionary bucket to tech if the combined entity's revenue becomes AI-dominated.
2. **TAM comparisons:** James cited Ark Invest figures of a $30 trillion TAM for Optimus and a $10 trillion (elsewhere stated as $40 trillion combined with Cybercab) TAM for autonomous ride-hailing, framing these as larger than the compute market for the next several years.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Optimus production timeline (unconfirmed):** James said unnamed sources ("little birds") suggest Optimus could enter production as early as September, but that early units may be kept in-house for training purposes ("building their academy") rather than shown publicly — explicitly caveated as secondhand and speculative.
2. **Optimus 3 reactions and thesis risk:** Herbert noted meeting two people who'd seen Optimus 3, with one calling it "creepy" and another "sublime" (no further detail given). Jeff Lutz and Alexandra Merz argued Tesla's Optimus program remains unproven — no confirmed useful real-world task performance yet — with the key gating factor being generalized robotic intelligence rather than hardware/manufacturing scale, which panelists view as comparatively de-risked.
