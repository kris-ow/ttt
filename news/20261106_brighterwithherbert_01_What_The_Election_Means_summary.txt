A panel of three Tesla/SpaceX bulls (Alexandra Merz, Jeff Lutz, and Cern Basher) on the brighterwithherbert channel discuss the post-election selloff in TSLA and SPCX stock, debating whether Democratic gains in Texas and Congress threaten AI data center buildout, robotaxi expansion, and the prospective SpaceX-Tesla merger. The panel's consensus is that market fear is overblown relative to the structural and legal realities governing Texas regulatory bodies, data center permitting, and merger mechanics.

## Market & Competition
1. **Post-election selloff:** TSLA and SPCX both sold off sharply after the election; SPCX fell below its $135 IPO price and TSLA fell below $350, with the panel noting the drop (described as roughly 10%, about $41) occurred against a backdrop of broader market volatility (the host cites NASDAQ up then down on subsequent days), though Tesla and SpaceX were hit harder than the overall market.
2. **Texas governance structure limits immediate political risk:** Panelists argue Texas's unusual biennial legislature (meeting roughly 5 months every two years) and staggered six-year terms on regulatory commissions (TCEQ for environmental/data center permitting, PUCT for utilities, and the Texas DMV board for autonomous vehicles) mean a newly elected Democratic governor cannot quickly replace commissioners or rewrite rules — the next commissioner turnover is cited as not occurring until August 2027, with Senate confirmation not until 2029, and the Texas Senate remains Republican-controlled.
3. **Texas governor-elect's actual data center stance is more moderate than headlines suggest:** Per the panel (citing Gavin Baker), governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has said all currently permitted Texas data centers will be allowed to finish construction, covering projects through roughly 2027 and into 2028; new rules for data centers permitted for 2028-29 delivery would require paying taxes, no consumer power price increases, protecting water availability, union labor, and local input. Analyst Dan Ives is cited estimating the Democratic sweep could slow AI data center buildout pace and that roughly 10% of a planned 800-1,000+ U.S. data center pipeline may not happen.
4. **Ted Cruz signals willingness to work with Musk on business, not politics:** In a cited TV exchange, Cruz said he remains a fan of Tesla and SpaceX and "a great business leader," but objects to Musk spending (cited at $200 million) against his opponent and using wealth to influence politics — drawing a line between opposing Musk's political spending and supporting his companies' technology. Musk reportedly responded "Nice of him to say that."
5. **If data centers are constrained in the US, demand moves elsewhere:** Jeff Lutz argues that globally constrained components (DRAM, cooling, GPUs) are unlikely to see cancelled orders, that ample land (including federally designated land) exists in the US, and that if domestic buildout is slowed, demand for "super intelligence" compute will simply be built in other countries with faster permitting — which Lutz calls "the globalization of super intelligence." He also argues artificial constraints would raise the cost per unit of compute, benefiting compute sellers like SpaceX.
6. **Musk's personal safety and political strategy flagged as a concern:** Alexandra Merz expresses concern about Musk's personal security given intensified personal attacks (naming Hillary Clinton as a critic) and calls on Musk to rethink his political donation strategy, contrasting his approach unfavorably with how other major donors operate; she argues the Democratic attacks stem specifically from his political spending rather than from his roles as an EV maker or SpaceX owner.
7. **No precedent for a state blocking a federally approved merger:** Jeff Lutz states he researched the question and found no precedent in several decades of a state attorney general successfully blocking a merger that cleared federal approval, pushing back on narratives that the election outcome threatens the Tesla-SpaceX merger prospects.

## Autonomous Driving / Robotaxi
1. **Texas has statewide preemption protecting AV operations:** Cern Basher notes Texas passed a 2017 law giving the state (not local cities/municipalities) authority over autonomous vehicle regulation, which the panel believes insulates robotaxi rollout from local political shifts; they expect no changes to Tesla's robotaxi expansion in Texas and note Waymo (described as aligned with Democratic-leaning support) continues expanding elsewhere without regulatory pushback, which the panel sees as a positive read-through for Tesla's own regulatory prospects.
2. **No federal AV framework emerged, and panel doesn't expect one:** Jeff Lutz notes a federal regulatory framework for autonomous vehicles did not materialize and doesn't expect it to be a near-term priority; robotaxi expansion is expected to continue being negotiated state-by-state and city-by-city rather than nationally.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **SpaceX's compute expansion plans and Arizona grid investment:** SpaceX is cited as planning to scale from roughly 3 gigawatts to 13 gigawatts of compute next year, with a further potential expansion path to around 20 gigawatts; the panel discusses a new Arizona data center project (cited at 6 gigawatts) where SpaceX is reportedly committing $3.6 billion to strengthen grid transmission capacity, which the panel says is projected to reduce electricity rates for area utility customers by 10-40% and is estimated to bring about 1,000 high-paying jobs and 5,500 construction jobs — structured specifically to preempt Democratic concerns about rising consumer electricity prices.
2. **Elon Musk's "data centers in space" remark:** Musk reportedly reacted to the political uncertainty by saying orbital data centers are "looking pretty appealing right now," which the panel interprets as both a genuine strategic hedge against terrestrial permitting friction and a signal that a moratorium would affect competitors more than SpaceX specifically.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation / Financials
1. **Merger premium calculation anchored to October 20 announcement date, per Merz's Wall Street contacts:** Alexandra Merz says she consulted multiple Wall Street contacts (unnamed) who told her — and she says she's about 95% confident — that the date SpaceX and Tesla announced their special merger committees (October 20, during the SpaceX earnings call held ahead of Tesla's) will serve as the anchor/reference date for merger premium calculations, intended to prevent speculative price swings since that announcement from skewing the deal math; she argues this makes the current post-election stock decline less consequential to the eventual merger terms than headlines suggest.
2. **Shareholder vote math requires a high approval threshold:** Merz estimates that because roughly 30% of Tesla shares won't vote, about 7 out of 10 of the shares that do vote need to vote "yes" just to clear a 50% threshold of outstanding shares — and that hitting only 50% would likely trigger subpoena/legal scrutiny, so she believes the deal needs 8, 9, or closer to 10 out of 10 "yes" votes among active voters to pass comfortably, which she argues requires a highly attractive premium, ideally with a price floor protecting against SpaceX share volatility.
3. **Comparison to a recent troubled M&A deal:** Jeff Lutz cites the Marvell-Lmentum fka acquisition, where both companies' stocks fell roughly 40% amid no deal collar/floor and dilution concerns, as a cautionary example he hopes the Tesla/SpaceX special committees take note of when structuring deal protections.
4. **Low litigation risk from a small shareholder base:** Merz notes that both companies being Texas-domiciled means a derivative lawsuit would require holding 3% of shares, a threshold she says is met by only a handful of major holders (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street on Tesla's side; Valor, Founders Fund/Peter Thiel, and Google on SpaceX's side), most of whom she judges unlikely to pursue such a suit.
