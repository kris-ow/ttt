This video from brighterwithherbert features host Herbert and guest Larry Goldberg, a serial entrepreneur and venture capital investor, discussing a wave of regulatory milestones for autonomous vehicles alongside the scrutiny Tesla continues to face over FSD-related incidents. The central argument is that the regulatory environment is meaningfully improving for the entire AV industry — including Tesla's Cybercab — but that Tesla is held to a far higher public and media standard than competitors like Waymo, making a cautious, deliberate rollout strategically necessary.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **NHTSA eliminates manual brake pedal requirement for fully autonomous vehicles:** NHTSA announced it will remove the mandate for hand- or foot-operated brake controls on vehicles designed never to be operated by a human. Existing rules continue to apply to AVs that retain manual controls, and all vehicles must still meet the same stopping distance performance criteria via alternative testing procedures. The host notes this builds on a 2022 loosening of rules that had already removed steering wheel and pedal requirements in principle — the new announcement solidifies and extends that framework specifically for brake controls.

2. **United Nations adopts first-ever global regulatory framework for autonomous driving:** The UN Economic Commission for Europe (UN ECE) adopted the world's first global technical regulation for automated driving systems, with a positive vote expected in June 2026. The framework incorporates elements of the U.S. DOT's AV framework and is intended to help streamline AV approvals across Europe and other participating countries. Larry Goldberg cautions that Europe has a history of layering additional regulatory complexity onto global frameworks, and notes that Tesla's association with Elon Musk makes it a political football in Europe, creating further uncertainty about whether full autonomy will materialize there on any near-term timeline.

3. **NTSB opens investigation into Texas crash; Tesla disputes FSD involvement:** The NTSB has joined NHTSA in investigating a recent Texas crash in which a woman in a house was killed when a vehicle struck it. Tesla and Ashok Elluswamy stated that telemetry data shows the driver had the accelerator floored the entire time, reaching 73 mph, and that the car was not operating under FSD in the manner portrayed. The family of the victim has filed a lawsuit against both the driver (identified as Michael Butler) and Tesla, alleging negligence. The host and guest emphasize this will not be the last such incident and that media coverage consistently attributes crashes to FSD regardless of actual fault.

4. **FSD safety vs. public perception disconnect:** Larry Goldberg argues that supervised FSD is already demonstrably safer than human driving and cites a personal anecdote from the same day — FSD automatically stopped his vehicle when a car ran a red light and turned directly in front of him at speed. He contends that even a genuine FSD-caused fatality in the future will be covered crash-by-crash rather than in the context of the dramatic overall reduction in road deaths that autonomy could deliver. The host notes the U.S. sees roughly 44,000 motor vehicle deaths per year, with approximately 30% attributed to texting, and argues that autonomous driving could reduce that figure dramatically even if it cannot reach zero.

5. **Waymo accumulates serious operational incidents while receiving minimal media scrutiny:** Dillon Loomis is cited as having compiled a list of recent Waymo incidents that the host argues have received far less public attention than any Tesla incident would. These include: hitting a child; illegally passing school buses with active red lights; fully blocking a street and obstructing ambulance and first responders during a mass shooting; six vehicles failing to recognize ramp closure signs and driving into active freeway construction zones in Texas; seven vehicles driving between cones and entering freeway construction lanes in California — prompting Waymo to voluntarily restrict its entire fleet from all freeway and highway operations. NHTSA also recalled approximately 3,800 Waymo vehicles after they drove into flooded roads, and Waymo suspended service in Atlanta, Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, and Nashville. The host argues Tesla could likely already scale beyond its current deployment but will not receive the same lenient treatment from media and regulators that Waymo appears to get.

## Robotaxi

1. **Regulatory clarity may be a factor in Tesla's Cybercab rollout pace:** The host speculates that Tesla may have been waiting for regulatory paperwork — specifically, formal confirmation that vehicles without steering wheels or brake pedals are permissible — before scaling Cybercab deployment on its robotaxi service. He notes that Tesla was able to self-certify the Cybercab without steering wheels or pedals under the existing framework, unlike Zoox, which sought self-certification and was denied by NHTSA due to insufficient crash test data. The guest suggests the delay reflects Tesla designing deliberately for true scale rather than a forced slowdown.

2. **Zoox unveils refined robotaxi vehicle, targets 100 units per week:** Zoox revealed a refined version of its robotaxi, featuring elevator-style sliding doors, face-to-face bench seating with no steering wheel or pedals, improved seat padding and ergonomics, an updated touchscreen, redesigned wireless charging, larger cup holders, and a two-way audio system. The vehicle is intended for Zoox's mass-production fleet, to be built at its Hayward manufacturing facility, with a stated target of up to 100 units per week. Larry Goldberg dismisses 100 units per week as nowhere near true mass production, arguing that scale requires something closer to 100 units per day at minimum, and that Zoox's volume cap (noted by the host as approximately 2,500–2,600 vehicles under current self-certification limits) makes it fatally constrained as a business. He contrasts this with Tesla's stated ambition of producing one Cybercab roughly every two minutes — approximately 30 per hour.

3. **Tesla's scale advantage remains decisive despite delays:** Both the host and guest agree that Tesla is uniquely positioned to reach the scale required to replace human drivers in meaningful numbers, pointing to its existing production infrastructure, its fleet of millions of consumer vehicles gathering real-world FSD data, and its purpose-built Cybercab. Larry Goldberg frames Waymo and Zoox as "playing at the edges" — producing on the order of 5,000 vehicles per year each — and argues that robotaxi economics only work at a scale of hundreds of millions of vehicles. He also notes the Cybercab will almost certainly be less expensive to produce than the Zoox vehicle. The host acknowledges the rollout has taken longer than anticipated but argues Tesla's higher self-imposed safety standard is the correct strategic choice given the asymmetric media and regulatory risk it faces.

## Market & Competition

1. **New York City blocking autonomous vehicle entry; pattern mirrors Uber resistance:** The host notes that New York City has effectively said no to Waymo operating there, drawing a parallel to the city's earlier resistance to Uber. He argues NYC has a pattern of using regulatory tools to block innovation rather than promote it, citing also the city's rejection of Amazon and Citadel headquarters. Larry Goldberg describes the broader dynamic as cities becoming resistant to change, though neither speaker expects this to ultimately stop the national or global trajectory toward autonomy.

2. **Rivian and Lucid called out as noise, not competitors:** The host briefly dismisses Rivian and Lucid as companies whose autonomous or robotaxi ambitions will remain "talk and promises for years to come" with no real scale or volume, contrasting them with Tesla's actual deployment progress.
