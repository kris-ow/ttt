This video is an interview hosted by Herbert (brighterwithherbert) with Andrew Kang, CEO of Robo Strategy (NASDAQ: BOT), and Dr. Scott Walter, Robotics Research Diligence Director at Robo Strategy. The core thesis is that the humanoid robotics market is massive — estimated at $5 trillion by 2050 — and while Tesla will be a major winner, multiple companies (particularly Figure AI and others) will also achieve trillion-dollar valuations, creating an investment opportunity that Robo Strategy aims to democratize for retail investors through its newly public fund focused on private robotics companies.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Elon Musk's role in catalyzing the humanoid industry:** Scott Walter argues that Elon Musk did more than ride a technological wave — he effectively created it. By publicly championing humanoid robots around 2021, he removed the social stigma from the field, allowing robotics engineers to pivot into humanoids without being dismissed by peers, and opened the door to serious venture capital. Walter draws a parallel to Steve Jobs and the smartphone, noting that while a convergence of enabling technologies was necessary, a catalyzing figure was also required to bring it all together.

2. **Deployment timeline and where the industry stands today:** Scott Walter characterizes 2026 as the beginning of the deployment phase, with more meaningful visibility expected in 2027 and clear, undeniable progress by 2030. He notes that demand signals have shifted dramatically — Fortune 500 companies and small businesses alike are now asking not "should we invest?" but "how do we get humanoids in for testing?" This interest spans the US, Europe, and other regions previously expected to be conservative adopters.

3. **Figure AI's manufacturing progress and road to scale:** Scott Walter reports that Figure was producing approximately 250 robots per month as of last month (May 2026), roughly one robot every 30 minutes. He describes this as part of a deliberate ramp-up since January 2026. Figure has locked the design for Figure 4 and is already setting up manufacturing infrastructure ("Bot Q") and supply chain processes so that the next generation launch does not start from scratch. Walter stresses that Figure has been using casting for components rather than CNC machining from aluminum billets — a key differentiator from Chinese manufacturers who rely heavily on CNC, a method that hits a production ceiling around 1,000 units and cannot scale an order of magnitude without transitioning to casting and stamping.

4. **China's robotics industry vs. American companies — investment thesis:** Andrew Kang distinguishes between technological progress and investable outcomes. He notes that Chinese humanoid companies benefit from heavy government funding and hundreds of companies competing, but this competition compresses margins to the bone — analogous to BYD selling more cars than Tesla while holding a market cap roughly 5% of Tesla's. American companies face lower domestic competition, can command premium pricing, and are shielded from Chinese import competition by likely tariffs (mirroring the 100% tariff on Chinese EVs under the Biden administration) and national security legislation being pushed through Congress that would restrict Chinese robots — which can see and hear everything in their environment — from being deployed in US homes and businesses.

5. **Physical AI intelligence timeline — GPT-3 to GPT-5 analogy:** Andrew Kang estimates that robots are currently at roughly the GPT-3 level of physical intelligence — sometimes useful, but unreliable. He argues the path from GPT-3 to GPT-5 equivalent for physical AI will be compressed to roughly one to two years (rather than the three or more years it took for LLMs) because many research innovations — post-training techniques, RLHF analogs, data filtering, annotation infrastructure — can be transferred from the LLM world. Once intelligence reaches that level, manufacturing and supply chain will become the binding constraint, with hundreds of thousands of robots possible in approximately two years and hundreds of millions to billions plausible in the 2030s.

6. **Simulation vs. real-world data and the "sim-to-real gap":** The two guests debate the minimum number of physical robots needed in the real world to close the sim-to-real gap. Both agree a minimum of roughly 1,000 physical robots is needed. Walking/locomotion can be simulated with reasonable fidelity, but manipulation — especially with deformable objects or fluids — is extremely difficult to simulate accurately. "World models" (neural simulation, built on video generation models like Sora) are emerging as a solution, allowing robots to develop an intuitive sense of how the world responds to actions without explicit physics modeling. Data collection methods are also rapidly evolving, from teleoperation with physical robots to data gloves to pure egocentric video capture.

7. **Emerging robot capabilities in 2026:** Andrew Kang highlights "language steering" — the ability to give a robot a plain-language instruction like "put that cup in the dishwasher" and have it execute — as an early capability now coming online. He also points to in-context learning (analogous to LLM emergent capabilities), where a robot can be shown a task once and remember the instruction for subsequent execution, as an exciting near-term development that is beginning to appear in frontier physical AI models.

8. **Path Robotics and Standard Bots — portfolio company highlights:** Scott Walter highlights two Robo Strategy portfolio companies. Path Robotics uses Motorman arms augmented with a decade of proprietary arc-welding process data, addressing one of the largest industrial robot applications and a worsening shortage of skilled welders. Standard Bots recently closed its Series C (led by Robo Strategy) and is building robot arms in America — the first American-made industrial arms since Unimation in 1961, a market previously dominated by Japanese, German, Swedish, and more recently Chinese manufacturers.

9. **Andrew Kang's early conviction in Figure AI:** Kang made an initial investment in Figure AI when it was valued at $2.6 billion post-money, and ultimately invested $19 million. At the time, most of his venture capital peers were skeptical because the robotics space had not previously produced venture-scale outcomes. He was convinced by founder Brett's track record, and by the team's pedigree drawn from Google DeepMind, Tesla, Boston Dynamics, and Apple. Figure is now valued at $39 billion. Kang believes Figure, Tesla, and other leading humanoid companies could ultimately be worth trillions to tens of trillions of dollars.

## Market & Competition

1. **Robo Strategy's structure and investment mandate:** Robo Strategy (NASDAQ: BOT) went public last month (May 2026) as a fund designed to give retail investors access to private robotics companies — a market previously accessible only to venture capitalists and well-connected insiders via SPVs (which typically charge a 20% carry on top of management fees). Robo Strategy charges a 2.5% management fee with no carry, which Kang argues compares favorably to the traditional VC structure of 2% management fee plus 20% performance fee. The fund focuses on private companies across the full robotics stack: humanoid robots, industrial arms, cobots, actuators, batteries, and other supply chain components.

2. **$2 billion equity facility and NAV dynamics:** Shortly after its NASDAQ listing, Robo Strategy announced a $2 billion equity facility with capital partner Roth, enabling the fund to raise additional capital by issuing new shares to invest in more private companies. Kang clarifies this is not leverage but an at-the-market equity issuance mechanism, to be deployed gradually and only when accretive to shareholders (i.e., when the stock trades above NAV). NAV is marked based on each portfolio company's last funding round valuation, with a lag of several weeks to a month for audit and administration. Kang notes that private market valuations are structurally lower than public market valuations for the same assets due to the much smaller pool of private market buyers, creating a structural arbitrage opportunity for the fund.

3. **Chinese robots and tariff/national security barriers to US market entry:** Kang argues Chinese humanoid robots face the same structural barriers to US market penetration as Chinese EVs — tariffs (the Biden administration applied 100% tariffs to Chinese EVs) and national security legislation. Bills currently moving through Congress would likely impose similar restrictions on Chinese robots, given the surveillance risk of devices that can see and hear inside American homes and businesses. This makes the US domestic robotics market largely captive to American companies from an investment standpoint.
