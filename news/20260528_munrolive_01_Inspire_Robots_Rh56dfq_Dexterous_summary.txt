This video from Munro Live features Mike Kasein, a senior electrical engineer at Munro and Associates, demonstrating and explaining the Inspire Robotics RH56DFQ dexterous robotic hand. The video serves as an introductory explainer on how robotic hands work — covering degrees of movement, actuation mechanisms, and sensor design — while previewing a forthcoming full teardown of the unit. The core argument is that robotic hands are among the most mechanically complex components of humanoid robots, and the video positions this as the first in a series exploring how these components are built.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Inspire Robotics RH56DFQ Overview:** The hand being demonstrated is the Inspire Robotics RH56DFQ, a six-degrees-of-freedom robotic hand. Six degrees of movement means the hand can move in six distinct ways — covering the five individual fingers plus a thumb that can articulate back and forth laterally. The hand is described as a commercially available, modular unit that can be purchased independently and integrated onto compatible robots, with Python programmability supported.

2. **Degrees of Freedom Explained:** Kasein uses a cheap 3D-printed hand as a visual aid to explain the concept of degrees of movement. A single degree means a finger can only move one way (curl/extend). Additional degrees add lateral finger movement, thumb abduction, and other articulations. He notes that a human hand has significantly more degrees of movement than this unit — for example, the ability to splay fingers sideways is a degree of movement the RH56DFQ does not possess.

3. **Actuation Mechanism — Micro Linear Actuators:** The RH56DFQ uses micro linear actuators (pistons that extend and retract) to drive each finger. A spring at the top of the finger assembly provides passive return force when the actuator retracts. This is a simpler and lower-cost approach compared to alternatives such as gear-driven or individual-motor-per-joint designs used in more expensive hands.

4. **Sensor Design — Pressure at Actuator Base, Not Fingertip:** A notable design detail is that the hand does not have sensors embedded in the fingertips. Instead, pressure sensors are located at the base of each actuator. This means tactile feedback is detected through mechanical resistance transmitted down to the actuator, not via direct fingertip contact sensing. Kasein demonstrates this by showing that separating the finger from the actuator eliminates any sensor response.

5. **Pricing and Market Positioning:** The RH56DFQ is priced at approximately $10,000 per hand and is described as one of the cheaper commercially available robotic hands on the market. At the high end of the spectrum, Kasein references a hand called the "Sharper Wave" (name as stated in transcript), which costs approximately $100,000, features approximately 26 degrees of movement, has sensors in every finger, and includes cameras embedded in the fingers as well.

6. **Force Specifications:** This lower-end model is capable of approximately 4 newtons of gripping force on the fingers and 6 newtons on the thumb. Higher-spec models from the same or competing manufacturers can reach approximately 6 newtons on fingers and 12 newtons on the thumb. Kasein notes that the current unit can grip objects but would likely not maintain grip under impact or resistance.

7. **Software Demo and Control:** The hand ships with test software that includes preloaded gesture commands. Kasein demonstrates several gestures including an "okay" sign, a pointing/flicking gesture, and a closed fist. Individual finger control is also demonstrated through the software interface, with each finger adjustable independently via sliders or similar controls.

8. **Upcoming Teardown Series:** Kasein announces that a full teardown of the RH56DFQ is planned as a follow-up video, and that Munro Live also has plans to tear down a full humanoid robot. This video is framed as the first installment in a broader series examining humanoid robot components.
