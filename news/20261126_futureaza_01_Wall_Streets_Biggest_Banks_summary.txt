FutureAzA host Brian and Herbert of the channel Brighter with Herbert discuss the proposed Tesla–SpaceX merger. They cover the special committees and their advisers, Musk's motive of securing voting control, the premium Tesla shareholders are likely to demand, and the vote math needed for approval. Herbert thinks a merger proposal is increasingly likely, but by the end he is unusually pessimistic that the two sides can agree on terms.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation
1. **Committees and advisers named:** Herbert notes that Musk had already disclosed the special committees on a prior earnings call, so their existence is no surprise. He says the Financial Times report added real information by naming the advisers: Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs for SpaceX, and Evercore for Tesla. He treats the rest of the report skeptically, including the aim of finishing by year-end, and stresses that no final decision has been reached.
2. **Control as the core motive:** Herbert argues the main reason for the merger is that Musk wants decisive voting control. He cites the recent Nvidia episode, where in his telling the board pushed out the founder-CEO who built a $5 trillion company. He also warns that large "whale" investors could conspire to vote Musk out at Tesla, which matters given the robotics push.
   - Brian defends the Nvidia board. He suggests the departure may have been a planned resignation that allowed a later rehiring, and says the CFO was only ever meant to be interim CEO.
   - On thresholds, Brian recalls Musk saying years ago that no one should hold more than 50% control, so that investors could remove him. Herbert believes Musk would not accept less than 50% in the combined company, though Brian thinks 45–48% could work.
3. **SpaceX approval effectively rests with Musk:** As SpaceX's controlling shareholder, Musk decides the SpaceX side. Brian argues the committees would never bring a deal to a vote that Musk would not support. Musk said in a recent interview that he cannot do anything that is not economically beneficial to SpaceX investors or he would be sued.
   - Herbert relays Alexandra Merz's view, from a panel on the Cyber Bull show, that a lawsuit is unlikely given the Texas reincorporation and how the board structured the process.
   - Herbert wonders whether Musk's remark is meant to prepare Tesla shareholders for less than they want.
4. **Speculation about Google's board seat:** Herbert says Google invested about $100 million in SpaceX roughly a decade ago, a stake now worth over $20 billion, and has since added more. Google's representative Don Harrison recently left the SpaceX board. Herbert explicitly labels the following as his own guesses:
   - Harrison was on the special committee and was slowing the process.
   - He left after a deal giving Google access to SpaceX compute.
   - Herbert links that departure to the recent increase in deal momentum.
5. **Why SpaceX may balk:** Herbert says SpaceX shares have fallen significantly while its business results are exceptional. He questions whether SpaceX holders would accept giving up over 50% of the company to Tesla holders, which he calls unfair. He ends saying he no longer knows whether the two sides can agree.

## Financials
1. **Premium skepticism:** Herbert dismisses a reported $3.3 trillion figure (possibly $3.5 trillion). He says it would imply a Tesla premium of only 10–12%, which he calls "very, very unlikely."
   - He sees potential for a 20–30% premium and thinks it should be higher.
   - Some argue the deal must value Tesla at least at its historic high of $500 per share to win most shareholders.
   - Brian says many investors would vote no below $400.
2. **Which reference price:** Per Merz's logic, the fair-value reference may be locked to the day Musk disclosed the special committees on the earnings call. After that, the stock price became "questionable." She says the alternatives are the announcement-day price or a model using six weeks to three months of trading data.
3. **Tesla vote math:** Approval needs 50% of all outstanding shares. Brian assumes Musk can vote his own shares. Shareholders in some countries cannot vote, so they count as automatic no votes, and retail holders make up only about 15%. Merz's analysis is that at least 70% of those voting must vote yes, which is hard and requires a compelling premium.
4. **Institutions follow the money:** Herbert says he has asked many people for months whether institutions would oppose giving Musk super-control. The answer is consistently no: they want the premium and may sell the next day. He notes that Tesla is the only "Magnificent 7" company where shareholders hold this much power, since founders control the vote elsewhere.
5. **Succession concern:** Under the current structure, Musk's super-voting shares would pass to his children or a trust for them. Herbert calls this a serious concern that could lead large holders to demand clarity, but not one that would block the vote. Brian notes that Meta and Google super-voting shares convert to Class A upon death.
