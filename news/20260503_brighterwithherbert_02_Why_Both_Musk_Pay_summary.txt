This video from "brighterwithherbert" analyzes Elon Musk's two major performance-based compensation plans — one at SpaceX (approved January 2026) and one at Tesla (approved November 2025) — and argues that their nearly identical market cap targets are not a coincidence. The host's central thesis is that both plans were structurally designed to converge, and that the most likely endgame is SpaceX acquiring Tesla, not the reverse, due to SpaceX's dual-class voting structure giving Elon permanent control. The video also walks Tesla shareholders through what a merger would mean for their shares, and why the conversion ratio — not the headline pay numbers — is the most important number to watch.

## Financials

1. **SpaceX Compensation Plan Details:** The SpaceX board approved a new performance award in January 2026 consisting of up to approximately 200 million Class B super-voting shares plus a second tranche of roughly 60 million additional shares. Elon's base salary at SpaceX remains $54,000 per year — unchanged since 2019 — and he receives none of the equity award unless he hits extreme performance targets. The first tranche requires SpaceX to reach a $7.5 trillion market cap (roughly 4x the ~$1.75 trillion IPO valuation being targeted for a summer 2026 IPO) and the placement of 1 million humans permanently on Mars. A second tranche is tied to deploying 100 terawatts of space-based compute capacity in orbit.

2. **Tesla Compensation Plan Details:** On November 6th, 2025, Tesla shareholders approved Elon's new performance award with 75% voting yes. The plan includes up to approximately 424 million restricted shares — roughly 12% of the company — across 12 tranches over a 10-year performance period, potentially worth close to a trillion dollars if all milestones are hit. The first tranche does not begin vesting until Tesla's market cap reaches $2 trillion (nearly double from approximately $1 trillion at the time of approval). Full vesting requires Tesla's market cap to reach $8.5 trillion, EBITDA to grow from $13 billion to $400 billion (a ~30x increase), delivery of 20 million vehicles, 1 million robotaxis in commercial operation, 1 million Optimus humanoid robots delivered, and 10 million active FSD subscriptions.

3. **The Coincidence of Near-Identical Market Cap Targets:** The host highlights that SpaceX's $7.5 trillion market cap target and Tesla's $8.5 trillion target are separated by only $1 trillion — essentially a rounding error at that scale. The host argues this alignment is not coincidental but rather suggests both plans were designed with a future combined entity in mind, where the two companies settle on a blended valuation together.

4. **Tesla's Investment in xAI and SpaceX Absorption:** Tesla made a $2 billion investment in xAI, which was subsequently converted into less than 1% of SpaceX equity when SpaceX absorbed xAI. The host frames this as the "first move" in what may be a broader consolidation. SpaceX's absorption of xAI is described as already having occurred prior to the video's publication.

5. **Tesla Cash Generation and Combined Balance Sheet:** The host notes Tesla generated approximately $30 billion in cash last year, and argues that a combined Tesla-SpaceX entity would have a capital base — supplemented by SpaceX's Starlink subscription cash flows — that no other company on Earth could match, enabling simultaneous funding of Mars, Optimus, and robotaxi programs from one balance sheet.

## Market & Competition

1. **Merger Probability and Analyst Views:** Dan Ives of Wedbush stated publicly that there is an 80–90% chance Tesla and SpaceX merge by early 2027. Bloomberg reported in January (2026) that SpaceX was considering a merger with Tesla or xAI. Prediction markets, by contrast, are pricing the probability of a merger before mid-2027 at 22–26%. The host suggests the truth is somewhere between these figures, but argues the structural pressures pushing toward a merger do not disappear even if it is delayed to 2028 or 2029.

2. **SpaceX's Dual-Class Share Structure and Voting Control:** SpaceX's upcoming IPO (targeted for summer 2026) will feature a dual-class structure: Class A shares carry one vote each, while Class B shares carry 10 votes each, with Elon holding the Class B shares. Post-IPO, Elon is expected to own approximately 42% of equity but control approximately 79% of votes. Crucially, the S-1 filing reportedly states that Elon can only be removed from the SpaceX board by a vote of Class B shareholders — effectively meaning only Elon can fire Elon at SpaceX. Tesla, by contrast, has a single-class share structure (one share, one vote), which exposed Elon to legal challenges in Delaware court.

3. **Why SpaceX Would Acquire Tesla, Not Vice Versa:** The host argues that in any merger, SpaceX would be the acquiring entity because its dual-class voting structure would be preserved, giving Elon 79% voting control over the combined company. Tesla shareholders would be rolled into the new SpaceX entity. The host identifies the conversion ratio — how Tesla shares are valued against SpaceX shares — as the critical number for Tesla shareholders to monitor.

4. **The "Committee Dance" Problem:** On the Q1 2026 earnings call, Elon described the friction involved in Tesla and SpaceX collaborating on joint projects (such as the Terafab chip fabrication facility being built together in Texas). Every inter-company transaction must pass through independent committee reviews at both Tesla and SpaceX, requiring weeks of legal and board sign-off for decisions that should take days. The host argues this operational inefficiency creates direct structural pressure toward a formal merger.

5. **Potential Synergies of a Combined Entity:** The host outlines several synergies: eliminating the committee dance on joint projects; deploying Optimus robots via Starship to remote sites; integrating native Starlink connectivity into Tesla vehicles without third-party negotiations; using SpaceX's compute capacity to train Tesla's FSD models; combining capital pools; and creating a unified "physical AI" investment narrative (robots, vehicles, rockets, satellites) that is easier for Wall Street to price. The host also notes that a combined company's regulatory exposure would be spread across multiple agencies (NHTSA, SEC, FAA, FCC, DoD), reducing the ability of any single regulator to disrupt the whole enterprise.

6. **Bearcase: Absurd Market Cap Targets:** The host acknowledges that a combined $16 trillion valuation target (SpaceX $7.5T + Tesla $8.5T) would be approximately four times the size of the largest company currently in existence (~$4 trillion). The host concedes this may never be achieved, but notes that partial milestone achievement still generates massive shareholder value — hitting even one Tesla tranche requires doubling the company's market cap to $2 trillion.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD Subscription Growth:** Tesla reported 1.28 million active FSD subscriptions as of Q1 2026, representing 51% growth quarter-over-quarter. The Tesla pay plan requires reaching 10 million active FSD subscriptions as one of its full-vesting milestones, meaning Tesla currently sits at roughly 12.8% of that target.

## Robotaxi

1. **Current Robotaxi Scale vs. Milestone Target:** The host notes that Tesla currently has "a few dozen" robotaxis running in Austin, contrasting sharply with the 1 million robotaxis in commercial operation required for full vesting of the Tesla pay plan. The host also states that robotaxi miles doubled quarter-over-quarter in Q1 2026. Per RobotaxiTracker.com, the active robotaxi fleet stands at 663 vehicles as of May 3, 2026 (Bay Area 550, Austin 102, Dallas 5, Houston 6), with 32 operating unsupervised (Austin 21, Dallas 5, Houston 6).

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus Current Status vs. Milestone Target:** The Tesla pay plan requires delivery of 1 million Optimus humanoid robots as one of its full-vesting conditions. The host notes that as of the video's publication, Optimus is still being "handbuilt in a workshop," making it one of the furthest milestones from completion alongside the robotaxi target.
