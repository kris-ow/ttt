This video explores why Elon Musk and Nvidia's Jensen Huang are teaming up to put AI data centers in orbit, arguing that power and cooling bottlenecks on Earth — not chip supply — are now the binding constraint on AI growth, and that space offers a physics-based way around them. It walks through the energy, thermal, and launch-cost math behind SpaceX's emerging "Starmind"/AI satellite program and concludes the plan only works economically if Starship reaches airline-like launch cadence.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Nvidia and SpaceX co-engineering space data centers:** On the BG2 podcast, Jensen Huang said Nvidia and SpaceX engineering teams are jointly developing a new server/architecture type suited for both space and Earth. On October 15th, Musk posted that the "Starmind" computer being co-engineered by SpaceX and Nvidia is "far better than almost anyone expects" and will reach volume production next year (for the computer itself; satellites carrying them come after).
2. **Ground power bottleneck driving the space push:** Lawrence Berkeley National Lab data cited: ~2,000 gigawatts of power projects were queued to join the U.S. grid at the end of last year, but only about 1 in 8 gigawatts of queued projects between 2000–2020 ever got built; large transformer wait times are now 2–3 years; data centers used just over 4% of U.S. electricity in 2023, projected to reach roughly 7–12% by 2028. Investor Ben Horowitz relayed a conversation with Amazon CEO Andy Jassy describing compute shortages extending beyond 2028.
3. **Musk's timeline and orbital solar economics:** At Davos, Musk said the lowest-cost place for AI compute will be space within two years, three at the latest. A dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit keeps solar panels lit almost continuously, yielding roughly 5x the energy of an average ground solar farm — a figure Musk himself cites.
4. **Thermal physics of space cooling:** Jensen noted space allows no conduction or convection, only radiation, requiring large radiators. Per Musk's figures at SpaceX's first AI satellite reveal (June 8th, with SpaceX's director of satellite engineering, Ian Ellis): solar arrays assumed at 250 W/m², radiators (double-sided, knife-edge to the sun) at 1,400 W/m², working out to roughly a 69°C radiator temperature.
5. **Radiation tolerance of AI chips in orbit:** Google tested its TPU chips for radiation; memory errors only emerged at nearly three times the dose expected over five years in orbit — considered tolerable for inference but more concerning for long training runs, per Google.
6. **Scale required for 1 gigawatt of orbital compute:** Using SpaceX's own figures, 1 gigawatt of orbital computing needs about 4 square kilometers of solar panels (~750 football fields) plus ~0.7 square kilometers of radiators — roughly 6,000 satellites of SpaceX's current design, weighing 10,000–13,000 metric tons (based on SpaceX's listed ~75 kW/ton design and FCC filing target of 100 kW/ton).
7. **First-generation AI satellite specs (AI-1):** Revealed June 8th, originally sized around 120 kilowatts of computing (about one AI server rack); SpaceX's website now lists up to 250 kW peak / 175 kW average, with ~75-meter wings and a 160-square-meter radiator — consistent with the stated 1,400 W/m² radiator figure. Musk noted the AI satellite is simpler than a Starlink satellite, lacking the complex antenna arrays, mainly needing solar cells, a radiator, and laser links.
8. **Nvidia's hardware role:** Nvidia announced (August 24th) that SpaceX's planned first-generation Starmind satellite will use an optimized version of its newest Vera Rubin AI server rack; Nvidia also announced a space-specific Vera Rubin variant called Space One in March. An older Nvidia H100 chip already flies aboard a satellite from startup Starcloud, and Google launched a prototype TPU satellite on a SpaceX rocket on October 1st.
9. **Launch targets and factory plans:** Musk's stated target is for the first AI satellites to launch in late 2027, with significant scale in 2028 (no official launch schedule published). SpaceX is building a "gigasat" factory in Bastrop, Texas intended to produce everything from solar-cell wafers to finished AI satellites, with SpaceX saying thousands could begin production as soon as late 2027; Musk's goal is a gigawatt of orbital compute per year by then, "aspirationally" scaling 10x annually thereafter.

## SpaceX — Starship
1. **Launch cadence needed to make the economics work:** Google researchers estimate reaching meaningful orbital compute scale would require about 1,800 Starship launches over 10 years; SpaceX's FCC filing sketches a goal of a million metric tons launched per year, equating to roughly 10,000 Starship flights annually (about 27/day) to add 100 gigawatts of compute per year. Starship has never flown more than five times in a year to date, and Flight 14 (September 28th) marked its first reach of orbit, deploying 26 Starlink V3 satellites before an early return due to an engine loss.
2. **Cost-per-kilogram as the deciding variable:** Historical expendable rockets cost roughly $10,000–20,000/kg to orbit; SpaceX's reusable Falcon 9 cut this to about $3,600/kg; Google researchers believe a fully reusable Starship could reach about $200/kg by the mid-2030s. At Falcon 9 pricing, launching 1 gigawatt of satellites would cost about $36 billion (comparable to the ~$40 billion+ Nvidia chip cost for a 1-gigawatt data center, per Jensen Huang); at $200/kg, launch cost falls to roughly a twentieth of the chip bill (~$2 billion for 10,000 tons).
3. **Jensen Huang's framing of orbital compute ROI:** On the BG2 podcast, Huang said Nvidia's compute is productive enough that even non-trivial launch costs become a small share of total value, implying only "high-value computing," not low-cost computing, gets launched to orbit. He separately stated on Nvidia's February earnings call that space-compute economics are currently poor but expected to improve, and on another appearance in March said full realization would "take years."
