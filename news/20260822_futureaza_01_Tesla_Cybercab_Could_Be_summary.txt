This video is an interview-style discussion between the host (Brian, of futureaza) and financial modeler Cern Basher about the potential unit economics of Tesla's Cybercab robotaxi, covering pricing scenarios, utilization rates, gross profit projections, cost inputs like tires and vehicle lifespan, and the operational/legal risks currently slowing robotaxi's scale-up.

## Robotaxi
1. **Pricing path from today's rideshare rates toward a lower long-run cost:** Cern Basher frames today's average rideshare cost at roughly $2.80/mile (with a wide range, citing San Francisco fares of $7–8/mile), versus owned-vehicle operating costs of roughly $0.75–0.80/mile. He argues Tesla could undercut Uber modestly at launch (e.g., ~$2.50/mile) rather than immediately targeting Ark Invest's often-cited eventual $0.25/mile figure, with costs falling over time as fleet scale increases and per-unit operating costs drop.
2. **$0.25/mile considered achievable but scale-dependent:** Basher says he believes $0.25/mile is achievable based on his modeling, but stresses it requires substantial fleet scale and high utilization (roughly 55–65% in his model, which he corrected mid-conversation from an initial 60–80% estimate), plus a much lower per-vehicle build cost achieved only at high production volumes (he references Cybercab production well below current low-volume costs once millions or "even something less" are built).
3. **Utilization-driven gross profit model:** In a hypothetical scenario with a $1 base fare plus $1/mile, Basher's model shows a vehicle generating close to $50,000/year in revenue at 30% utilization; after a modeled 35% Tesla take rate (Tesla ~$17,000, owner ~$32,000), gross profit for the owner is about $14,000/year at 30% utilization, rising into the $30,000s and up to $41,000 at higher utilization. These figures exclude package-delivery revenue and include only a minimal allowance for advertising/entertainment revenue (assumed to accrue mostly to Tesla).
4. **Key cost assumptions — vehicle cost, tire life, charging:** Basher's model uses a $30,000 per-vehicle cost, an expected useful life of 600,000 miles, tire replacement at 60,000-mile intervals, and roughly $3,000 per set of four tires. The host argues these tire-cost and mileage assumptions are conservative (likely overstated) given Tesla's smooth driving profile and potential for high-volume tire procurement and custom tread depth; adjusting tire costs downward would add roughly $1,500–$6,000/year to gross profit depending on utilization tier.
5. **Tesla's charging/energy cost advantage over Waymo/Zoox:** The host argues Tesla's in-house charging network erases third-party charging markups that competitors like Waymo pay, and cites Cybercab's real-world (not projected) energy consumption of 165 watt-hours per mile as far below competitors, contributing to a lower operating cost basis.
6. **Idle-time monetization via distributed compute:** Basher raises distributed inference/AI compute as a potential additional revenue stream for parked Cybercabs, noting his model doesn't currently account for it; the host counters with data-security and network-availability concerns raised by two unnamed industry contacts, though notes Cybercab's onboard Starlink connectivity could mitigate the network-availability issue.
7. **Cargo/delivery as a path to raising utilization:** Both speakers suggest that reaching the higher utilization rates needed for the lowest per-mile costs will likely require off-hour cargo and package delivery (not just passenger rides), since human demand alone doesn't support round-the-clock utilization.
8. **Rollout pace constrained by legal and reputational risk, not just technology:** Basher argues Tesla's cautious, gradual robotaxi rollout reflects a deliberate tradeoff between the large revenue opportunity and the risk of lawsuits plus reputational damage from service failures (e.g., cars stuck in loops, poor drop-off accuracy); he says Tesla must model potential lawsuit rates per rides and is currently erring on the side of caution while working to improve both the legal exposure and user experience.
9. **Poor pick-up/drop-off performance attributed to lack of training data:** The host attributes ongoing complaints about robotaxi pick-up/drop-off accuracy to a lack of relevant training data (supervised FSD driving doesn't teach the system preferred drop-off spots), and speculates that an upcoming FSD version (referred to as "version 15") may integrate Grok to let riders give natural-language pick-up/drop-off instructions.
10. **Unresolved question on Uber's take rate versus Tesla's potential margin structure:** The host questions why Uber retains a large revenue share yet reportedly still struggles to turn a profit, suggesting insurance and driver-vetting costs don't fully explain it; Basher acknowledges he hasn't studied Uber's model in depth and says his own Tesla-side model includes a placeholder "black box" cost allowance for supervision and legal/operational overhead that can't be precisely broken out until Tesla actually reports on real-world robotaxi economics.
