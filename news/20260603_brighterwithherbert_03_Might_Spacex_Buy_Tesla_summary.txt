This video features a three-way discussion between host Herbert, Alexandra Merz, and Jo Bhakdi on the thesis that SpaceX will acquire Tesla following SpaceX's upcoming IPO, with speculation about the timing, structure, and valuation of such a deal. The core debate centers on whether the merger would be a one-to-one market cap deal or whether SpaceX could acquire Tesla at a significant premium — potentially two times Tesla's current valuation — and what governance and financial engineering considerations would shape the offer. The conversation draws on breadcrumbs from SpaceX's S1 amendment, share authorization numbers, Elon Musk's stock option exercise deadlines, and index fund inclusion mechanics to build a speculative but detailed case for an announcement window between end of June and August 15, 2026.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX IPO and merger thesis overview:** SpaceX is scheduled to IPO on June 12, 2026. The hosts discuss widespread speculation that SpaceX will at some point announce an acquisition of Tesla, with the community debating whether it would be a "merger of equals" (both valued at roughly $2 trillion) or whether SpaceX might offer a significant premium. Jo Bhakdi introduces the framing that a 2-to-1 acquisition — SpaceX buying Tesla at double its own valuation, i.e., $4 trillion — is not impossible, though he stresses this is not a prediction but a scenario worth modeling.

2. **Jo Bhakdi's Elon control modeling:** Jo explains that he modeled the voting and economic implications of various merger ratios for Elon Musk. His conclusion is that whether the deal is 1-to-1 or 1-to-2 in favor of Tesla, the difference in Elon's control over the combined entity is only marginal. Specifically, he notes Elon's ownership would decline from approximately 35% to approximately 29% in a scenario where SpaceX acquires Tesla at twice its own valuation. His key takeaway is that Elon has no strong incentive to push for any particular merger ratio — he is largely indifferent between the scenarios from a control standpoint.

3. **SpaceX's 36 billion authorized shares as a breadcrumb:** SpaceX currently has approximately 12.86 billion shares outstanding, yet its S1 authorizes 36 billion shares — roughly three times the total current share count. Jo argues this is a meaningful breadcrumb suggesting SpaceX has pre-authorized enough shares to acquire Tesla at up to two or even three times SpaceX's own valuation, without needing to return to shareholders for additional authorization. Alexandra Merz counters that large authorized-vs-issued gaps are standard corporate practice, citing Tesla's own history of authorizing 2 billion shares at IPO in 2010 while issuing fewer than 100 million, a ratio that held for 12 years.

4. **Jo's directional valuation scenario — SpaceX down, Tesla up:** Jo's base case prediction for the 9–12 month horizon (i.e., by approximately mid-2027) is that SpaceX will struggle to maintain its IPO valuation over time due to missing near-term earnings catalysts, while Tesla will trend significantly upward driven by robotaxi scaling. His most likely directional scenario is SpaceX at $1.5 trillion and Tesla at $3 trillion, which would make a one-to-one merger structurally difficult from Tesla shareholders' perspective.

5. **SpaceX index inclusion mechanics — six months of forced buying:** Alexandra details that SpaceX faces three major index inclusions in its first 15 trading days (MSCI, FTSE, and NASDAQ), followed by S&P 500 inclusion potentially in mid-December 2026, which she estimates could trigger over $100 billion in passive index fund purchases — larger in impact than Tesla's own S&P inclusion in 2020. She argues lockup period expiries are staggered with these inclusion waves intentionally, and that the S&P has already softened its profitability requirements and float thresholds specifically for this IPO. This six-month engineered support structure, she argues, is designed to keep SpaceX's price elevated long enough to make a merger offer attractive to Tesla shareholders.

6. **Competitive and governance risk of waiting past midterms:** Alexandra argues that one key reason to pursue the merger quickly is political. With the Trump administration currently favorable to Elon Musk's interests, delaying past the 2026 midterms risks Democratic gains in Congress leading to hostile committee hearings, subpoenas, and targeting of Tesla. She notes that a merged Tesla-SpaceX entity would be effectively untouchable given SpaceX's critical role in U.S. defense infrastructure. Her estimated timeline for the combined entity having a single ticker is Q2 2027, after a shareholder vote (likely November 2026) and approximately four to seven months of regulatory approval.

7. **Waymo and other suitors as merger complication:** The discussion briefly notes, citing a point from a commentator named Warren, that Tesla is an attractive acquisition target for parties other than SpaceX, and that a competing offer landing on the table could put Tesla's board in an awkward position and potentially delay the process.

## Financials

1. **Elon's 2018 stock option exercise deadline — August 15, 2026:** Alexandra explains that Elon's 2018 compensation package options must be exercised before August 15, 2026, converting them into restricted stock units (RSUs) that he must hold until 2033. The exercise of these options would add approximately 300 million shares to Tesla's outstanding share count. The exercise price is $23 per share, meaning the total cost to exercise is approximately $7 billion. Alexandra notes this is not a tax event (that comes in January 2028) but purely a bureaucratic purchase. The higher Tesla's share price at the time of exercise, the less Elon needs to sell, pledge, or net-exercise to cover the $7 billion cost, giving him a direct financial incentive to have Tesla's stock price elevated before August 15.

2. **Tesla outstanding shares approaching 4 billion:** Alexandra explains that Tesla's current outstanding share count, after the 2025 compensation package added approximately 400 million shares in November 2025, will reach approximately 4 billion shares once Elon exercises his 2018 options by mid-August 2026. Tesla currently has a 6 billion share authorization. She stresses that any market cap-based merger offer must be calculated using this 4 billion share count, not a smaller number, making it a critical input for valuing the deal.

3. **Merger structure debate — ratchet vs. fixed ratio:** The panel converges on a proposed deal structure: a one-to-one market cap offer with a guaranteed minimum floor price per Tesla share (a "ratchet"), so that Tesla shareholders are protected if SpaceX's price falls between announcement and the shareholder vote. Jo argues this is necessary for game-theory reasons — without a floor, uncertainty about the vote outcome would itself destabilize both stock prices. The ratchet would allow Tesla shares to move upward with SpaceX but not downward, creating certainty that would likely make the vote self-fulfilling. Example numbers discussed include a hypothetical floor of $750 per Tesla share if Tesla is at $500 at announcement, representing a roughly 50% premium.

4. **SpaceX share math limits a strict 2-to-1 deal:** Alexandra and Jo work through the arithmetic showing that a strict 2-to-1 acquisition (SpaceX buying Tesla at twice SpaceX's market cap) is mathematically constrained by the 36 billion authorized shares. With SpaceX already at approximately 13 billion shares outstanding (including shares to be issued for Grok/xAI-related entities described as marginal at ~3%), a 2-to-1 deal would require approximately 26 billion new shares plus the existing 13 billion, totaling approximately 39 billion — exceeding the 36 billion authorization. Alexandra concludes the math works for up to approximately a 1.9-to-1 ratio but not a clean 2-to-1.

5. **SpaceX S1 amendment explicitly references Tesla acquisition:** The hosts note that SpaceX's S1 amendment contains language explicitly stating that SpaceX is reserving authorized shares to acquire Tesla, which Alexandra describes as a clear and unambiguous breadcrumb embedded in an amendment rather than the main prospectus body. She suggests this placement was intentional — visible to those reading changes but not prominently featured.

6. **Tesla shareholders need 50% plus one vote to approve merger:** Alexandra clarifies the vote threshold is 50% plus one of all outstanding shares (not 66%). She identifies the key blocs needed to pass: Elon's approximately 19.09% economic interest in Tesla, plus Vanguard (already invested in both SpaceX and Tesla), BlackRock (reportedly seeking $10 billion of the SpaceX IPO), ARK Invest, and Baron Capital, plus approximately one-third of retail shareholders. She argues this coalition is achievable but notes it requires a premium juicy enough that retail shareholders holding Tesla at elevated prices won't vote no out of fear of giving up further upside.
