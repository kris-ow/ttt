This video (from creator Herbert) argues that Wall Street misjudged Tesla's Cybercab launch by evaluating it as a conventional car rather than as industrial equipment for a robotaxi network. It synthesizes commentary from Joe Tegtmeyer, Dave Lee, and an analyst referred to as Cern (Sam/Sorn Butcher), alongside Elon Musk's confirmation of a 2022 decision to build the vehicle without a steering wheel or pedals, to make the case that current "disappointing" framing misses the multi-year thesis underlying the vehicle's design.

## Robotaxi
1. **Cybercab launch and market reaction:** Tesla held a closed Cybercab launch event in Austin on a Thursday, with brief presentations from Tesla executives including Ashok Elluswamy and lead designer Ian Kettle (Musk did not attend); the Cybercab became available on the robotaxi app in the Austin service area the next day. Wells Fargo issued a report calling the event "disappointing," and TSLA shares fell about 6% that Friday.
2. **NHTSA audit launched:** On the day Cybercab became available to the public, NHTSA opened an audit into how Tesla self-certified a car lacking a steering wheel and pedals, examining roughly a thousand Cybercabs and whether Tesla's determination that certain federal safety standards don't apply to driverless cars was valid. The host frames this as a fact-finding audit, not an established violation, but notes a prolonged audit could slow scaling.
3. **Elon confirms the 2022 steering-wheel decision:** Responding to a social media post referencing Walter Isaacson's biography, Musk confirmed ("True") that in August 2022 he overruled Chief Designer Franz von Holzhausen's objections and insisted the robotaxi have no mirrors, pedals, or steering wheel, taking personal responsibility for the risk. Executives continued pushing back, and by February 2023 Franz had won approval to build both a robotaxi and a separate ~$25,000 car with a steering wheel — a second-car plan Musk later canceled in an October 2024 earnings call, which the host says explains the absence of Giga Mexico.
4. **Joe Tegtmeyer's "slider" framework:** Tegtmeyer, who attended the launch event, published an article arguing Tesla should stop framing robotaxi use as a binary (own vs. hail) and instead offer a spectrum — single trip, multi-stop session, hourly/daily block, subscription, owner-added-to-network, and full private ownership — with the same vehicle moving between modes throughout the day. He argues population density determines which points on this scale are viable in a given area (dense downtown vs. his own rural area near Canyon Lake) and that a mixed fleet (Cybercab alongside Model Y) is needed since a two-seat Cybercab can't serve families.
5. **Tegtmeyer on deployment metrics and disclosure:** In a follow-up piece, Tegtmeyer says at launch only 45 Cybercabs were registered in the Austin robotaxi fleet out of roughly 420 total robotaxis, and that most Cybercabs seen in his drone footage were test vehicles with steering-wheel kits. Passengers currently see 15–25 minute wait times with no ETA during high-demand messages; he wants Tesla to disclose real cost-per-mile (currently estimated at 50–80 cents) and show the trend over time, and wants sub-6-minute waits between 6am–10pm as a target metric to watch.
6. **Fare comparison in Austin:** The same route in Austin was priced at $12.10 for a Model Y ride versus $7.77 for a Cybercab ride, cited as early evidence the cost structure is showing up in fares.
7. **Musk's cost/price targets from October 2024:** At the "We, Robot" event, Musk said operating costs would be around 20 cents per mile with customer pricing around 30–40 cents per mile — figures other analysts use as a benchmark against current, much higher actual costs.
8. **Goldman Sachs report on Cybercab economics:** A Goldman Sachs report (dated the 6th) argued a scaled Cybercab could hold a 5–30 cents/mile cost advantage over robotaxi competitors costing 2–3x as much to build, but that robotaxi economics will ultimately be determined by how much the software can operate with less human assistance.
9. **Unattended miles growth:** Tesla reported unattended (driverless) miles rising from 380,000 in a prior quarter to 1 million by the time of the presentation; the host flags that what fraction of those miles carried an actual passenger is the next number to watch.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Dave Lee's "AI-first" vehicle thesis:** Dave Lee argues Cybercab represents a deliberate architectural break — prior Tesla vehicles were designed for human control (performance, handling) while Cybercab and its successors are designed from the ground up for AI control, prioritizing passenger comfort. He suggests unboxed manufacturing, brake-by-wire, plastic body panels, and the new drivetrain/thermal/electronics stack could underpin a family of cheaper four-, five-, six-, or seven-seat vehicles built on the same platform, though the host notes this remains a "wish list" with no confirmed Tesla plans, roughly a 3–5 year horizon by Lee's own estimate.
2. **Why the cheap Tesla never arrived:** Lee argues Musk deliberately abandoned plans for a cheaper conventional car with a steering wheel to force company-wide focus onto Cybertruck and FSD, calling it "losing his parachute" — with the tradeoff being that Cybertruck failed to deliver the promised sales volume in the interim, contributing to stalled profit growth and a stuck stock price.
3. **Vehicle durability target:** Engineers reportedly told Dave Lee the Cybercab's structure was designed to last a million miles, and its outer casing uses mass-dyed plastic requiring no paint shop — details the host flags as relayed by engineers, not independently confirmed.

## Financials
1. **Cern's lifecycle cost-per-mile framework:** An analyst referred to as Cern (Sam/Sorn Butcher) argues Cybercab should be judged like a commercial asset (delivery truck, aircraft, data-center server) meant to last 500,000+ miles, not as a consumer car. Using illustrative figures, a $30,000 vehicle depreciates to 10 cents/mile at 300,000 miles, 6 cents/mile at 500,000 miles, or 3 cents/mile at 1 million miles; spending $3,000 more to extend life from 500,000 to 750,000 miles would cut cost-per-mile from 6 cents to 4.4 cents despite the higher upfront price — implying durability may be the vehicle's most important economic lever.
2. **Design choices explained through lifecycle-cost lens:** Cern connects several Cybercab design choices to this logic: two seats (citing an April report that 82% of ride-hailing miles carry two or fewer passengers), electricity consumption of about 165 Wh/mile (~$1,650 per 100,000 miles), automated doors, and easily-swappable plastic body panels (a 15-minute panel swap vs. paint-shop repair) — with a downtime example where a $400 repair causing 3 days idle becomes an effective $850 problem in lost revenue.
3. **"Empty range" / deadhead miles as a key hidden metric:** Cern highlights the percentage of miles driven without a paying passenger as a metric Tesla hasn't disclosed; at 20 cents/mile operating cost, 80% passenger-occupancy implies a real cost of 25 cents per paid mile, while 60% occupancy pushes it to 33 cents — meaning fleet software efficiency could matter as much as the driving software itself.
