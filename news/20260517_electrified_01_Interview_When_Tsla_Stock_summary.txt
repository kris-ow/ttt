This interview features Dillon Loomis of Electrified speaking with Gene Munster, veteran tech analyst and partner at Deepwater Asset Management, about what it will take for Tesla stock to break out of its multi-year trading range. Munster frames Tesla's investment thesis around physical AI — encompassing robotaxi deployment, Optimus, and FSD — while acknowledging that near-term profitability concerns and the gap between vision and tangible results remain the key tension for investors. He also discusses Waymo's competitive position, the possibility of a Tesla-SpaceX merger, and offers a price target of $600 by end of 2027.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD Reaching Near-Zero Interventions:** Munster describes a personal breakthrough in his FSD experience over the past couple of months, noting that interventions have dropped to almost none compared to multiple interventions per day six months prior. He views this as a major inflection that most people don't fully appreciate, and recommends Tesla's two-day test drive program for skeptics to experience the technology firsthand.

2. **FSD Safety Case — 37,000 Lives Per Year at Stake:** Munster highlights that 43,000 people in the US died in car accidents in 2024. Taking Tesla's own data that Autopilot/FSD is approximately 7x safer than human driving, he argues that roughly 37,000 lives per year could be saved. He calls it "silly" that regulatory and public scrutiny continues to slow deployment of technology that demonstrably saves lives.

3. **FSD Miles Driven Still a Fraction of Total:** Drawing from the Tesla investor deck and calculations via Grok, Munster estimates that as of the March 2026 quarter, FSD-driven miles represented approximately 0.15%–0.2% of total US light passenger vehicle miles driven (which he pegs at approximately 3.3 trillion miles in 2025). He uses this to argue that the addressable market remains almost entirely untapped.

4. **Traditional Auto Falling Further Behind on Autonomy:** Munster notes that a year ago, Elon Musk reportedly reached out to traditional automakers to license FSD, and across the board they declined. He finds it ironic that legacy OEMs simultaneously claim autonomy is their long-term holy grail while refusing to invest in it, and believes this leaves them in an increasingly difficult competitive position.

## Robotaxi

1. **Waymo vs. Tesla — 50-to-1 Miles Gap:** Munster estimates Waymo is currently driving approximately 4 million miles per day, while Tesla's robotaxi operation is driving approximately 80,000 miles per day — a roughly 50-to-1 ratio in Waymo's favor. He describes the current score as "50 to zip" in a sporting analogy but maintains Tesla holds the technology lead and superior long-term cost structure.

2. **Autonomous Ride-Hailing Still Under 2% of Market:** Munster frames the scale of the current robotaxi market: US ride-hailing (primarily Uber and Lyft) accounts for just over 4 billion rides per year at an average of approximately 6 miles per ride, representing about 1.5% of total US miles driven. He estimates that approximately 2% of ride-hailing miles will be autonomous in 2026, up from roughly 1% in 2025, underscoring how early-stage the market remains.

3. **Rerating Threshold — 5,000+ Vehicles and 5–10% of Ride-Hailing:** When Dillon Loomis asks whether 1,000 robotaxis on the road would be a rerating catalyst, Munster says it is "incrementally positive" but likely already priced in given the trajectory from roughly 60 unsupervised vehicles today. He argues that a true stock rerating — a step-function re-valuation — requires Tesla reaching 5,000+ vehicles and demonstrating that 5–10% of the ride-hailing market is going autonomous, at which point the average person would viscerally understand the opportunity.

4. **Cybercab Pricing — Modest Discount, Not Aggressive Disruption:** Munster draws a parallel to Tesla's EV pricing history, noting that despite enormous scale advantages, Tesla's average selling price for Model Y/Model 3 combination is approximately $38,000–$42,000, well above the long-anticipated $25,000 entry-level vehicle. He expects Tesla's robotaxi pricing to be approximately 5–10% cheaper than Lyft/Uber rather than a dramatic market-disrupting slash, consistent with Tesla's historical behavior of monetizing scale rather than passing all savings to consumers.

5. **Waymo Has a Durable Seat at the Table:** Despite Tesla's cost and scale advantages, Munster believes both Tesla and Waymo will succeed in the robotaxi market. He notes Waymo's last valuation was approximately $120–$150 billion earlier in 2026, versus Tesla's approximately $1.6 trillion market cap, and suggests Waymo may have licensing opportunities given it doesn't manufacture vehicles. He sees Uber and Lyft in the most difficult competitive position.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus — Biggest X-Factor But No Analyst Models:** Munster states that Optimus carries more optionality value than robotaxi over the next year, precisely because the bar is so low — virtually no analyst has Optimus in their financial models. If Tesla were to shut Optimus down today, the stock would fall not because of lost earnings but because of what it would signal about innovation health at the company. He describes it as genuinely in "science project to beta" transition territory in most investors' minds, despite Tesla's official claims of beta status.

2. **Elon's 1 Million Unit Target Not Credible to Market:** Munster says "very few people" believe Elon Musk's stated goal of 1 million Optimus units next year is achievable. He notes that if Tesla announced 50,000 units, the market wouldn't object — expectations are that low. He views the problem as "orders of magnitude more difficult than autonomy on the road," and rates it as genuinely 5–10 years away from meaningful scale, a timeframe he admits signals he has "no idea" when it truly takes off.

3. **Third-Party Customer Sale as the True Rerating Catalyst:** Both Munster and Dillon Loomis agree that the true rerating event for Optimus will not be factory deployment or production announcements, but rather a credible third-party commercial customer taking delivery at scale. Munster cautions against "head fakes" like prior announcements of small pilot customers (referencing a past episode with the Tesla Semi and a California state order) that never materialized.

4. **Elon Views Optimus as Tesla's Biggest Long-Term Business:** Munster references conversations with people close to Tesla who confirm internally the company genuinely believes Optimus is its most important long-term opportunity. Elon has cited physical labor as approximately half of global GDP as the addressable market for humanoid robots.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Tesla Delivery Growth Recovering but Modest Near-Term:** Munster expects Tesla deliveries to grow approximately 10% in 2026, calling this the current consensus expectation. He sees a scenario within the next five years where growth re-accelerates to 20–30% annually, driven by the continued long-term shift to electrification and the compounding competitive disadvantage of legacy automakers who have cut EV investment by approximately one-third on average since their peak commitments.

2. **Legacy Auto in a Structural Trap:** Munster argues that traditional automakers are painting themselves into a corner — cutting EV investment now means they won't have the scale to produce EVs profitably when consumer demand accelerates. He points to Honda as an example of a brand that has effectively become invisible to consumers, and highlights General Motors' retreat from its all-electric commitment as emblematic of the strategic incoherence plaguing the industry.

3. **Electrification as an Efficiency Story, Not a Political One:** Munster frames EV adoption as an inevitability rooted in energy efficiency physics rather than politics or environmentalism — electric powertrains are simply more efficient at moving things. He believes FSD and autonomy will serve as an additional accelerant to EV adoption, elevating both the safety and convenience profile of EVs and forcing legacy auto toward a reckoning sooner than the industry's typical 15-year timeline assumption.

## Financials

1. **Capex Increase Overreaction by Market:** Munster notes that when Tesla reported its March 2026 quarter, the market reacted negatively to Tesla raising its capex guidance from more than $20 billion to more than $25 billion for calendar year 2026. He views this reaction as a "headscratcher" given Tesla holds more than $30 billion in cash and is running roughly break-even on a cash flow basis. He argues that when building infrastructure for a paradigm shift in human and vehicle movement, near-term profitability concerns are largely irrelevant.

2. **Tesla Valuation — A "Religious Question":** On valuation methodology, Munster says traditional DCF models can be made to produce almost any number and that Tesla's valuation is ultimately a "religious question" — do you believe AI will surpass human intelligence, and do you believe Tesla will infuse AI across its products? He acknowledges he could construct a $10 trillion valuation but declines to do so in the interest of intellectual honesty. He sets a price target of $600 by end of 2027 as a near-term anchor.

## Market & Competition

1. **Tesla-SpaceX Merger — Greater Than 50% Probability Within Five Years:** Munster assigns greater than 50% odds to some form of Tesla-SpaceX combination occurring within the next five years. His thesis is that SpaceX is positioning itself as a sovereign AI company — controlling the full stack from energy to chip design, manufacturing, satellite distribution (Starlink), and AI models (Grok) — and that physical AI is the one glaring gap in that stack that Tesla fills. He estimates a combined entity would be approximately a $3 trillion company today, with Nvidia at $5.7 trillion for comparison, and believes a fully integrated sovereign AI company could become the most valuable company in the world.

2. **Macro Outlook — Modest Pullback, Not a Bubble:** Munster's near-term macro view calls for approximately a 5% pullback in tech over the next six months, driven by geopolitical uncertainty and interest rate dynamics. However, he argues the AI buildout is far more significant than inflation or rate concerns and describes the current moment as being in the "second inning" of AI — actually earlier than his view a year ago when he said "third inning." He challenges investors to consider that the more dangerous mistake is assuming we're at a bubble peak rather than still in the early stages.
