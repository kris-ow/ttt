Denmark's Road Traffic Authority has provisionally approved Tesla's FSD (Supervised) system, making it the fourth European country to clear the software in approximately eight weeks, following the Netherlands, Lithuania, and Estonia.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **Denmark approves FSD despite prior EU-level objections:** Færdselsstyrelsen accepted the provisional type approval originally issued by the Dutch authority RDW on April 10, after conducting its own independent technical review. The approval is notable because Denmark had previously been among Nordic nations — alongside Sweden, Finland, and Norway — that raised concerns at the EU level about FSD's tendency to exceed speed limits, its performance on icy roads, and whether the "Full Self-Driving" name overstates the technology. The Danish authority emphasized that "the system does not make the car self-driving. The driver is still fully responsible for driving."

2. **EU-wide approval remains stalled, with significant downside risk:** The national-recognition route continues advancing, but EU-wide approval is not expected before October or December 2026, with full recognition potentially slipping into early 2027. A qualified majority requires at least 15 of 27 member states representing 65% of the EU population — Nordic opposition could complicate that threshold. Critically, if the EU Commission ultimately rejects the system, the Dutch provisional approval becomes invalid after six months, taking all four national approvals down with it. FSD Supervised pricing in Europe is €99/month, or €49/month for owners who previously purchased Enhanced Autopilot.

## Market & Competition

3. **Tesla gains competitive ground as German OEMs retreat from Level 3:** Both Mercedes-Benz and BMW have pulled back from Level 3 autonomous systems in Europe — Mercedes paused Drive Pilot from upcoming S-Class and EQS facelifts, and BMW discontinued its Personal Pilot L3 system. Both are pivoting to Level 2+ systems. This leaves Tesla's FSD Supervised as the most capable driver-assistance system widely available in Europe. Belgium and Greece are reportedly moving quickly toward national approvals, while Ireland, Germany, France, Italy, and Spain are still reviewing or testing.

## Editorial Stance
Author Fred Lambert argues Denmark's approval — from a prior skeptic — suggests the technical documentation was more convincing than initial political objections implied, but cautions that national mutual-recognition approvals are "low-hanging fruit" and the EU Commission vote remains the real test. He notes Tesla's Level 2 approach is "winning the regulatory race in Europe by default" while Level 3 systems from German automakers proved commercially unviable, attributing much of the difference to manufacturers' willingness to take responsibility for their systems.
