The video discusses SpaceX's recent acquisition of a 444-acre parcel of land near Brownsville/Starbase, which the speakers believe will become a new data center site (informally dubbed "Colossus 3" by the host, though he notes this isn't confirmed as an official name). They walk through aerial imagery of the site, its gas pipeline infrastructure, and model out potential revenue if multiple "Mini Hard" data center buildings are constructed there, framing the buildout as a major and underappreciated value driver for SpaceX.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **New Brownsville land tied to data center expansion:** At the end of a recent August, SpaceX purchased a 444-acre piece of land near Brownsville, Texas, which had just been removed from the city of Brownsville as that city was considering a moratorium on data centers. The host speculates this land could become part of Starbase and a new data center location, which he informally calls "Colossus 3," while stressing this name is his own guess, not confirmed official terminology. A guest (credited to RGV Aerial Photography for the site imagery) notes a natural gas pipeline — separate from the existing Starbase pipeline — is being laid across the site to supply power.
2. **Existing Colossus sites and "Mini Hard" buildings:** Colossus 1 and Colossus 2 are located in the Memphis, Tennessee and northern Mississippi area; the Colossus 2 location includes at least three data center buildings referred to as Macro Hard, Macro Harder, and Mini Hard. One Mini Hard in that area has recently opened and is operational; another is expected to open in October, and per a guest's relay of comments from Brian Wang and Elon Musk, additional ones could open by November and possibly December. The guest states each Mini Hard building takes about five months to construct.
3. **Compute capacity and revenue trajectory (attributed estimates):** The host states that Elon Musk said on an earnings call SpaceX would aim for 5–10 gigawatts of compute capacity, closer to 10, by 2027, and has since suggested the figure could exceed 10 gigawatts. The host estimates current capacity at 2 gigawatts generating roughly $100 billion a year, and says adding the Mini Hard buildings could add roughly $20 billion more, projecting SpaceX could potentially reach $300 billion in annualized revenue if it hits the halfway point (5 gigawatts) of its 10-gigawatt goal next year — this is presented as the host's own speculative extrapolation, not a company guidance figure.
4. **Revenue modeling for the new 444-acre site:** A guest estimates the new 444-acre Brownsville-area site could conservatively house six Mini Hard buildings. Using current Grace Blackwell-generation systems (estimated at roughly $22 billion in potential annual revenue per data center of that size), six buildings could generate up to roughly $132 billion/year; using next-generation Vera Rubin systems (cited to current SemiAnalysis estimates as roughly 40% more revenue per data center), the same six-building cluster could generate up to roughly $180 billion/year. The guest says Vera Rubin systems are expected to arrive in the first quarter of 2027, and speculates two or three Mini Hard buildings could be completed on that generation by the end of that year, with the number of buildings ultimately built on the site still uncertain (guest estimates at least two, possibly up to six or more).
5. **Comparison to Tesla and Big Tech revenue:** The guest notes that even the low end of the modeled revenue range for the six-building cluster ($130–180 billion) would exceed Tesla's entire annual company revenue (cited as roughly $100 billion). The host separately compares this to quarterly revenues of other large tech firms — citing Amazon at roughly $200 billion/quarter, Alphabet at roughly $120 billion/quarter, and Apple and Nvidia each at roughly $100 billion/quarter — suggesting SpaceX's data center revenue could approach that scale within the stated timeframe.
6. **Infrastructure advantage versus competitors:** The host argues SpaceX has an edge over competitors like Microsoft in data center buildout speed because SpaceX already owns land, has secured permits, and has power/energy supply in place at its Nashville-area and new Brownsville sites, whereas he says Microsoft has had data centers sitting without power for an extended period (described as "several months, maybe even a year"). This is presented as the host's characterization, not a sourced Microsoft disclosure.
