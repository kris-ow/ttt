The article, an opinion piece by Electrek's Fred Lambert, argues that Tesla's new pitch for businesses to buy and operate Cybercab fleets on its Robotaxi network is a repeat of a broken 2019 promise, and that Tesla's willingness to sell the vehicles rather than run them itself proves the fleet economics don't work as advertised.

## Robotaxi
1. **Tesla circulates Cybercab fleet-buyer interest form:** At its September 3 Cybercab event, Tesla began circulating an interest form asking businesses about "Cybercab fleet vehicle purchasing," inviting buyers to purchase Cybercabs and run them on Tesla's Robotaxi network with revenue split with Tesla.
2. **Echoes 2019 "Tesla Network" promise that never materialized:** At Autonomy Day in 2019, Musk said owners could add cars to a "Tesla Network" and earn up to $30,000/year in gross profit per vehicle, and called Teslas "appreciating assets." The article states this never happened: Tesla runs the robotaxi service itself, and owners who paid up to $15,000 for FSD on that promise cannot use their cars as robotaxis.
3. **MisterGreen bankruptcy cited as cautionary case:** Dutch leasing firm MisterGreen bought more than 4,000 Teslas betting on value appreciation and future robotaxi income; instead, Tesla cut new-car prices and used Teslas depreciated (per the article) roughly three times faster than the broader used-car market. MisterGreen went bankrupt in December 2025, wiping out bondholders and losing $40 million, according to the article.
4. **Author's structural argument against the fleet-sale model:** The article argues that because Tesla controls the Cybercab, the Robotaxi network, software, dispatch, pricing, and revenue split, it would keep any genuinely profitable robotaxi economics in-house rather than sell vehicles to third parties; instead, selling to fleet buyers shifts capital cost and depreciation risk to them while Tesla retains software margin and its fare cut, and can undercut, deprioritize, or geofence buyer fleets.

## Editorial Stance
The author (Fred Lambert) characterizes the Cybercab fleet-buyer offer as "asset-light offloading dressed up as an opportunity" and a repeat of the FSD sales playbook — selling a promise of future income, collecting money upfront, and leaving buyers to bear losses when returns don't materialize. He also references, without detail, another company that built a Tesla fleet in LA (2018-2020) anticipating "Tesla Network" income that never came and had to shut down.
