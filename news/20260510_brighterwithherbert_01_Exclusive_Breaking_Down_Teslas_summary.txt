This video features host Herbert alongside retail analysts Cern Basher and Brian Wang (Next Big Future) breaking down near-term Tesla robotaxi ramp scenarios for 2026. The central discussion revolves around Brian Wang's thesis that unsupervised Tesla robotaxi operations in Austin, Dallas, and Houston could grow roughly 10x within 60 days, and a broader debate about the sequencing of Model Y fleet expansion versus the eventual Cybercab rollout as the true robotaxi brand vehicle.

## Robotaxi

1. **Current Fleet Snapshot and Brian Wang's 10x Thesis:** Per RobotaxiTracker.com, the unsupervised robotaxi fleet stands at approximately 38–39 vehicles across Texas: roughly 27 in Austin, 5 in Dallas, and 6 in Houston — with Dallas and Houston operating 100% unsupervised from launch. Brian Wang argues this fleet could grow to over 300 unsupervised vehicles by end of June 2026, a roughly 10x increase in ~60 days. His logic: Dallas and Houston proved 100% unsupervised operations are viable at small scale, Austin proves it works at larger geographic scale, and there are reportedly 80+ parked robotaxi-badged vehicles in each city ready to be activated.

2. **Austin Conversion to Fully Unsupervised:** Brian Wang's near-term prediction is that Tesla will convert all remaining supervised Austin vehicles to unsupervised within approximately 10 days, bringing Austin's total unsupervised fleet from 27 to 50+. The panel largely agreed there is no clear logical reason to keep the remaining ~24 Austin vehicles supervised given that over half are already running unsupervised. The one caveat raised was a possible A/B test — running supervised and unsupervised cars in parallel to directly measure whether safety monitors are doing anything meaningful before removing them entirely.

3. **Dallas and Houston as the Proof of Concept for Unsupervised Launch:** A key insight discussed was that Tesla launched Dallas and Houston directly as unsupervised operations, skipping the supervised-driver phase that Austin went through. The panel theorized this was enabled by FSD version 14.x reaching a sufficient safety threshold, combined with months of pre-launch supervised (non-ride-hail) testing in those cities that went unannounced. This strategy — adapting to the local environment with supervised vehicles but never announcing a supervised ride-hail phase — was seen as deliberate to avoid bad PR and allow full data collection before a clean unsupervised launch.

4. **Geographic Coverage and Expansion Logic:** Austin's robotaxi geofenced area is 244 square miles, while Dallas covers 31 square miles and Houston covers 25 square miles. The panel noted Dallas and Houston may have started with slightly larger initial geofenced areas than Austin's early ~20 square miles. The small number of vehicles (5–6) relative to those geographic areas was characterized as still very much in pilot/test mode. Brian Wang described a competitive internal team dynamic — Austin, Dallas, and Houston teams effectively competing on metrics — that he expects will drive stepwise geographic expansion and eventual activation of new cities like Las Vegas, Phoenix, Miami, and San Antonio.

5. **Bay Area Supervised Fleet — The Open Question:** The Bay Area fleet, described as operating under a ride-hail limo-service permit with human drivers using FSD, covers roughly 1,000 square miles and reportedly involves approximately 1,000 vehicles and drivers. The panel noted Tesla has not applied for a California autonomous vehicle permit despite operating long enough to accumulate the required 50,000 miles of data. Theories included political caution, a preference to wait for federal autonomous vehicle legislation, or a desire to avoid bad headlines — but the panel described it as genuinely puzzling given FSD's apparent capability in the region.

6. **End-of-Year Fleet Size Estimate:** Herbert's personal estimate, offered as his own view rather than Tesla guidance, is that Tesla will reach 10,000–15,000 robotaxis by end of 2026 across approximately 15–20 cities in roughly 12 states. He frames this as approximately 500–1,000 vehicles per city across that number of markets, with the bulk eventually being Cybercabs rather than Model Y vehicles.

7. **Model Y Fleet Role and the 10% Passenger Capacity Argument:** The panel agreed Model Y robotaxis will remain a necessary component of the fleet even after Cybercab scales, because approximately 10% of rides require more than two passengers. This creates a natural equilibrium where a city fleet might be ~90% Cybercab and ~10% Model Y (and eventually Cybertruck or robo-vans for specialty needs). Herbert framed the current Model Y robotaxi deployments as the testing/learning phase, with Cybercab being the true robotaxi brand that will constitute the bulk of the scaled fleet.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD Version 14.x as the Safety Threshold:** Brian Wang cited FSD version 14.3/14.x as the version that gave Tesla sufficient confidence to launch Dallas and Houston unsupervised. The panel noted that even minor point-version differences in FSD have been shown in testing to produce measurable differences in safety outcomes, and that hardware generation (HW3 vs. HW4) also matters — HW3 was explicitly cited as incapable of unsupervised operation. Cern Basher noted that as a daily FSD user, he cannot recall the last time he intervened for safety reasons, though navigation preferences and convenience-related interventions (e.g., parking garage entrances) remain common.

2. **Elon's Earnings Call Pullback and Version 15 Uncertainty:** The panel referenced Elon Musk's comments on the most recent earnings call, where he downplayed near-term robotaxi materiality and suggested FSD version 15 may be needed before truly scaled unsupervised deployment. He also indicated a goal of unsupervised FSD for consumer-owned vehicles by Q4 2026 or Q1 2027. The panel acknowledged this creates uncertainty about how deep the remaining "non-safety" issue list is and how long it will take to resolve items like drop-off location precision and in-service edge cases.

3. **Transfer of FSD to New Geographies:** Brian Wang noted Tesla demonstrated the ability to transfer FSD capability from the US to China using video training alone, achieving high baseline performance in new geographies without requiring extensive local miles first. He framed the general capability level as improving such that deploying to new cities becomes increasingly straightforward, with the remaining work being closing the gap from high-nine safety performance to the even higher threshold Tesla requires for fully unsupervised commercial service.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Cybercab Production Ramp and Validation Timeline:** The panel discussed a video of a Tesla Semi loaded with Cybercabs being delivered, with Joe Tegtmeyer reporting approximately 40–50 Cybercabs sitting on a lot with roughly 20 more arriving daily. Herbert estimated production at approximately 20 Cybercabs per day currently, projecting roughly 500–600 per month and approximately 2,000 within three months. The panel consensus was that Cybercabs are currently being distributed nationally for road validation — not yet for commercial robotaxi service — and that Tesla will need approximately 1,000 Cybercabs driving in a given area for several months before feeling confident enough to launch unsupervised commercial service under the Cybercab brand.

2. **Cybercab Hardware Differentiation:** Brian Wang argued Cybercab likely contains not just cost-optimized hardware but also superior camera placement and sensor hardware compared to robotaxi-badged Model Y vehicles, drawing on lessons learned from the Cybertruck camera integration. He believes this hardware advantage, combined with the brand importance of Cybercab as Tesla's robotaxi identity, makes it critical that the Cybercab launch be flawless — any high-profile Cybercab incident would damage the brand in a way that a Model Y incident would not.

3. **Cybercab Supervisor Impracticality:** The panel noted a structural reason Cybercab cannot run in a supervised mode with a safety driver: the vehicle only seats two people, making it socially and practically awkward to have a safety monitor sitting beside a passenger. This means Cybercab must launch commercially as fully unsupervised from day one, raising the validation bar further and explaining the extended pre-launch testing period.
