The article explains and contextualizes Tesla's announcement at its Cybercab event of a new rare-earth-free permanent magnet motor, arguing the achievement is real but more modest in practical impact than the framing suggests.

## Robotaxi
1. **New Cybercab motor claims:** At Tesla's Cybercab event, the company said the Cybercab's new motor is 18% smaller, 25% lighter, and more efficient than other top-performing drive units; Elon Musk (who was not at the event) added on Twitter that the motor does not use rare earth metals. The article notes Tesla had no communications department present to confirm whether this is the fruit of a rare-earth-free motor effort it announced in 2023.
2. **Cybercab efficiency and fleet size:** The Cybercab is certified to consume 165Wh/mi, which the article says makes it the most efficient electric car on the road, attributed to its small size, aerodynamic design, light weight, two-seat layout, and likely its motor. The article notes Tesla currently has roughly 45 Cybercabs registered for use in Texas.

## Market & Competition
1. **Rare earths explained:** Rare earth elements (lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium) are not especially rare — neodymium is about two-thirds as abundant as copper — but are concentrated in supply because most global processing occurs in China. Neodymium, along with additives like dysprosium, terbium, and praseodymium, is used in the permanent magnets found in DC permanent-magnet motors; only about 1kg of these elements is used per vehicle motor. Tesla's AC induction motors (used before the Model 3) do not require rare earths, while its permanent magnet motors (introduced with Model 3 and later used across its lineup) do.
2. **China's export controls raised urgency:** The article states that over the past year China imposed export controls on rare earth processing, which it frames as a response to U.S. actions; this increased urgency for automakers to find non-Chinese supply or reduce rare earth usage, a dynamic the article says motivated Tesla's motor redesign.

## Editorial Stance
The author argues the rare-earth-free motor is a genuine engineering achievement but downplays its significance: it has nothing to do with batteries, offers only a marginal efficiency gain since EV motors are already 90%+ efficient, and isn't a groundbreaking new technology — rather it's an implementation of known approaches (e.g., iron ferrite or aluminum-nickel-cobalt magnets) responding to supply-chain risk that recently became more acute. The author is also critical of Elon Musk, sarcastically blaming him for contributing to the political tensions that led to China's export controls.
