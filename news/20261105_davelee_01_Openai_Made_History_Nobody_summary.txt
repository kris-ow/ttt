This video recounts OpenAI's claim to have solved the Navier-Stokes "blowup" problem — a 90-year-old open question in fluid dynamics with a million-dollar prize — using 10,000 AI agents, and the subsequent controversy over whether anyone, including OpenAI itself, actually understands the resulting proof. ## Market & Competition
1. **OpenAI claims a math breakthrough that nobody fully understands:** On September 1st, OpenAI ran 10,000 copies of an unreleased AI model as agents against the Navier-Stokes blowup problem; after 88 hours it produced a 160-page proof claiming the rules governing fluid motion can "blow up" (reach infinite speed) under a specific smooth stirring condition. A second AI translated the proof into the Lean formal-verification language, which confirmed the logical steps were internally consistent, but mathematicians — including Terence Tao, described as one of the world's top mathematicians — say they have "learned almost nothing mathematically" from it and that nobody, including OpenAI, fully understands the reasoning.
2. **Verification gap between English proof and formal code:** In October, three mathematicians found at least one place where the English-language proof and the Lean code used to verify it didn't match, yet the automated check still passed; one of them believes there could be on the order of a hundred similar mismatches. Separately, Scientific American reported that OpenAI's claim its AI used the same method as a Madrid-based team working on the same problem was disputed by experts, who said the AI's approach was actually different — meaning OpenAI had mischaracterized its own model's work.
3. **Rival Anthropic team was racing the same problem:** Mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpage (the latter working at Anthropic) spent roughly a year using AI assistance to get close to solving the same Navier-Stokes problem via an easier intermediate version. Buckmaster alleges OpenAI's Sebastian Bubeck offered to put Buckmaster's name on OpenAI's proof while excluding Alpage because of his rival-company affiliation, and that he was warned about "ruining his career" when he objected; Bubeck disputes the characterization but apologized for the career remark. Buckmaster rushed his own work online just before OpenAI announced its solution.
4. **Mass "proof dump" raises credibility concerns:** On October 6th, OpenAI posted 722 math papers addressing roughly 4,000 previously unsolved problems, having spent about three hours of compute per problem; some claimed results would reportedly take a human mathematician a full career to produce. At least three papers were retracted within a day, and mathematicians have nicknamed the episode the "mythocalypse." Mathematician Tim Gowers, another top figure in the field, expressed concern that young researchers may stop investing years learning underlying methods if AI outputs answers that are faster to generate than to understand.
5. **Narrower scope than headlines suggest:** The AI's result applies only to the "stirred" version of the problem (using an idealized smooth external forcing) at a scale a thousand times thinner than a human hair, where continuum fluid equations aren't even expected to apply; whether the unstirred, "natural" version of Navier-Stokes can blow up — the version most mathematicians care about — remains unsolved. The practical takeaway highlighted is speed: what took human mathematicians about a year, AI did in under four days, shifting the bottleneck to human verification and trust rather than discovery itself.
