This video from FutureAzA examines Ethiopia's emergence as an unlikely leader in EV adoption, driven primarily by economic pressures rather than environmental idealism. The host argues that Ethiopia's ban on importing internal combustion engine vehicles (enacted in 2024), combined with dramatically cheap hydroelectric power, has rapidly transformed the country's vehicle fleet. The video also serves as a fundraising appeal for the host to travel to Ethiopia in late 2025 to report on the story firsthand.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Ethiopia's ICE import ban:** In 2024, Ethiopia became the first country in the world to ban imports of petrol and diesel vehicles entirely. The policy was driven primarily by fiscal pressure — Ethiopia was spending roughly $4.5 billion per year on imported petrol, money that left the country and did not return to the local economy. By contrast, domestically generated electricity keeps spending within the local economy.

2. **Rapid EV adoption from a near-zero base:** EV adoption in Ethiopia has grown from less than 1% to nearly 5–6% of all vehicles (not just new car sales), placing it above the global average of approximately 4% of all vehicles. Within Africa, Ethiopia is the clear outlier — its roughly 15,000 EV passenger cars represent more than four times the EV stock in South Africa. The government's target is 500,000 EVs on the road by 2030, up from approximately 115,000 currently (though the host notes the article citing that figure is about nine months old).

3. **Policy levers — carrots and sticks:** Beyond the import ban, Ethiopia has zeroed out VAT and import taxes on EVs, introduced 100 new electric buses in the capital Addis Ababa (population 4.2 million), and announced plans to build 17 EV assembly plants using imported parts, primarily from China. Middle-class consumers are finding it easier to obtain credit for new EVs than for secondhand petrol cars, lowering the financial barrier despite relatively high sticker prices for a low-income country (a BYD Seagull hatchback is cited at approximately $23,000).

4. **Ultra-low electricity costs as a structural advantage:** The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam — which took 14 years to build and came online roughly a year and a half before the video's publication — is the foundation of the country's EV economics. A chart cited in the video places Ethiopia's electricity rate at approximately 0.6 cents per kilowatt hour, which the host calculates would cost under $1 to fully charge a large EV. Even at a higher government-cited figure of 10 cents per kilowatt hour — roughly half the rate of neighboring countries — a full charge would cost approximately $8, still dramatically cheaper than petrol given chronic fuel shortages and long queues.

5. **Domestic manufacturing ambitions:** Ethiopia launched what it describes as its largest EV factory, a $52 million facility targeting production of 1,000 cars per year. The host acknowledges that figure is modest but expects capacity to grow. Plans for 17 assembly plants using Chinese knockdown or semi-knockdown kits are underway, partly because Ethiopian labor costs are among the lowest in the world — a key incentive for Chinese manufacturers like BYD to agree to final assembly in-country. An earlier assembly partnership with Hyundai in 2020 collapsed due to foreign currency shortages.

## Market & Competition

1. **BYD's dominance in emerging EV markets:** BYD is described as the most prevalent EV brand on Ethiopian roads and is noted as having overtaken Tesla as the world's largest EV maker. The host emphasizes that BYD is not hesitant about entering emerging markets, which Western brands have historically avoided.

2. **U.S. policy contrast:** The host briefly notes that under Republican leadership, the U.S. has cut EV subsidies, forcing automakers to scale back production and absorb significant financial losses as demand-side incentives were removed — a contrast to Ethiopia's aggressive pro-EV stance.
