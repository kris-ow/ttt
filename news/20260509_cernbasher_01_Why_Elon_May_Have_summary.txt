This episode of the Cern Basher channel features host Cern alongside regular guest Phil and new guest Kenyon Wells — a longtime Tesla investor and enterprise IT professional with decades of experience — discussing the potential merger of Tesla and SpaceX, the looming compute supply crisis driven by AI demand, Tesla's Etherloop/Ethernet wiring architecture in the Cybercab, and the political and societal risks of AI deployment ahead of the 2028 U.S. election. The central thesis is that the demand for AI inference compute is growing so fast (approximately 3.4x per year) that terrestrial chip production cannot keep up, pointing toward orbital data centers and eventually lunar manufacturing as the only viable long-term solution — and that a Tesla-SpaceX merger is the logical outcome of Elon Musk's vertically integrated vision. The conversation also explores how Tesla's robotaxi rollout, Optimus, and the broader "Musconomy" ecosystem represent the clearest near-term path to demonstrating tangible AI benefits to ordinary consumers and voters.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX–Anthropic Partnership and the Compute Supply Crisis:** Phil opens by discussing the newly announced deal between SpaceX (operating as "SpaceX AI" following the dissolution of xAI as a separate brand) and Anthropic, in which Anthropic — whose primary cloud host is Amazon AWS, with additional presence on Azure and Google Cloud — will gain access to Colossus 1, while Colossus 2 remains dedicated to xAI's work. Colossus 1 is described as housing 220,000 Nvidia GPUs including H100s, H200s, and GB200s. Phil frames this as a symptom of a broader supply wall: AI inference demand is growing at approximately 3.4x per year, far outpacing semiconductor production capacity, and Anthropic had little practical alternative but to partner with SpaceX for orbital compute capacity.

2. **The Compute Demand Curve and Terafab:** Phil presents a detailed analysis showing that global AI compute capacity today stands at approximately 30 gigawatts (he notes Elon's own chart uses ~20 GW, but calls the difference immaterial). Growing at the historical CPU-era rate of 1.58x per year, it would take 7.7 years to reach 1 terawatt and 17 years (approximately 2043) to reach 100 terawatts. But at the current AI growth rate of 3.4x per year, 1 terawatt is reachable in just 3 years, and 100 terawatts in 7 years. Adjusting for hardware efficiency gains, Phil revises these estimates to 4.3 years for 1 terawatt and 10 years for 100 terawatts. He argues that Terafab — described as producing 1 terawatt of compute capacity annually and requiring at minimum a 100-million-square-foot facility (five Pentagons or two Central Parks) — is necessary but insufficient on its own; one Terafab eventually cannot keep pace with accelerating demand.

3. **Orbital and Lunar Computing as the Long-Term Answer:** Phil argues that within 5–10 years, terrestrial chip production will dominate, but beyond that, the industrialization of chip fabrication on the Moon becomes inevitable. He estimates that 90% or more of all AI inference will ultimately be delivered to Earth via space, while terrestrial data centers will be mostly dedicated to AI training (which requires coherent supercomputer-scale clusters). He describes a starting concept of 100-kilowatt satellites each hosting roughly 250 GPU-equivalent chips (at approximately 400 watts per chip), scalable to a million satellites. Elon has stated approximately 80% of Terafab chips will go to space; Phil believes the true long-term figure will be even higher.

4. **Tesla–SpaceX Merger Debate:** The hosts debate the merits of combining Tesla and SpaceX. Phil's core argument is not primarily economic but operational: managing two separate companies with deeply intertwined intellectual property (Tesla's AI chip design team building chips for both Tesla and SpaceX; Terafab; Optimus for off-planet manufacturing; Starlink benefiting Tesla's connectivity) is "dilutive" to Elon's focus and energy. Cern notes a critical economic nuance for Tesla shareholders: if Tesla doubles in value but SpaceX triples before a merger, Tesla shareholders have not improved their relative ownership stake in a combined entity — making the pre-merger stock price largely beside the point. Both hosts agree that a SpaceX IPO (described as being in a quiet period) is imminent and that pre-IPO hype is currently drawing narrative attention away from Tesla. Phil speculates that if a merger were perceived as likely, Tesla's stock price would begin to track a "pretend merger value," allowing Tesla shareholders to benefit from SpaceX's momentum.

5. **Legacy Auto's Inability to Catch Up:** Phil, drawing on his experience at Rivian (including attempted deals with General Motors — rejected by Rivian — and an executed partnership with Ford involving a skunk works team to reimagine vehicle architecture), argues there is no viable path for legacy automakers to catch up with Tesla's software-defined vehicle architecture. The core problem is that legacy OEMs are assemblers of supplier-built components, making it structurally impossible to rearchitect the vehicle from scratch without rejecting their entire supply chain model. He describes a BMW engineer spending a career extracting 1% more efficiency from a combustion engine per product cycle as emblematic of the cultural barrier.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD and Robotaxi Safety Posture:** Kenyon raises the question of whether Tesla is being too cautious in its robotaxi rollout given the 2028 election timeline. He concludes that the incidents observed so far have been minor, and he does not believe safety is the primary remaining obstacle. He suggests Tesla is working through "the long tail of operational related things" rather than fundamental safety barriers. He adds that he will not be seriously concerned unless, by the end of 2026, Tesla is still not fully rolling out robotaxi at scale.

2. **Etherloop Architecture in the Cybercab:** Phil provides a detailed technical breakdown of Tesla's Etherloop technology — a zonal Ethernet-based architecture that replaces traditional wiring harnesses, eliminating approximately 80–100 pounds of wiring per vehicle. In the Cybercab's "unbox" manufacturing process, major vehicle assemblies snap together, with wiring and power connections reduced to essentially two small connectors at each junction. The Cybercab uses a zonal architecture with compute surfaces embedded in each major assembly, connected by Ethernet and power rather than complex harnesses. Phil notes that a key Tesla innovation within Etherloop is time-division multiplexing, where every controller has a fixed address and a synchronized time slot on the wire, enhancing system reliability and safety. He traces the lineage: Cybertruck was the technology demonstration platform (including the move to 48V), and the Cybercab is the elegant realization of everything Cybertruck proved. Kenyon notes that other automakers — with the exception of Chinese manufacturers — have not meaningfully adopted these advances despite Tesla demonstrating them at scale.

## Robotaxi

1. **Robotaxi Deployment Strategy and Geography:** Kenyon suggests Tesla will likely roll out robotaxi more geographically broadly than the current city-by-city handful of vehicles implies. He specifically highlights the triangle of the three major Texas cities (Austin, Dallas, Houston) as a near-ideal initial expansion zone, covering both urban and suburban environments and representing a large share of the second-largest U.S. state economy. He also raises the broader potential for robotaxi infrastructure in home delivery and last-mile logistics, particularly when combined with Optimus for the final 50 feet of delivery.

2. **Fleet Management as the Software Monetization Layer:** Cern and Phil discuss how the operational software stack built for robotaxi — managing fleets and subfleets of robotic entities — is directly transferable to Tesla Semi logistics and Optimus warehouse deployment. Phil describes a future in which a Tesla Semi communicates its manifest to a cloud orchestration layer, and Optimus units at a loading dock know exactly what to unload and where to place it before the truck even arrives. Kenyon adds that this orchestration layer — which he compares favorably to Grok or a similar solution — represents a high-value, recurring subscription service that Tesla will first develop for internal use, then sell as a platform. Cern references "Tesla One" software as a potential unified platform tying all of these systems together.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus as the Enabler of Off-Planet Industrialization:** Phil argues that Optimus is the critical piece that makes lunar and Martian industrialization viable, providing generalized robotic labor in environments hostile to humans. He reiterates a point from a prior episode that Optimus units will outnumber humans on Mars or the Moon by orders of magnitude. He draws a direct line from Tesla's decade of manufacturing innovation — culminating in the Cybercab's unbox process — to the manufacturability and scalability of Optimus.

2. **Etherloop Principles Applied to Optimus:** Phil and Kenyon discuss how the internal wiring and network architecture developed for Cybercab will be applied to Optimus, with Ethernet connecting controlling extremities (e.g., a forearm controller articulating hand movements) using the same time-division synchronization approach. Phil clarifies that inter-bot communication will not use peer-to-peer Etherloop but rather a cloud-based fleet orchestration system, likely using in-building Wi-Fi and 5G/Starlink mobile for connectivity.

3. **Optimus Supply Chain Is Entirely New:** Phil notes a fundamental difference between Optimus and the automotive supply chain: virtually every Optimus component is new, meaning suppliers cannot simply pull a part from existing inventory. This makes scaling harder initially but also means Tesla carries no legacy architecture debt — a structural advantage over any competitor attempting to build humanoid robots using existing industrial supply chains.

## Market & Competition (AI Policy)

1. **The 2028 Election and the Risk of AI Backlash:** Kenyon's primary contribution centers on his concern that AI and data center construction are becoming a partisan political issue ahead of the 2028 U.S. election. He warns that fear-mongering narratives — including proposed bans on data center construction, restrictions on AI, and increased AI liability — could gain enough votes to become policy. He draws an analogy to "Dutch disease," where the AI industry is crowding out investment capital, talent, and attention from the broader economy in a way that, while not yet manifesting in labor displacement, is already distorting where money flows. He argues the industry must shift its messaging from cost-cutting and job displacement to demonstrable consumer benefits — reduced grocery costs, cheaper energy, lower cell bills via Starlink — to make AI politically safe before the election.

2. **Jensen Huang and the "Leadership Lacks Imagination" Thesis:** Cern recounts an interview in which CNBC's Jim Cramer asked Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang why companies were using AI to cut workers rather than to grow revenue. Huang's answer: "Leadership lacks imagination." Kenyon elaborates, arguing that companies announcing 10% workforce cuts due to AI represent either a business failure or a leadership failure — the correct approach is to use AI to grow the top line by more than 10% rather than shrink the bottom line by 10%.
