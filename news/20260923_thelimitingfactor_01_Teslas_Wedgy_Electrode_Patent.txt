This will help reduce
cell defects during
battery production and use, and
open up opportunities
to increase
energy density
through thicker
electrodes and higher
silicon content.  Before

patent title is “
Electrode foil coatings
and methods of
applying them,” and the application
was filed on September 2,
2025.  The inventors
are listed as Yang Shao-yun,
Siva Muthukumar, Song
Shao-ping, and Li Zhu

their invention as a
coating or sleeve
for
battery electrodes that can
reduce

internal short
circuits.  However,
after reading all 52
pages of the patent
application, it seems that
this is not one invention, but
at least three.  Study Park

one invention in a
patent application
that was separated into a
separate patent
application related to
electrode narrowing.
With that in mind,
let's take a look at
each invention and what
they do.  When
the electrodes of a
battery
cell are coated
at the factory, this
usually
happens on a
wide electrode
foil in so-called
strips.  As you can see,
for the
Tesla electrode coating process, it
looks like eight
stripes.  Once the
electrode foil
is coated with strips,
each strip
is cut lengthwise
to create a ribbon of
electrode
material, which is then
cut to
length, bonded
to a separator,
rolled up, and
inserted into a
battery cell
.  The separator keeps
each layer electrically
isolated, and in a
typical
battery
cell,
a single tab is welded to the electrode foil
,
protruding safely beyond the
separator.  However,
Tesla's 4680 model
uses a so-
called tabless
design, where there is no
tab welding step.  Instead,
a series of
laser-cut "flags" are used.
When these tabs are
bent and
welded to the top and
bottom of the battery
roll, it increases
the risk of the
cathode contacting the anode, which
will cause
the cell to short-circuit and
potentially cause it to catch
fire.  To
prevent this,
the patent application
suggests covering
some of the exposed
metal between the
electrode strips with plastic
, using a
liquid coating or
tape to protect
against short
circuits.  And in my
teardown video of the
4680 element, in collaboration with
the University of California, San
Diego, we saw
exactly that.  The black is the
active
electrode material, and the white
stripe appears to be a
plastic
material
applied to the
metal foil to
provide a
safety margin against short
circuits.  In other
words, one of
the inventions described in
the patent is probably
already used
in the 4680 element. The
next invention
concerns the
gradual
reduction of the thickness of the
active
electrode material.  When
the electrode is cut
to the desired length, an
edge is formed with
sharp right
angles and an instantaneous,
rather than gradual, change in
thickness.  Why is this
important?  In a roll
battery, the anode
strip is slightly longer and
wider than the cathode strip.  This is
because
it
accepts lithium ions from the
cathode during charging.  So having a
small excess of
anode material
serves as a reserve to
avoid the deposition of
pure
lithium metal on the anode, which
could form a
bridge between the cathode and
anode and cause
the battery to short-circuit.  When
cathode and anode
tapes of different
lengths are rolled into a
roll, there is a
non-uniformity where the
cathode
ends abruptly and
sharply, which I will call the "
tip."  Because
this tip is sharp,
it can cause other
layers to bend
inward, creating a
loss of electrical
contact between the layers,
which reduces
energy density.  And because
the tip has sharp
edges, during
battery charging and discharging, as the
layers expand and
contract, it can
snag and
cut through the layers,
causing a short
circuit.  Interestingly,
as Tesla moves to
higher
energy density cell designs in
the future, these
failure mechanisms will
likely only
get worse.
This is because future
increases in energy density will likely
depend on either
increasing the
silicon content or
thickening the
cathode.  A thicker
cathode will mean a
larger cavity behind
the tip, and
increasing
silicon content will mean
higher stresses in
expansion and
compression,
leading to
more
warping,
engagement, and
protrusion.  In
any case, whether
we're talking about the
current or
future state of
lithium-ion batteries,
Tesla's patent suggests
that one way
to mitigate these
mechanical stresses is to
narrow the coating on the
cathode.  This results
in what we see
in Figures 18 A–18 C,
namely a smooth
transition at the
cathode tip, instead of the
sharp one seen
in Figures 17 A–17 C.
However, there is more to this story
than
just the narrowing of the
electrode material,
and this brings us to the
electrode sleeves.  Although
the narrowing of the
electrode coating significantly
reduces mechanical
stress, it
still leaves a sharp
edge on the
electrode foil under
the coating.  This means
that even if the cathode is
significantly narrowed, it
only removes part of
the voltage inside the
coil.  Tesla's solution
is to
use a
plastic sleeve
that covers only
the foil, or to forgo the
tapering of the
electrodes in
favor of a sleeve that
simultaneously creates a
tapered edge for the
electrodes and foil.
However, in my opinion, the
electrode sleeve
is too much for
reducing mechanical
stresses, and a
tapered electrode would
probably be
sufficient.  There are three reasons for this
.  First,
applying a sleeve
takes more time
than changing the thickness of the
active
electrode material.  Secondly, the
best detail is
its absence.    An
electrode sleeve
requires additional
parts, process, and
equipment, while
electrode tapering does
not.  Third,
as far as I
know, conventional
battery
cells currently do not
use
any
tapering processes or
the use of sleeves,
but still
achieve high
energy density and
long
life.  So, my
guess is
that the narrowing of the
electrodes and sleeve
is a preparation for
future
cylindrical
batteries with higher
energy density
or longer
service life.  The
tapering process itself,
while not eliminating
internal stresses
completely, should
provide
sufficient
safety margin for
stresses
induced by
thicker
electrodes and higher
silicon content.  Why do
I say that these inventions are
specifically designed for
cylindrical
elements?  Because
cylindrical elements
have a void in
the center that must
be
strong and stable enough
to hold its shape,
or require support,
which adds cost and
weight.  Obviously, the
unsupported option is
better, but it
makes the core
more vulnerable to
warping,
unwinding, or
breaking.
Prismatic and
package elements, on
the other hand, are flat
wound, folded or
pressed, which I
understand means
there are no similar
problems in the center of
the roll, as
there is no need
to maintain a
perfectly round
void.
Finally, for
each of
the inventions mentioned today,
