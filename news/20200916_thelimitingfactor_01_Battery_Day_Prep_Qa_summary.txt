This is a pre-Battery Day (2020) preparation stream from "thelimitingfactor," co-hosted with a battery-focused correspondent (Ryan) who obtained a ticket to attend Tesla's Battery Day in person. The hosts review speculative battery technology theories (dry battery electrode speed gains, metal-air hybrid cells, cell size, voltage/charging trade-offs) and compile a list of questions for Elon Musk, framing the event's biggest open question as when Tesla can scale to terawatt-hour battery production and what that implies for valuation.

## Energy
1. **Metal-air hybrid battery patents:** Tesla has filed patents related to lithium metal-air battery technology since roughly 2010, with the most recent patent filed in 2017 and granted at the start of the current month (per the correspondent). The theoretical specific energy of a pure lithium-metal-air reaction is stated at 13,000 Wh/kg, dropping to 3,000–1,000 Wh/kg for rechargeable versions; the correspondent says labs have achieved 800 Wh/kg. He describes Tesla's patent as covering a hybrid pack (half metal-air, half conventional lithium-ion) that could theoretically push pack-level specific energy above 400 Wh/kg, but calls full metal-air commercialization "a long shot" that is "unlikely but possible," noting a chat commenter's view that metal-air battery commercialization is decades away (cited as ~2050). The patent is attributed to a former Tesla engineer (now at QuantumScape) who the correspondent says also worked on the second-generation Model S/X battery pack per his LinkedIn.
2. **Dry battery electrode (DBE) production speed:** The host argues a 2x–4x line-speed increase from Maxwell's dry electrode process is plausible, citing Maxwell/A123 documentation from 2012 showing a pilot line running at roughly 25 meters/minute versus a typical ~5 meters/minute pilot line, and noting full-scale wet-slurry lines run around 80 meters/minute. He also cites Maxwell's claimed 10–20% cost decrease from DBE, which he says lines up with a third-party (George Bowers) estimate of similar cost reduction from a 4x speed increase. He raises electrostatic and flame-spray deposition as even faster (but unlikely for Battery Day) coating alternatives beyond the DBE extrusion process.
3. **Cathode/anode thickness trade-offs:** The hosts discuss increasing electrode thickness to raise energy density (more active material relative to inactive foil), but note this raises internal resistance, heat generation, and manufacturing difficulty (creasing when rolling). They frame this as one leg of a "triangle" trade-off with charge/discharge rate and cell size, arguing Tesla would need tabless electrode design plus improved cooling to solve all three simultaneously — which the host calls a "trifecta" if achieved.
4. **Terra vs. Roadrunner lines:** The correspondent speculates Tesla may be running two distinct battery lines — an R&D line called "Tara" (already operational, oriented toward mass-market cost/scalability) and "Roadrunner" (a newer, higher-performance line still under construction as of late August per his research, intended for vehicles like Cybertruck, Roadster, and Plaid variants). Both hosts frame this as speculative.
5. **12V battery replacement idea:** Prompted by a chat comment, the correspondent floats replacing Tesla's 12V lead-acid buffer battery with a supercapacitor tied directly to the main pack, which the host calls "a brilliant idea" that could eliminate 12V battery failures — though both stress this is speculative brainstorming, not a reported plan.
6. **LFP for energy storage and low-cost EVs:** Both hosts favor lithium iron phosphate (LFP) for Tesla's lower-range vehicle lineup and energy storage products, citing cost savings (host cites roughly $20/kWh savings, though he flags uncertainty on the exact unit) and citing an unnamed battery researcher's (Shirley Meng, per the correspondent) view that LFP is currently the best option for energy storage due to long cycle life (~10,000+ cycles cited).
7. **Battery Day question list (host's speculative agenda):** The hosts compiled candidate questions for Elon Musk covering: the next cost target after $100/kWh at the pack level; combining LFP with Maxwell DBE tech potentially enabling a sub-$25,000 (or ~$20,000) EV and ~$60–70/kWh energy storage; synthetic vs. natural graphite trends; single-crystal cathode cost impacts; and — described as the single most important question — when Tesla expects to reach terawatt-hour-scale annual production, which the host ties directly to valuation (estimating each terawatt-hour of capacity as roughly $1 trillion in market cap if Tesla were purely a battery manufacturer, and personally projecting ~2 TWh of annual production by 2030).
8. **Larger cell format speculation:** Referencing a leaked cell image (attributed to a Twitter user, "Fred"), the hosts speculate the cell may be roughly 54mm wide based on a "54" marking, estimating it could be roughly five to six times the volume of Tesla's current 2170 cell — which they say would cut the number of cells needed per pack by a proportional amount and could aid both manufacturing speed and energy density via reduced packaging material. This is explicitly speculative interpretation of an unverified leaked image.

## Financials
1. **Valuation tied to battery scale-up:** The host's rough estimate is that each terawatt-hour of annual battery production capacity corresponds to roughly $1 trillion in market cap (in a hypothetical pure battery-manufacturer scenario), underpinning his view that Tesla's terawatt-hour production timeline is the most important Battery Day data point for investors.

## Market & Competition
1. **Panasonic energy density roadmap:** A viewer question cites Panasonic's stated roadmap to increase energy density by 20% over five years; the host believes the broader battery industry (including Panasonic) is moving toward higher silicon anode content, single-crystal cathodes, and higher voltages regardless of Tesla's Maxwell-specific dry electrode tech.
2. **Possible Redwood Materials recycling partnership:** In response to a viewer question, the host says it "seems suspicious and extremely convenient" that Redwood Materials' facility sits near Giga Nevada, given existing logistics routes between Fremont and Nevada, but stresses this is speculation with no confirmation of an exclusive deal; the correspondent adds his own view (explicitly speculative) that Redwood may be positioned to sell recycled materials to multiple automakers rather than exclusively to Tesla, and that Tesla's in-house recycling efforts may have been scaled back in 2019 for cost reasons, prompting Redwood's founder to pursue recycling independently.
