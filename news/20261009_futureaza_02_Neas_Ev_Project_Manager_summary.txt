This video is an interview conducted by the channel's host with Sagar Gevali, an electromobility project manager at the Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA), covering the buildout of Nepal's EV charging network, hydropower/solar supply constraints, and policy gaps — it contains no substantive discussion of Tesla, SpaceX, or any related thesis.

## Market & Competition
1. **Nepal's EV charging buildout:** NEA has built out charging infrastructure across Nepal, placing stations roughly every 60–100 km on major highways; NEA itself owns 62 charging stations with 8.8 MW of capacity, while the country overall has about 2,000 charging stations (from small to large) and over 50 EV charging hubs with more than 10 connection points, developed via public-private partnerships.
2. **Fuel displacement and vehicle mix:** Gevali estimated electrification is displacing around 500,000 liters of oil per day nationally (a minimum estimate, by his own characterization), driven largely by electric minibuses (7–11–20 seats) suited to Nepal's mountainous terrain; he noted electric buses, trucks, and other commercial vehicles are still in early adoption ("the film is still ahead") while electric bikes and cars have already grown significantly.
3. **Charger speed evolution and stranded early investment:** Charging technology moved quickly from 30 kW ("fast" in 2019) to 50, 80, 100, 120 and now up to 180 kW ultra-fast chargers enabling 15–20 minute charging; Gevali flagged that operators who invested early in now-obsolete lower-power chargers may not have recouped their investment, and urged the government to require forward-looking technology assessments before future investment approvals.
4. **NEA's own charging economics:** NEA's 120 kW stations made it the country's largest and preferred charging operator; NEA now sells about 35,000 units of electricity per day, serving roughly 2,000–2,500 EVs daily, versus total public charging sales nationwide exceeding 500,000 units per day — meaning NEA represents a small share of the national market. NEA prices charging about 20–25% below other providers to keep costs down, without creating competitive problems given its small relative volume.
5. **Policy incentives and their evolution:** NEA eliminated capacity fees for charging stations (charging only for energy used) to lower risk for private operators, and previously provided free 200 kVA transformer capacity to new stations; as global charger prices fell and station economics shifted, NEA reduced this subsidy to 100 kVA, while now recommending operators install at least 300+ kVA with six to seven connectors per site to avoid long customer wait times.
6. **Grid capacity and hydropower seasonality:** Nepal currently imports electricity from India during the dry/winter season and exports during the wet season; Gevali expects domestic hydropower generation to be sufficient to cover winter demand within two to three years as new hydroelectric plants come online. He noted total installed charging capacity nationally is about 350 MW, with only roughly 25–60 MW used at peak, meaning EV charging currently has limited grid impact — though this is expected to change as buses and trucks on long intercity routes convert to electric.
7. **Policy and manufacturing gaps:** Gevali argued Nepal lacks clear government policy and vehicle/technology standards, warning that without defined standards the country risks importing EVs of inconsistent quality from China or elsewhere; he called for Nepal to move beyond being a pure importer toward developing domestic manufacturing, design, and technical expertise (training engineers/technicians) to manage battery lifecycle, maintenance, and import-dependency costs.
8. **Solar energy expansion:** Following a 2021 energy crisis when Nepal had to import electricity from India at roughly four times average price, NEA shifted to incorporate solar into its energy balance; Gevali said Nepal's solar panels can generate roughly twice the electricity of equivalent panels in Europe, and that NEA has signed deals for 800 MW of solar alongside growing private-sector and rooftop solar adoption.
