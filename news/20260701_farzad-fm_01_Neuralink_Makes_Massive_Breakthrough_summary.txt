1. **First Transdural Surgery Completed:** In May 2026, Neuralink performed the first-ever transdural implant surgery as part of its ongoing clinical trials. The procedure was conducted alongside Dr. Lozano at UHN in Toronto, Canada, and represents the most advanced version of the surgery Neuralink has performed to date. The key innovation is that the dura — the tough, leather-like outermost protective membrane of the brain situated just beneath the skull — is left fully intact rather than being opened and partially removed as in all prior Neuralink surgeries.

2. **Why Preserving the Dura Matters:** In the standard Neuralink procedure, the dura is opened (a step called a durotomy) so surgeons can directly visualize the brain's surface and insert electrode threads. The new transdural approach skips this step entirely: the robotic insertion device now threads electrodes directly through the intact dura and into the brain. Eliminating the durotomy reduces procedural risk, shortens surgery time, and simplifies the operation — all factors that Neuralink says are critical to eventually scaling the procedure to far more patients with unmet medical needs.

3. **Engineering Challenge #1 — Mechanical Penetration of the Dura:** The dura's toughness posed an immediate mechanical problem: Neuralink's original needle design could not reliably puncture it. The team's solution was to modestly increase the needle diameter just enough to allow electrodes to pass through the dura consistently. To validate this, engineers developed a synthetic dural membrane that replicated the thickness and puncture-force characteristics of human dura, enabling hundreds of bench-top insertion tests before any clinical use.

4. **Engineering Challenge #2 — Seeing Through an Opaque Membrane:** With the dura intact, surgeons lose direct visual access to two critical pieces of information: the location of blood vessels (which must be avoided during insertion) and the precise distance from the dura's surface to the cortex. To solve the blood-vessel visibility problem, Neuralink's robot uses ICG (indocyanine green) videoangiography — a dye injected intravenously that, when illuminated with infrared light, causes blood vessels to glow visibly through the dura, allowing the robot to steer electrode threads safely around them.

5. **Engineering Challenge #3 — Measuring Depth with OCT:** To determine how deep to insert threads, Neuralink developed an optical coherence tomography (OCT) system integrated into the robot's head module. OCT uses laser light delivered via fiber optics to generate a real-time 3D volumetric map of the brain's layers, accurately measuring the changing distance from the dural surface to the cortex — a distance that shifts continuously in a living patient due to brain motion. This high-precision depth measurement allows threads to be inserted to the correct cortical depth reliably.

6. **Path Toward Automated, Scalable Surgery:** Neuralink frames the transdural approach as a stepping stone toward fully automated, robotically supervised surgery. The team's philosophy is to first eliminate unnecessary procedural steps (like the durotomy) before automating what remains — reducing complexity for the robot and shortening the overall procedure. The goal is to enable as many surgeries as possible, with clinical oversight but decreasing dependence on highly specialized manual surgical skill, in order to reach a broader population of patients.
