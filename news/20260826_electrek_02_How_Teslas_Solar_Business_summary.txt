The article traces how Tesla's residential solar business has shifted from a struggling in-house installer to a hardware-and-software model built around a new self-manufactured solar panel, Powerwall 3, and a growing network of third-party certified installers — a shift underscored by Tesla's recent discontinuation of Solar Roof.

## Energy
1. **Solar Roof discontinued after never scaling:** Tesla discontinued its Solar Roof glass tile product (launched 2016) earlier this month. By the end of 2022 it had installed only about 3,000 Solar Roofs in the US, roughly 0.17% of residential solar installations that year.

2. **Energy segment growth driven by storage, not solar:** Tesla's energy business generated $12.8 billion in revenue in 2025, up 27% year over year, but the article states this growth is almost entirely from battery storage products (Megapack and Powerwall) rather than rooftop solar, which has shrunk since Tesla acquired SolarCity for roughly $2.6 billion in 2016.

3. **New in-house solar panel launched:** In January 2026, Tesla launched a US-made 420-watt solar panel built at its Buffalo, New York Gigafactory (the former Solar Roof plant) — described by Tesla as the first panel it has fully designed and manufactured itself, featuring an all-black design, integrated mounting, and improved shade performance. It is designed to pair with Powerwall 3, which combines battery and solar inverter in one unit; Tesla's configurator now defaults to bundling solar with Powerwall 3, though solar-only remains an option.

4. **Shift to third-party certified installers:** Tesla began recruiting third-party "Certified Installers" in September 2024 while winding down its own installation crews. Tesla's support pages describe a hybrid model where certified installers handle installation in areas Tesla doesn't serve directly. The certified installer network grew from about 2,000 installers across 14 countries in late 2024 to more than 7,000 installers in 35-plus countries with over 1 million installations today, all operating on Tesla's "Tesla One" software platform.

5. **Two-tier sales/install model with unclear geography:** Tesla no longer publishes which states it installs in directly; buyers only learn whether they're in a "direct service territory" (Tesla installs) or a certified-installer territory (third party installs) by entering their address into Tesla's online tool. Sales are effectively national, with instant quotes, a $100 refundable deposit, a base cash price around $2.30 per watt before installation and incentives, and standardized financing: solar loans around 7.24%, Powerwall loans around 6.99%, and leases around 8.5%. Tesla's 25-year solar lease (12 years for Powerwall-only), with a five-year buyout option and 3% annual price escalator, is available only in Tesla's direct service territories.

## Editorial Stance
Fred Lambert argues Tesla has deliberately repositioned itself as a solar manufacturer and technology/software provider rather than a national installer, offloading the low-margin, labor-heavy installation work to certified partners while retaining hardware and software control; he notes the trade-off is that buyers face inconsistent experiences depending on whether they're in a Tesla direct market, and that Tesla makes this deliberately hard to determine upfront. He states rising electricity rates are currently the strongest argument for going solar.
