The video covers Wall Street's evolving SpaceX revenue estimates, framed around Cathy Wood's comments on collapsing AI inference costs and Morgan Stanley analyst Adam Jonas's bull case that adds Cursor as an unpriced upside driver for SpaceX stock. A guest, Jo Bhakdi, discusses why SpaceX's real long-term advantage is being a monopoly-like compute provider rather than an AI model developer, and the two hosts debate the likelihood and timing of a rumored SpaceX-Tesla merger along with China-related complications, drawing on a separate Ark Invest panel discussion.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation
1. **Morgan Stanley's Jonas sets a $600 SpaceX bull case tied to Cursor:** Adam Jonas of Morgan Stanley reportedly set a bull case of $600 per share for SpaceX stock, citing Cursor as a bridge to that upside — separate from Grok, X data, and Starlink, which he leaves out of the estimate entirely. Jonas's Cursor revenue estimates are $8 billion by year end and $33 billion by 2030, and he reportedly notes most investors he speaks with are not bullish on SpaceX's AI business beyond compute/data-center leasing.
2. **Deutsche Bank reportedly backs a $100 billion run-rate this year:** Deutsche Bank is said to see a $100 billion revenue run rate as achievable this year, citing cloud deals worth $45–50 billion plus Cursor as a second growth engine; the host notes Elon Musk has called $100 billion the "base line" (paraphrase, not a direct quote in transcript).
3. **Debate over merger timing:** The hosts discuss an Ark Invest panel where speakers speculated about SpaceX-Tesla merger timing, ranging from imminent to 6-12 months out. Jo Bhakdi argues a near-term merger announcement-with-consummation is unlikely because SpaceX's share price discovery is still in progress and most SpaceX shares remain unlocked; he estimates a merger is more likely "earliest next year." The hosts interpret Elon Musk's public comment "China's awesome, I strongly encourage people to visit" (in response to merger/China discussion) as a signal he is not denying a merger is under consideration, though this is speculative.
4. **China as a possible complicating factor for a merger:** Panelists in the Ark Invest discussion (not identified as any of the tracked interview subjects) speculated that Chinese regulators could use robotaxi cash flow or defense-contract concerns to complicate a SpaceX-Tesla merger; Bhakdi and the host both push back, arguing China is unlikely to block Optimus sales given its historically permissive, competition-driven approach to foreign tech (citing the smartphone market as precedent).

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Bhakdi's thesis: SpaceX as the dominant compute provider:** Jo Bhakdi argues that as AI model costs commoditize (per Cathy Wood's 99.99%/year cost-decline estimate), value shifts to whoever owns compute infrastructure — a moat he says SpaceX and roughly 5-10 hyperscalers/new clouds will control. He predicts SpaceX will lead terrestrial compute buildout for the next few years, then move to space-based compute, with a multi-year lack of competition in launch capacity.
2. **Bhakdi is skeptical of Cursor/Grok as a massive standalone revenue driver:** While agreeing directionally with Jonas's bull case, Bhakdi says he expects Cursor/Grok-related revenue to reach roughly $100 billion, not the "trillion"-scale outcomes some project, arguing enterprise AI adoption will be too fragmented and company-specific for any single vendor to capture outsized share of the broader (his figure) $22 trillion enterprise AI opportunity.
3. **Bhakdi predicts Musk will keep Grok's most advanced capability in-house:** He argues Musk's strategy is vertical integration — using Grok/Cursor internally to supercharge Optimus, robotaxi, Terafab, and space AI — rather than primarily licensing the technology externally, though he still expects "a few hundred billion" in external leasing revenue as a byproduct.

## Market & Competition
1. **China's asymmetric competitive strategy discussed:** Bhakdi contends China's strategy toward foreign tech (including any future Optimus imports) mirrors its historical approach to the smartphone market — allowing foreign products in, then rapidly domesticating and scaling manufacturing (citing Huawei and Xiaomi) rather than blocking imports outright, and argues U.S. regulators are more likely than China to restrict cross-border robot/AI exports.
