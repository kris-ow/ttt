This video is a recorded talk by a retired biologist and sustainability volunteer in Portland, Oregon, who advocates for buying used electric vehicles as a low-risk, affordable entry point into EV ownership. Her core thesis is that most people overestimate how much range and vehicle capability they actually need for daily driving, and that a used EV — matched carefully to real driving habits — offers exceptional value. The talk is personal and anecdotal rather than data-driven, drawing on her own decade-plus experience owning two minimalist EVs.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Personal EV ownership history — 2007 Zap Zebra:** The speaker's first EV was a 2007 Zap Zebra three-wheeler, a Chinese-made vehicle with a 40 mph top speed, four doors, four seat belts, no airbags, and a 20-mile range using lead-acid batteries. She drove it for eight years, primarily for a 3.5-mile commute and school runs, finding that her typical daily driving was 10 miles and a busy Saturday rarely exceeded 15–20 miles. The vehicle eventually became impossible to maintain due to parts unavailability — each of the three production years used different parts — and she gave it to a hobbyist.

2. **Second EV — 2012 Mitsubishi i-MiEV purchased used:** After eight years with the Zap Zebra, she transitioned to a 2012 Mitsubishi i-MiEV purchased in 2016 from a CarMax in Clackamas for $9,000. The car had only 13,000 miles on it, was 10 miles from her home (drivable without shipping), had a 62-mile EPA range, freeway speeds, and airbags. She has now owned it for approximately 10 years and reports virtually no battery degradation until recently — the car was even reading slightly above its rated range (around 67 miles) until a tire issue arose. The current problem involves a flat tire; Dunlop no longer makes the original tire size, and the heavier Continental replacements have reduced her range to approximately 55 miles.

3. **Core used EV argument — match vehicle to real driving patterns:** The speaker's central recommendation is that buyers should carefully audit their actual daily driving needs before purchasing any EV. She argues that most urban dwellers drive far fewer miles per day than they assume, and that a modest used EV with a shorter range is sufficient for the vast majority of trips. For longer, occasional trips — such as a road trip to national parks — she suggests simply using a gas car or renting, rather than buying a large, expensive EV to cover every possible use case.

4. **Battery longevity and chemistry improvements:** The speaker notes that lithium-ion batteries are dramatically more durable than the lead-acid batteries in her first EV. She observes that battery chemistry has continued to improve over time — becoming less toxic and less reliant on materials from conflict regions — and that her 2012 i-MiEV battery held its charge with minimal degradation for roughly a decade.

5. **Used Nissan Leaf caution — pre-2016 battery cooling issues:** The speaker references a handout from a Washington DC EV group that warns buyers about used Nissan Leafs, noting the handout contained a typo ("pre-2026" instead of "pre-2016"). She explains that Nissan Leafs manufactured before approximately 2016 had inadequate battery cooling systems, making battery pack longevity a concern — especially for cars that originated in hot climates like Arizona. She credits Nissan for being the first automaker to commit fully to selling an electric car to the public without hedging (no fleet-only or lease-only restrictions), but expresses disappointment that Nissan rested on its laurels with range improvements for too long.

6. **Chevy Bolt as high-value option:** The speaker highlights the Chevy Bolt as offering exceptional dollar-per-mile-of-range value, noting the base model is currently around $30,000 new and has approximately 250 miles of range. She notes that consumers complained loudly enough when GM tried to discontinue the Bolt that the company brought it back, which she views as a strong endorsement. She mentions watching for a used Bolt in five to six years at roughly half the new price as her own potential next vehicle.

7. **Automotive industry trend toward luxury over minimalism:** The speaker observes that automakers find it difficult to generate profit margins on minimalist, affordable vehicles and have increasingly shifted toward luxury and feature-heavy models. She uses the example of the Chevy Spark EV — a compliance car sold only in a handful of states with limited cargo space — as a case where GM failed to commit to an affordable EV segment, ultimately discontinuing it in favor of the higher-priced Bolt. She expresses concern that lower-income workers who need basic, affordable transportation to commute are being left behind by this trend.

8. **Local used EV dealership — Platt Auto Group:** The speaker recommends a local Portland-area dealer called Platt Auto Group, located on 99E between Milwaukie and Oregon City, as a starting point for used EV shoppers. She describes them as brand-neutral, no-haggle, knowledgeable about EVs, and thorough in servicing vehicles (e.g., taking a used Bolt to a Chevy dealer to ensure all recalls have been addressed). She states she has personally referred seven buyers to them. She also mentions other channels including Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, and national online platforms (with the caveat of added shipping costs).

9. **Autonomous driving and aging-in-place consideration:** An audience member raised the topic of Tesla's driver-assistance technology as a potential benefit for aging drivers — the idea that advanced safety features could help older individuals remain independent longer. The speaker acknowledged some merit in this argument, noting that safety expectations have progressively increased (from seat belts to airbags and beyond), while also referencing a personal experience riding in a Waymo in Austin that "got us where we needed to go and seemed to be doing it safely." She did not advocate for or against Tesla specifically, noting her own discomfort with the brand without elaborating further.
