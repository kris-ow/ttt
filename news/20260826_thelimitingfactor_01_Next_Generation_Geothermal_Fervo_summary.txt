## Market & Competition
1. **Fervo uses oil-and-gas-style horizontal drilling and fracking:** Fervo drills two parallel wells in dry rock, fractures the rock between them, and circulates water through a binary-cycle heat exchanger to generate power; its Project Red test site in Nevada hit a stress-test peak of 3.5 MW and a steady-state average of 1.4 MW (used to offset Google's local data center demand), and it's building a full-scale Utah facility targeting 100 MW by early 2027, 500 MW by 2028, and up to 2 GW long-term.
2. **Eavor's closed-loop "Rock Pipe" design underperformed in testing:** Eavor drills multi-branched, sealed closed-loop wells enabling passive thermosiphon circulation with minimal pumping and low seismic risk, but its German test site targeted 8.2 MW and instead delivered only 0.5–2 MW; as a result, Eavor is pivoting from building its own plants to licensing its drilling technology.
3. **Quaise's gyrotron drilling is the most speculative but highest-potential approach:** Quaise uses a high-power gyrotron (roughly 1 million watts at ~100 GHz, versus a microwave's ~1,000 watts at ~2.5 GHz) to vaporize ultra-hot, dense rock, enabling drilling 10–50x faster than conventional methods (an estimated 10 km well in about 100 days versus roughly 10 years) and eliminating the need for casing since the borehole walls vitrify into obsidian; the company still faces unresolved engineering challenges (plasma generation, debris removal, borehole stability) and targets a 50 MW well by 2030, though the host expects that timeline to slip.
4. **Cost comparison across the three companies:** Fervo targets $100/MWh within a few years and $50/MWh in the 2030s; Eavor's test facility implied costs over $200/MWh but the company claims a path to $75/MWh; Quaise's cost estimates range widely from $20–115/MWh, reflecting early-stage uncertainty. For context, solar and gas turbine generation currently run around $100/MWh.
5. **Host's overall industry outlook:** the host estimates the three companies combined might reach roughly 1 GW of next-generation geothermal capacity by 2030, with Fervo's approach seen as closest to commercial scale-up due to reliance on mature oil-and-gas drilling equipment and expertise, while permitting and grid interconnection remain the main bottlenecks; geothermal is framed as a strong baseload complement to solar, positioned favorably against gas (pollution concerns) and next-gen nuclear (financing/engineering challenges).
