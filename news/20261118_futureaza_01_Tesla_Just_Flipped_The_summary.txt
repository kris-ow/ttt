Host Brian talks with Chris of the Dirty Tesla channel, who has taken over a hundred robotaxi rides, about whether Tesla's robotaxi is ready. They argue the service has moved from a stalled, tightly limited phase back into rapid expansion over the last two to three weeks. Cars are being registered almost daily, geofences are growing and new areas are being added. They also debate Cybercab ride quality and the unit economics.

## Robotaxi
1. **Expansion has re-accelerated:** The host says Tesla expanded the geofence early on, then seemed to stall, and has now resumed. Over the last two to three weeks Tesla has registered cars almost every other day, enlarged geofences and added completely new areas. Fort Worth and San Antonio are mentioned as launching the next morning. Chris expects the next six to twelve months to be "just crazy" and to surprise people.
2. **Service evolution from the rider's seat:** Early rides had a safety passenger who watched closely and occasionally intervened or called base. Rides became smoother and more affordable once the passenger was removed. Chris describes early availability as poor: app waits of 10 to 40 minutes, and around March 2026 he could not get an unsupervised ride after an hour inside the geofence. He says that a month later, at the South by Southwest X takeover, Model Y and Cybercab rides arrived in about five to ten minutes.
3. **Cybercab as a product:** Chris calls the Cybercab a much better experience than the Model Y, citing space, quiet and novelty. He and the host usually chose it even when it cost about two dollars more per ride. He argues the premium is temporary because early units carry new-line depreciation, and he believes the Cybercab's marginal cost per unit is lower than the Model Y's. His complaints are minor: the USB-C ports need illumination, and Tesla should add grab handles for accessibility. He also expects Starlink connectivity to be added eventually.
4. **Geofences and mapping:** Chris does not expect Tesla to declare statewide Texas operation. He believes Tesla still does some mapping and needs to check roads. He finds it odd that the newly added cities do not have Cybercabs when the Model Y can already operate there. Both hosts are skeptical that cars actually drive everywhere inside the advertised geofence, and Chris was turned back when he tried to travel far within it.
5. **Nevada permit:** Chris says the Nevada permit approves 5,000 vehicles and, as he recalls, carries a December 18 deadline to launch. He expects Tesla to use it, even if only with a handful of cars at first.
6. **Operations after removing drivers:** The hosts argue a robotaxi fleet still needs staff for cleaning, lost and found and tire checks, but far fewer than human-driven fleets. Chris notes that cars of the same model on the same local roads will wear tires on the same schedule, which makes maintenance predictable. He also argues that Cybercab service will be consistent across cities, unlike human-driven ride-hailing.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Software version on the Cybercab:** Chris says Ashok Elluswamy indicated on X that the Cybercab runs FSD version 15, and he recalls him saying only about half of 12 planned features had been implemented before a customer release. He says it drives with more confidence than his own car did, with no unnecessary hard braking when a vehicle slows at a stop sign. He adds that his own car no longer does this either.

## Market & Competition
1. **Cost per mile is unresolved:** Chris estimates electricity at roughly 4–5 cents per mile in the worst markets and about 1 cent in the Northwest. He thinks total operating cost including tires and maintenance could land under 20 cents per mile. The host pushes back that service centers, platform costs, insurance, payment processing and registration fees add up, and notes that other ride-hailing networks have charged around $2 per mile in platform fees and still posted losses. They agree nobody knows the true figure yet.
2. **Versus used cars and Waymo:** Chris argues Tesla could build a Model 3-class car for roughly $17,000 at scale, so a used car under that price would be costly to maintain and would not last 300,000 to half a million miles. He calls Waymo the only serious competitor, with a significantly higher vehicle cost, many more sensors and slower mapping. He sees no competitor with a comparable path to lower cost.
