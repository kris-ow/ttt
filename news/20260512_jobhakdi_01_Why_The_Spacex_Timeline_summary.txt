This video from Jo Bhakdi covers the emerging thesis around orbital AI data centers, centered on SpaceX's growing partnerships with major tech companies (Google and Anthropic) to deploy compute satellites in space. Bhakdi argues that a severe terrestrial energy crisis — driven by surging AI electricity demand — makes space-based compute not just viable but inevitable, and that SpaceX's Starship is the critical bottleneck and monopoly asset enabling this transition. The video also touches on implications for Tesla shareholders in the context of a potential Tesla-SpaceX merger.

## Market & Competition

1. **Google and Anthropic Partner with SpaceX for Orbital AI Compute:** Bhakdi highlights two major breaking developments: Anthropic announced a deal to access SpaceX's Colossus One terrestrial AI data center (described as a massive compute facility originally built by xAI and then acquired by SpaceX), and as part of that agreement, expressed interest in partnering with SpaceX to develop "multiple gigawatts" of orbital AI compute capacity. Shortly after, Google announced it is in talks with SpaceX to place an orbital data center in outer space. Bhakdi claims he was among the first to model the economics of orbital AI compute, doing so in November of the prior year.

2. **Terrestrial Energy Crisis as the Core Driver:** Bhakdi presents data showing AI and cloud compute consumed 4% of total U.S. electricity in 2023, rising to 7% in 2026, projected to reach 20% by 2030 and 50% by 2035 — a trajectory he describes as completely unsustainable. He notes that gas turbines are the only near-term tactical solution, but that the three companies that manufacture them globally (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Power) have order books stretched to 2029–2030, and turbine prices have nearly tripled since 2019, reaching approximately $600 per kilowatt by end of 2027. He concludes that the energy buildout cannot keep pace with AI demand on Earth.

3. **Space Compute as the Energy Solution:** Bhakdi argues that orbital data centers solve the energy crisis because space provides approximately five times more solar energy per square foot per day compared to Earth, with 24/7 sunlight, 40% higher solar flux, and no need for batteries. He adds that space requires no land lease, no water cooling, and no grid infrastructure. The only meaningful cost becomes orbital lift, which he frames as entirely dependent on Starship's cost-per-kilogram trajectory.

4. **Starship Lift Cost as the Unlock:** Bhakdi states that current heavy-lift costs run approximately $25,000 per kilogram, and that Starship is projected to bring this down to $200 per kilogram — which he describes as a 10x cut he expects to be achievable by 2027. He further references a theoretical endpoint of $10 per kilogram but says he'll believe it when he sees it. He argues that the $200/kg figure alone is sufficient to economically justify shifting all AI compute buildout to space, and predicts this transition will begin in earnest in 2027, not 2029 or 2030.

5. **SpaceX's Satellite Launch Dominance:** Bhakdi cites a chart from analyst Christian Kyle showing that SpaceX is now only approximately 200 satellites away from having launched as many satellites as the rest of the world combined in all of history — with the rest of the world at 15,662 total historical launches. He uses this to characterize SpaceX as holding a monopoly on orbital access, comparing it to having a monopoly on ships during the age of exploration.

6. **Hyperscaler Race to Space:** Bhakdi predicts that rather than purchasing Elon's satellites, major hyperscalers will each book Starship capacity to launch their own proprietary space compute. He forecasts that Amazon will be the next announced partner, followed by Microsoft and Oracle, with a final landscape of approximately five or six "space clouds" — SpaceX, Google, Anthropic, Amazon, Microsoft, and possibly Oracle — each owning orbital AI infrastructure. He argues Elon is strategically correct not to monopolize all space compute, as diversity prevents political and reputational backlash.

7. **Ron Baron on SpaceX IPO Mechanics:** Bhakdi closes with a clip of investor Ron Baron discussing the SpaceX IPO. Baron notes that approximately $70 billion of the offering would be available to public investors, and describes an accelerated index inclusion timeline in which SpaceX would be added to one index five days post-IPO, a second index at ten days, and a third at fifteen days. Baron confirms SpaceX has chosen to list on NASDAQ rather than the NYSE. Baron also explains the "not in my backyard" political dynamic — citing governors in New Jersey and Virginia running on platforms opposing data center construction due to rising electricity bills and water usage — as a further political tailwind for moving compute to space.
