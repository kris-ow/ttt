This video from "brighterwithherbert" argues that corporate skeptics of Elon Musk's ventures (AT&T, T-Mobile, Verizon, Delta) tend to be right about timing but wrong about ultimate feasibility, drawing parallels to past doubters of Tesla Semi, FSD, and robotaxi who were eventually proven wrong on substance even when their delay predictions held up. The host frames this as a lens for investors: ask what's at stake for the person making the doubt before weighing it.

## SpaceX — Starlink
1. **Telecom CEOs dismiss Starlink's phone ambitions:** AT&T CEO John Stankey told Axios that SpaceX's plan to turn Starlink rooftop antennas into neighborhood cell towers (via compact base stations/femtocells) is an "unviable strategy," citing cost parity with macro networks and the need for regulatory permission to broadcast cellular signals from private rooftops; he also argued that if it worked, AT&T would already be doing it.
2. **T-Mobile and Verizon echo skepticism:** T-Mobile CEO Srini Gopalani told the Financial Times the Starlink threat is exaggerated; T-Mobile CFO Peter Osvaldik said "physics" prevents satellite-to-phone from being a full substitute, estimated the small-cell plan would need over a billion femtocells, and noted satellite communications are currently just 0.0002% of T-Mobile's traffic. Verizon CEO Dan Schulman said he sees zero discernible impact from Starlink on Verizon's broadband growth, calling Verizon's network up to 1,000x more efficient in cities.
3. **SpaceX pushes back with growth numbers:** On SpaceX's August 4 earnings call, President Gwynne Shotwell noted carriers collectively earn about $600 billion a year and said SpaceX expects to win customers from them; Elon Musk said Starlink's terrestrial-adjacent network would likely offer higher bandwidth than current cellular. Telecom stocks fell the next day. Elon also said Starlink's upcoming V2 satellites (launching next year, using spectrum bought from EchoStar for about $20 billion) will offer 100x the bandwidth of V1. Full Starlink phone service isn't expected until late 2027, and Starlink has also launched a new program letting subscribers share their home connection with others for a revenue cut.
4. **Historical parallel — AT&T's McKinsey miscall:** The host cites a secondhand account (via a post from a user named Amy) that AT&T once had McKinsey forecast the mobile phone market at under a million phones by 2000, versus an eventual actual figure above 100 million, leading AT&T to later pay $12.6 billion for McCaw Cellular to re-enter the market it had dismissed — used as an analogy for current telecom skepticism toward Starlink.
5. **Bernstein flags limits of the thesis:** Analyst Madison Resai wrote that cell towers "won't disappear" and that Starlink mobile may fill a real niche in rural areas and during disasters but remains far from replacing terrestrial networks, citing indoor signal difficulty and consumer reluctance to switch carriers.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Tesla Semi enters mass production after years of doubt:** Daimler Trucks CEO Martin Daum said in 2018 that "the same laws of physics" working in Germany and California made Tesla's 500-mile Semi claim implausible; Bill Gates separately wrote in 2020 that EVs were unlikely to be practical for 18-wheelers even with battery breakthroughs. Tesla opened a dedicated Semi factory in Sparks, Nevada (capacity 50,000 trucks/year) and began handing over long-range (500-mile) Semis to PepsiCo, DHL, and US Foods — about seven years later than the originally planned 2019 production start.

## Robotaxi
1. **Former Waymo CEO's "I'm still waiting" claim overtaken:** In August 2025, former Waymo CEO John Krafcik said he was "still waiting" for Tesla to launch a real robotaxi, arguing a car isn't a robotaxi if an employee is inside — a fair point at the time given Tesla's in-car safety inspectors. On September 4, Tesla opened Cybercab rides to the public in Austin with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no driver present.
2. **Cybercab fleet still small at launch:** At the Austin launch, 45 Cybercabs were permitted for trips, a number the host says has since grown (no updated figure given); Tesla also retains remote operators who can intervene if a vehicle gets stuck.

## Market & Competition
1. **Pattern across Elon ventures — right on "if," wrong on "when":** The host runs through a string of past doubts (Daimler on Semi in 2018, Bill Gates on EV trucks in 2020, Bob Lutz saying in 2017 Tesla was "going bankrupt," Morgan Stanley's 2019 worst-case $10/share Tesla forecast, Krafcik on robotaxis, a Barron's writer's September admission that SpaceX "won" its merger with xAI rather than losing as predicted) alongside a counter-list of Elon's own missed timelines (2017 coast-to-coast self-driving promised by end of 2017, actually achieved December 31, 2025 per a Tesla owner's unintervened FSD trip; 2019 prediction of a million robotaxis by 2020, not realized; the 2017 Roadster now demoed October 15, 2026 after another delay; shifting Mars-vs-Moon timeline statements in early 2025). The host's thesis: skeptics are usually right about delays and wrong about ultimate feasibility.
2. **Delta's Starlink rejection framed as personal, not technical:** Delta chose Amazon's Leo satellite internet over Starlink, reportedly over wanting to keep its own Wi-Fi login portal; Elon publicly criticized the decision. An account ("John NYC") relayed unconfirmed remarks allegedly made by Delta CEO Ed Bastian at corporate events, including "we don't want to deal with Elon Musk" — the host stresses these quotes are unverified rumors that Delta has not confirmed. United has Starlink on over 560 aircraft with full fleet coverage planned by end of 2027, versus Delta's Amazon rollout not starting until 2028 across 500 aircraft. A similar fight occurred with Ryanair's CEO in January over Starlink cost/drag concerns.
3. **Upcoming bets facing fresh skepticism:** Bernstein's Tony Sacconaghi said Tesla's Terafab chip plant "could be more difficult than sending rockets to Mars"; Sam Altman called orbital data centers "pointless" for now, though he allowed they could matter eventually.

## SpaceX — Starship
1. **Starship reaches orbit on first attempt:** Starship launched into orbit for the first time on its first attempt last Monday, carrying 26 Starlink satellites; Elon called it a success. This follows a history of doubts, including former NASA head Sean Duffy saying last October that SpaceX was "falling behind" on the lunar lander contract and opening it to competitors.
