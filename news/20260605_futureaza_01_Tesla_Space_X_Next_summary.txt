This video features Brian (FutureAzA) in conversation with Herbert from Brighter, discussing what they frame as the next trillion-dollar opportunity emerging from a joint Tesla/SpaceX venture to build a vertically integrated semiconductor manufacturing facility called Terafab. The hosts also explore SpaceX AI's broader valuation case, Starlink's growth trajectory, and the potential of Macrohard (an agentic AI product) as part of the SpaceX AI ecosystem.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Terafab land acquisition signals real momentum:** A purchasing entity called Terafab Wit LLC, sharing a physical address with XAI in Palo Alto, has acquired 3,000 acres in Grimes County, Texas near the Gibbons Creek Reservoir area. Herbert describes this as part of SpaceX Reinvestment Zone Number One and characterizes it as a joint Tesla/SpaceX project to manufacture chips — not just ideas on paper, but active forward movement. Herbert cautions, however, that Terafab is unlikely to be in production for at least three or more years.

2. **Chip production rationale mirrors the Giga Nevada battery logic:** The hosts draw a direct parallel between Elon Musk's decision to build Giga Nevada for battery production — when conventional wisdom said to just buy batteries — and the current Terafab chip strategy. Herbert explains that Musk calculated the number of chips needed for humanoid robots, autonomous vehicles, AI data centers, and potentially personal devices (watches, glasses, necklaces), and concluded no external supplier could meet the demand. Herbert believes chip output won't be sold like Nvidia sells chips, but rather rented as a compute service — with any excess capacity licensed out short-term, as illustrated by a referenced deal renting compute to Anthropic for $1.25 billion per month, with a 90-day exit clause Musk reportedly insisted upon.

3. **Terafab's design philosophy: optimized inference chips, not general-purpose:** Herbert argues that unlike TSMC, which must design for every conceivable use case, Terafab's chips will be narrowly optimized for end-device inference — bots, cars, and AI data centers. This focus could allow Tesla/SpaceX to skip legacy manufacturing steps that incumbents maintain due to sunk costs. Musk reportedly hinted at one such simplification on a recent podcast, suggesting he could eat a burger inside the facility — implying the sterile cleanroom environment would be limited only to the area where chips are actually moving, rather than the entire facility. Herbert also notes that a partnership with Intel brings decades of manufacturing experience, meaningfully reducing (though not eliminating) the risk of failure. Grok was cited as an early analyst of the Terafab concept, reportedly identifying legacy steps that could be eliminated.

4. **Bot form factors beyond humanoids:** Herbert emphasizes that Elon Musk frequently references not just C-3PO-style humanoids but also R2-D2-style non-humanoid robots, suggesting a wide range of bot form factors is planned. Every device — bots, cars, personal electronics — will require chips, and Herbert argues that even Terafab's projected output (described as roughly 10x current available capacity) may ultimately be insufficient to meet total demand.

## Market & Competition
1. **Morningstar's SpaceX AI bearish report challenged:** The hosts reference a Morningstar report arguing SpaceX AI is worth less than half its current valuation, which Brian and Herbert push back on. Herbert cites independent space analysts Aaron Bernett of Mach 33 and Larry Goldberg, whose modeling reportedly shows Starlink revenue is already comparable in scale to robotaxi revenue. Herbert argues Morningstar underestimates Starlink's near-term trajectory and that the AI data center and enterprise AI revenue streams are closer than a five-plus-year horizon implies.

2. **Starlink V3 represents a potential 10x capacity expansion:** Herbert describes the rollout of Starlink V3 satellites — enabled by Starship's greater launch capacity and lower per-launch cost versus Falcon 9 (whose upper stage is expendable) — as a transformational capacity upgrade. Each V3 satellite offers substantially greater bandwidth, and Starship can deploy substantially more satellites per launch. Herbert states that the current Starlink user base is approximately 10 million, and that V3 deployment could support a roughly 10x increase in capability. Starlink reportedly lost $600 million in the prior year (2025), which Brian attributes to the service still being in expansion mode rather than a structural weakness.

3. **Macrohard: agentic AI with vision-only thesis, but valuation withheld for now:** Herbert introduces Macrohard (also referred to as "Grok Build") as SpaceX AI's agentic AI product, whose competitive thesis rests on vision-only interaction — the ability to look at any screen visually and control it like a human, without requiring API integrations. Herbert argues this could give it an edge over Anthropic and OpenAI in enterprise settings. Brian, however, explicitly assigns Macrohard zero value in his current model, stating he will only assign value when revenue is demonstrably real — contrasting it with rockets and satellites, which he can see and evaluate today.

## Financials
1. **SpaceX AI TAM cited at $26.5 trillion:** Brian references a total addressable market figure of $26.5 trillion for SpaceX AI, which he uses to justify not dismissing any part of the business — though he withholds valuation credit for Macrohard until tangible revenue emerges.

2. **Compute monetization model: rent, don't sell:** Herbert argues Musk's preferred model for surplus chip and compute capacity is rental rather than outright chip sales. The referenced Anthropic arrangement — $1.25 billion per month, 90-day exit clause — is presented as the template: use what you need internally, rent out the rest on short-term terms to retain optionality.
