Conventional lithium-ion batteries&nbsp;&nbsp;
use a small weight percentage of binder in the&nbsp;
electrodes for cohesion and adhesion. Cohesion&nbsp;&nbsp;
means that the binder holds the electrode&nbsp;
together. Adhesion means the binder allows&nbsp;&nbsp;
the electrode to stick to the current collector&nbsp;
foils and to a lesser extent the separator.
&nbsp;
For battery chemistries used in vehicles,&nbsp;
that binder must maintain its structural&nbsp;&nbsp;
and chemical integrity over thousands of cycles&nbsp;
and for more than 10 years. That means the best&nbsp;&nbsp;
binders typically have low surface energy,&nbsp;
which means their internal chemical bonds&nbsp;&nbsp;
are strong and they don t seek bonds with&nbsp;
other chemicals. That makes them slippery,&nbsp;&nbsp;
non-reactive, and repellent to most liquids.
However, although those qualities add durability&nbsp;&nbsp;
and longevity to the battery cell, they create&nbsp;
challenges for battery manufacturing particularly&nbsp;&nbsp;
for electrolyte wetting. Let s take a look.
After the electrode is coated, it s married&nbsp;&nbsp;
with a separator and rolled into what&nbsp;
s called a jellyroll. The jellyroll&nbsp;&nbsp;
is then inserted into the battery cell,&nbsp;
which is then filled with electrolyte.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The entire jellyroll must completely absorb that&nbsp;
electrolyte because it s what allows lithium-ions&nbsp;&nbsp;
to travel between the cathode and anode, which is&nbsp;
how lithium batteries store and release energy.
&nbsp;
Binders interfere with that absorption, or wetting&nbsp;
process, because they form a smooth waxy surface&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the electrode that repels liquids. That matters&nbsp;
because if it takes days for the battery cells&nbsp;&nbsp;
to fully absorb the electrolyte and the factory&nbsp;
is producing millions of battery cells per day,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it means increased warehousing costs.
To avoid that, manufacturers use a variety&nbsp;&nbsp;
of tricks to accelerate the absorption of the&nbsp;
electrolyte, such as creating a vacuum in the&nbsp;&nbsp;
cell before injecting the electrolyte, injecting&nbsp;
the electrolyte with high external pressure,&nbsp;&nbsp;
high temperature filling, laser structuring to&nbsp;
create capillary channels, and surfactants to&nbsp;&nbsp;
reduce the surface tension of the electrolyte.
All of those come with disadvantages. For example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
placing the cell under vacuum and then extreme&nbsp;
pressure can reduce the stiffness of the electrode&nbsp;&nbsp;
by about 50%. That, in turn, can cause the tight&nbsp;
inner spiral of the battery cell to unwind, or in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the words of the patent application, collapse.
In July of 2024, core collapse was cited by Steve&nbsp;&nbsp;
LeVine of The Information as a major hurdle&nbsp;
that the 4680 needed to clear before high&nbsp;&nbsp;
volume manufacturing. It looks like, as usual,&nbsp;
solving that problem took longer than expected,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it looks like it was likely solved&nbsp;
by plasma treatment of the electrodes.
&nbsp;
Unfortunately, the patent application&nbsp;
didn t precisely explain how plasma&nbsp;&nbsp;
treatment solved the core collapse&nbsp;
problem, but I have a few guesses.
&nbsp;
First, the sudden pressure changes caused&nbsp;
by placing the cell under vacuum and then&nbsp;&nbsp;
high pressure which I ll call whiplash - could&nbsp;
collapse pores in the electrode and separator,&nbsp;&nbsp;
weakening them like ballons that ve been&nbsp;
inflated then deflated. That in turn is what&nbsp;&nbsp;
might be causing the 50% reduction in stiffness.&nbsp;
If the electrolyte was able to wet the electrode&nbsp;&nbsp;
without those pressure changes, it could maintain&nbsp;
the pore integrity and therefore stiffness.
&nbsp;
Second, that whiplash could also push and&nbsp;
pull on the inner spiral of the jellyroll.&nbsp;&nbsp;
That again could be avoided by reducing the&nbsp;
vacuum and electrolyte injection pressure.
&nbsp;
Third, as we ll see later in the video, the&nbsp;
plasma treatment causes the electrode to stick&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the separator, which effectively&nbsp;
glues the entire jellyroll together&nbsp;&nbsp;
so that it s less likely to unwind.
Fourth, the plasma treatment might also&nbsp;&nbsp;
help the electrode to stick more&nbsp;
firmly to the current collector.&nbsp;&nbsp;
That matters because in cylindrical cells like&nbsp;
the 4680, as the spiral gets tighter in the core,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the stress at the leading edge of the electrode&nbsp;
coating can exceed its bond strength with the&nbsp;&nbsp;
current collector and cause it to delaminate.
Tesla s dry coating process creates an additional&nbsp;&nbsp;
challenge here because it s able to create&nbsp;
thicker electrode coatings than a typical wet&nbsp;&nbsp;
coating process. Although a thicker electrode&nbsp;
coating is desirable because it increases the&nbsp;&nbsp;
amount of active energy storing material in&nbsp;
a battery cell, it s also stiffer and more&nbsp;&nbsp;
resistant to bending, which can increase the&nbsp;
likelihood of delamination at the core.
&nbsp;
As a side note, the reason why a wet coating&nbsp;
process can t make electrodes as thick as a&nbsp;&nbsp;
dry process is because the wet coating mixture&nbsp;
is made up of a solvent, cathode particles,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and additives. If that mixture is applied too&nbsp;
thick, the particles can sink to the bottom of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the liquid before it dries which renders the&nbsp;
electrode unusable. The dry coating mixture,&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the other hand, consists of particles&nbsp;
and binder that form a kind of dough,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which carries no risk of stratification.
Before we move on, interestingly,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the plasma treatment process could also be&nbsp;
very useful in a wet electrode coating process,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but Tesla discovered the plasma treatment process&nbsp;
first as a matter of necessity. That s because the&nbsp;&nbsp;
surface energy of the PTFE, or Teflon, that&nbsp;
s used in the dry electrode coating process&nbsp;&nbsp;
is even lower than the binders used in a wet&nbsp;
coating process, meaning it s more resistant&nbsp;&nbsp;
to electrolyte saturation and was likely causing&nbsp;
Tesla headaches for manufacturing throughput.

How does the plasma treatment process work?
&nbsp;
As I said earlier, binder material, especially the&nbsp;
PTFE binder that Tesla uses for their dry process,&nbsp;&nbsp;
has a stable and non-interacting surface that&nbsp;
makes it slippery and repellent to most liquids.
&nbsp;
In order to make the electrode more absorbent&nbsp;
so that it can be saturated with electrolyte and&nbsp;&nbsp;
sticky so that it can adhere more strongly to&nbsp;
the separator or current collector, the stable&nbsp;&nbsp;
bonds in the PTFE on the surface of the current&nbsp;
collector need to be disrupted or modified.
&nbsp;
But wouldn t the PTFE bonds throughout the volume&nbsp;
of the electrode need to be disrupted to allow&nbsp;&nbsp;
wetting not just the surface? No, because&nbsp;
as long as the surface bonds are disrupted,&nbsp;&nbsp;
capillary action does the rest and draws&nbsp;
the electrolyte through the electrode.
&nbsp;
Tesla s solution to disrupt the bonds, or in&nbsp;
their words activate the binder, is to use a&nbsp;&nbsp;
plasma made from hydrogen, nitrogen, argon, or&nbsp;
oxygen gas. That results in defluorination and&nbsp;&nbsp;
hydroxylation at the electrode surface.
Beyond those two broad chemical mechanisms,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Tesla provides no information on the&nbsp;
deeper chemistry involved so I made&nbsp;&nbsp;
the image on screen to illustrate what could&nbsp;
be happening. However, I only have certainty&nbsp;&nbsp;
that the first panel is correct and the last&nbsp;
panel is plausible. The two middle panels are&nbsp;&nbsp;
just guesses based on the definition of&nbsp;
defluorination and hydroxylation because&nbsp;&nbsp;
depending on the gas used to create the plasma&nbsp;
many different chemical routes are possible.
&nbsp;
Defluorination means breaking fluorine&nbsp;
bonds, which are present in PTFE,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and hydroxylation means adding hydroxyl groups.&nbsp;
Both of those broad chemical processes can lead&nbsp;&nbsp;
to higher surface energy that s better&nbsp;
for bonding and electrode wetting.
&nbsp;
Either way, despite the large number of chemical&nbsp;
mechanisms that could be taking place, overall,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the plasma treatment process is straightforward&nbsp;
and it surprises me that Tesla appears to be the&nbsp;&nbsp;
first in the industry to try it. The difficult&nbsp;
part is finding the optimum recipe, so to speak,&nbsp;&nbsp;
for a high throughput production process.
In that vein, the patent application lists pages&nbsp;&nbsp;
of options in terms of plasma power, temperature,&nbsp;
pressure, duration and flow rate. But,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as usual I ll skip grinding through those details&nbsp;
because the options are infinitely variable and&nbsp;&nbsp;
what matters more is cost and results.
It doesn t appear to be a costly process&nbsp;&nbsp;
because in terms of material costs the gases are&nbsp;
cheap and used in extremely low concentrations,&nbsp;&nbsp;
such as .1%. As for energy costs the plasma&nbsp;
only uses between 200-4000 watts of power,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is minuscule for an industrial process.&nbsp;
As for time, it only took .1-10 seconds using&nbsp;&nbsp;
an atmospheric plasma treatment process at several&nbsp;
hundred watts, and for a vacuum plasma treatment&nbsp;&nbsp;
process it took up to 20 minutes at 4000 watts.
That is, the atmospheric option was much faster,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so it seems to be more promising as&nbsp;
a scale-up candidate for industrial&nbsp;&nbsp;
scale battery manufacturing, where line&nbsp;
speeds can exceed 100 meters per minute.

That, in turn, might have positive&nbsp;

In summary, necessity is the mother of invention.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The PTFE used in Tesla s dry electrode&nbsp;
coating process was likely creating greater&nbsp;&nbsp;
bottlenecks for electrolyte wetting than&nbsp;
a typical wet coating process because PTFE&nbsp;&nbsp;
has a greater tendency to repel liquids. 2g1
That forced them to ditch the typical methods&nbsp;&nbsp;
of improving wettability and rethink the wetting&nbsp;
process at a first principles level, which led&nbsp;&nbsp;
to plasma treatment. Plasma treatment improves&nbsp;
electrode wetting to such a great degree that it&nbsp;&nbsp;
may allow them to skip process steps like vacuum&nbsp;
filling, high pressure electrolyte injection,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and soaking which would reduce the size of&nbsp;
the manufacturing line and cell costs.
&nbsp;
The plasma treatment process also holds&nbsp;
potential benefits for performance by&nbsp;&nbsp;
decreasing the core size of the battery cell to&nbsp;
increase energy density, reducing energy losses&nbsp;&nbsp;
during formation, and improving longevity.
