This video recaps a lengthy MotorTrend interview/test drive of the Tesla Semi, discussed by two hosts, centering on FreightWaves editorial director Adam Wingfield's shift from skeptic to believer after driving the truck loaded and unloaded. The core thesis: the Semi's torque, single-gear drivetrain, regenerative braking, and operational advantages (cold-weather handling, software-driven amenities) make it compelling versus diesel despite initial doubts about range and blind spots.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Skeptical trucking veteran reverses his view after driving the Semi:** Adam Wingfield, a 30-year trucking veteran and owner-operator (editorial director at FreightWaves), said he initially doubted Tesla's range claims — noting Mercedes claims around 200–250 miles and other manufacturers around 150–300 miles for electric tractors — and expected Tesla's 500-mile claim to be implausible given weight limits. After driving the Semi unloaded and then loaded, he said his skepticism reversed, calling it a product that "changes the market" and repeatedly noting he was not paid for the video.
2. **Central seating and blind spots were a non-issue:** Wingfield expected the Semi's centered driver seat to create blind spots when backing into docks, but found that the A-pillars are positioned far enough out that he could see past them with minor head movement.
3. **Strong torque and minimal speed loss on grades:** While driving with a stated payload of 37,000 pounds, Wingfield climbed a 7–8% grade starting at 35 mph and said his speed dropped only to about 34 mph, holding the pedal down for 40–50 seconds without issue — a contrast to diesel trucks, which he said would typically require shifting through sixth or seventh gear on a similar climb.
4. **Regenerative braking reduced reliance on service brakes:** Wingfield said he used the physical brakes only once or twice on his long test trip, with regenerative braking handling most deceleration — which he preferred over traditional engine braking, comparing the sensation to manual-transmission driving.
5. **Ride quality assessed as comparable or slightly better than diesel:** Driving unloaded from the Giga Nevada area to Fremont, Wingfield said the ride over bumps felt a bit better than what he recalled from diesel trucks, while praising the turning radius and acceleration.
6. **Energy consumption data shown crossing Donner Pass:** The hosts described graphs from the Giga Nevada-to-Fremont test drive showing battery charge dropping sharply on the climb and partially recovering via regeneration on the descent.
7. **Charging costs more complex than simple per-kWh pricing:** One host said he'd previously been wrong to assume roughly 10 cents per kWh for Semi charging, noting that peak/night rates and demand (capacity) charges — where a single high-usage 15-minute window can raise costs for the entire month — plus charger hardware and potential site electrical upgrades or Megapack buffering, all add to operating costs. Even so, he argued the Semi still comes out ahead of diesel even if diesel prices fell to $3–4 per gallon.
8. **Cold-weather depot savings cited by a fleet buyer:** An unnamed owner of HMD, a company that ordered 50 Tesla Semi tractors, told the interviewer that in Chicago winters his depot currently needs around 20 workers to keep diesel trucks warmed up and running so fuel doesn't thicken or freeze; he expects the Tesla Semi to eliminate much of that need via remote warm-up or simply plugging in, reducing staffing requirements.
9. **Driver-profile and software amenities praised:** Wingfield highlighted a transferable, adjustable driver profile and over-the-air software updates as incremental conveniences that, combined with diesel-equivalent functionality and lower operating costs, made him question why he'd buy anything else.
