Electrek reports that Tesla filed four new "Robotaxi" crash reports with NHTSA, including one in Houston where a Tesla remote operator crashed the vehicle into a hidden tree stump while trying to extract it from a dead-end road — marking the first time Tesla has formally coded a crash as caused by a "Remote (Commercial / Test)" operator in its structured NHTSA data.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Houston remote-operator crash:** In May, a Model Y stopped on a dead-end residential road in Houston; while a remote assistance operator maneuvered it out, the vehicle entered a grassy slope and struck a hidden tree stump at 2 mph. Damage was minor (property only, no injuries, no tow). This is report 13781-15395, and the first of Tesla's 22 unique ADS incident reports where Tesla explicitly lists the driver/operator type as remote.

2. **Pattern of three remote-operator crashes:** This is the third Tesla-reported crash caused by a remote operator taking control after the ADS got stuck: a July 2025 Austin incident where a teleoperator drove up a curb into a metal fence at 8 mph, and a January 2026 incident where a teleoperator hit a construction barricade at 9 mph. The author notes Tesla doesn't disclose intervention frequency, so no rate can be calculated, but every disclosed remote intervention has ended in a collision.

3. **Contrast with Waymo's teleoperation policy:** Per a February 2026 letter to Senator Ed Markey cited in the article, Waymo says it has not used remote driving/teleoperations where a human performs the driving task — its remote agents only provide guidance the Waymo Driver can accept or reject. Waymo's one exception is a training-only tool allowing a specially trained agent to nudge a stuck vehicle forward at 2 mph with fixed steering angles, which Waymo says has never been used on a public road.

4. **Additional crash reports (Austin, Dallas):** An Austin crash (report 13781-15342, June) involved an SUV reversing into a stopped Tesla in a parking lot — the only 5-Day report of the batch and the only one involving a tow; the narrative says the Tesla "came to a stop" but the structured data field lists its movement as "Backing," a contradiction the article flags. A Dallas crash (report 13781-15399, May) — Tesla's first reported crash in that city — involved the car hitting a metal chain blocking a parking lot entrance at 5 mph, echoing a nearly identical September 2025 Austin incident at 6 mph. A second Austin crash (report 13781-15400, May) involved the Tesla following a construction truck that reversed into it; two of the four reports in this batch were attributed to the other party.

5. **Disclosure history:** Tesla was previously the only ADS operator redacting all crash narratives under NHTSA's Standing General Order, citing potential financial harm, while competitors like Waymo, Zoox, Avride, and May Mobility filed detailed accounts. Tesla reversed course in a May data dump, unredacting 17 crash narratives, which the article says largely showed most Tesla ADS crashes were caused by inattentive human drivers rather than the ADS.

6. **Low vehicle counts amplify incident rate:** The article cites Robotaxi Tracker data noted in Electrek's June analysis showing Tesla had roughly three active vehicles in Houston and seven in Dallas at the time, with Austin at roughly 16 — meaning Houston and Dallas each produced one crash off a small fleet fraction.

## Editorial Stance
Electrek's Fred Lambert credits Tesla for ending narrative redactions, saying it vindicated Tesla's safety record on fault, but argues the same transparency exposes a real weakness in teleoperation: with only three cars in Houston, one needed a remote rescue that ended in a crash, illustrating how often the cars need human bailout and how poorly that goes. The author contrasts this with Waymo's more conservative, largely unused remote-recovery approach and concludes Tesla still has significant work before its unsupervised robotaxi/FSD can scale to higher-speed, unsupervised operation.
