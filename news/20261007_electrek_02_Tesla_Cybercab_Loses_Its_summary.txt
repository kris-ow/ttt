A Tesla Cybercab crashed in Philadelphia this week, losing its entire left door panel, raising questions about both the vehicle's build quality and whether it was operating without proper permits.

## Robotaxi / Autonomous Driving
1. **Crash details and cause unknown:** A gold Cybercab was photographed stopped on South Broad Street in Philadelphia with its entire driver-side door skin missing, exposing the inner frame, window regulator, and wiring; a Reddit user (ryephila) who posted the photos did not witness the crash but described it as a low-speed incident. The article states it is unknown who was at fault, whether Tesla's software was driving, or whether there were injuries; a second car stopped behind the Cybercab may or may not have been involved.

2. **Panel design explains the damage:** The Cybercab's body panels are unpainted molded plastic with color mixed into the material, attached via fasteners accessible from inside — a design Tesla's Lars Moravy and Franz von Holzhausen reportedly explained to Sandy Munro last year. The article notes this design means a door skin coming off whole is roughly expected behavior, unlike a steel-bodied car which would need bodywork and paint; Tesla could in theory bolt on a new skin, though the article notes likely more significant damage near the rocker trim could complicate repairs.

3. **No Pennsylvania permit for automated operation:** Pennsylvania requires a PennDOT certificate of compliance to operate highly automated vehicles (SAE Level 3+), and Tesla is not among the seven companies on PennDOT's certificate list (Waymo is, for Philadelphia, with a driver on board). The article infers the Cybercab was therefore most likely either hand-driven or running Tesla's software as a Level 2 driver-assist system with a human legally responsible. Gold Cybercabs reportedly began appearing around Philadelphia in mid-September, with several photographed at a Tesla store in Devon per the Philadelphia Inquirer, and Tesla reportedly declined to say what they were for.

4. **Reporting implications:** Under NHTSA's Standing General Order, crashes involving an automated driving system must be reported for property damage alone, while Level 2 driver-assist crashes are only reported if there's a death, hospitalization, airbag deployment, or a struck pedestrian/cyclist. The article notes that if reported, this crash would join what it describes as a record 207 Autopilot/FSD crashes Tesla reported in a single month earlier this year, with redacted incident descriptions as is standard for Tesla's entries.

## Editorial Stance
The author withholds judgment on fault, noting the photos alone cannot establish whether Tesla's software was involved. On the panel failure, the author presents two competing interpretations as unresolved: the clean break could reflect smart design (a cheap swappable skin absorbing impact instead of requiring a body shop) or could indicate a panel that detaches too easily; the author states the available photos cannot settle which is true.
