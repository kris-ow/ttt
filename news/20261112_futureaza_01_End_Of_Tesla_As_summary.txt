Two commentators (the host, Brian, and guest Herbert) discuss Tesla's evolving product roadmap following the discontinuation of the Model S/X and the announced cancellation of the Cybertruck, arguing Tesla is pivoting away from human-driven personal vehicles toward a robotaxi-centric lineup built on the Cybercab platform.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Model S/X and Cybertruck wind-down:** The hosts note Model S and X production has ended with final deliveries at a special event, and Elon Musk has said Cybertruck production will continue until around September of next year before ending. They see this as closing out Tesla's "legacy" human-driven vehicle lineup, alongside the removal of a mysterious human-driven pickup variant from the roadmap (believed to be a Cybercab-platform vehicle with a driver).
2. **Model Y L reception:** Herbert views the Model Y L (the Premium Launch Edition variant, distinct from the standard Model Y shown on some roadmap graphics) as a more significant product than Tesla is presenting it as, predicting strong sales once pricing drops — he expects a price point in China near the Model Y Performance level (around the mid-$50,000s, by his estimate) to drive significant volume, while a price near $50,000 would be too low to sustain profitably in his view.
3. **Cybertruck pricing/cost structure:** The hosts reject the idea that Tesla could simply relaunch a Cybertruck at its original roughly $39,000 price point, noting inflation alone would push that to roughly $58,000 today, and that the actual low-end "mid-range" Cybertruck trim that shipped was priced near what should have been a mid-tier price while being sold below cost, with the exoskeleton design failing to deliver the cost, weight, and complexity savings Tesla had hoped for. Herbert calls it "less successful than the Edsel."
4. **Rural/pickup truck market gap:** The hosts acknowledge Tesla now lacks any pickup truck offering for buyers (e.g., rural America) who need that form factor, but argue Tesla's strategic answer will be a cargo/truck version of its robotaxi-based transport lineup rather than a traditional consumer pickup.

## Robotaxi
1. **Cybercab platform as Tesla's future focus:** Both hosts argue Tesla, per statements from Lars Moravy, is shifting focus entirely to a robotaxi-based lineup, with the Cybercab's underlying platform architecture extendable into multiple form factors, including a "Robovan" and new truck/van variants to fill gaps left by discontinued human-driven models.
2. **Debate over why Cybertruck space/batteries were cited:** The hosts dispute Tesla's stated rationale that factory space is needed for Cybercab production, questioning whether battery supply (4680 cells, partly produced for Model Y in Berlin and Texas) was truly a constraining factor, concluding the real reason for cancellation was simply weak Cybertruck sales rather than resource competition with Cybercab.
3. **Future vehicle interior concepts — "robomobile":** Herbert speculates Tesla's next robotaxi-platform vehicles may need larger, more flexible interiors than the current two-seat Cybercab — citing a London-cab or Zoox-style layout with opposing bench seats, screens, and bed-like convertibility — to serve a wider range of trip types (work, sleep, entertainment) beyond short urban rides, rather than Tesla needing a separate large van model purely for passenger capacity (he notes larger people-mover EVs like the Kia EV9 and Cadillac Escalade IQ sell in low volumes).
4. **Flexible rental/ownership model theory:** The hosts, crediting a theory from Dillon Loomis, describe a possible future Tesla model where customers could pay per-trip, per-week, per-month, or per-year for robotaxi vehicle access, with the ability to swap form factors (e.g., a "Robotruck" for moving weekends) — framing this as Tesla's answer to the loss of dedicated vehicle categories like the Cybertruck.

## Market & Competition
1. **Comparison to Ford's discontinued sedan lineup:** Herbert draws a parallel to Ford's historical phase-out of its passenger sedan lineup (Escort, Tempo, Taurus, Crown Victoria), leaving only the Mustang, and notes Rivian has never sold a traditional passenger car, only trucks and SUVs — used as precedent for automakers narrowing focus to profitable or strategic segments.
2. **Second-line Cybertruck/Cybercab factory space debate:** The hosts reference a social-media analysis (attributed to "Jen Ma") arguing that an existing production line is already capable of producing roughly a million vehicles a year, so added factory floor space would be for a much larger-scale buildout (potentially millions of units) rather than simply replacing Cybertruck capacity — with that commentator estimating such an additional line couldn't come online until roughly 2028–2029, a point the hosts say remains unconfirmed by Tesla.
