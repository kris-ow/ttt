FutureAzA host Brian and guest Randy Kirk evaluate three financial media narratives about Tesla's investment thesis, pushing back on legacy automaker recommendations as alternatives to Tesla and defending the long-term bull case. The video's core argument is that mainstream financial journalists are missing Tesla's biggest growth stories — particularly energy storage and Optimus — and that critics dismissing Tesla's valuation fail to account for the scale of these businesses. The hosts also touch on near-term energy deployment dynamics and speculate about Cortex compute capacity being made available for rent.

## Market & Competition
1. **Legacy Automakers Pitched as Tesla Alternatives:** A 24/7 Wall Street article recommends General Motors, Ford, and Stellantis as "value-driven automotive stalwarts" for retirees instead of Tesla, citing Tesla's trailing price-to-earnings ratio of 373x as unsustainable. Brian and Randy reject the framing, arguing that while GM and Ford are generating near-term cash flow and buybacks, their dependence on internal combustion engines makes them poor long-term bets, as most of the world is expected to restrict or ban ICE vehicles within 5 to 10 years.

2. **Ford and GM Short-Term Metrics vs. Long-Term Risk:** Ford is noted to have produced 11.4% margins and 30% year-over-year growth in software subscriptions, while GM is buying back stock and raising guidance. Randy gives GM's Mary Barra partial credit for delivering shareholder value recently but calls both companies "dead men walking" when evaluated against a forward-looking lens. Randy also highlights Ford's chronic recall problem — described as roughly 12 recalls for every 5 at Tesla — as a fundamental operational failure.

3. **Stellantis Called the Weakest of the Group:** Stellantis is described as trading at $7.35, down 32% year-to-date, with a price-to-book ratio of 0.32. Randy calls it potentially the worst major automotive company in the world right now. Brian criticizes Stellantis's EV strategy in the US — launching large vehicles like the Charger EV and Wagoneer S when neither product resonated with buyers — and notes the Charger EV has been discontinued. The hosts acknowledge Stellantis has more compelling EV offerings in Europe but says that hasn't translated to the US market.

4. **Rivian and Lucid as Cautionary Tales:** Randy briefly notes he would want to see at least two profitable quarters before considering Rivian. Brian references a video he made roughly four years ago (circa mid-2022) warning that Lucid insiders were deeply concerned about the company's survival — a prediction that he argues was vindicated, noting that an investor who bought on the day that video published would be down 97%, with the company only surviving due to an unforeseen injection of billions from the Saudi PIF.

5. **Wall Street Bets Sentiment on Tesla:** The article notes Reddit's Wall Street Bets community has posted sentiment scores as high as 88 (very bullish) on Tesla, driven by robotaxi, Optimus, and China deal chatter. Randy interprets this positively, arguing younger retail investors who are bullish on robotaxi "get it" in a way that traditional financial media does not.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Optimus TAM Too Large for Open AI Competition to Matter:** The video addresses a claim that Tesla stock dropped over 4% on a Monday due to a partnership between Unitree and Figure, framed as new competition from Open AI. Randy calls this "the most ridiculous reason" and argues institutional investors aren't even pricing Optimus into Tesla's valuation yet. He contends the total addressable market for functional humanoid robots is so large that even if Open AI enters the space, it will be irrelevant — Tesla will be the largest producer by a significant margin and likely producing the best product.

2. **Tesla's Optimus Production Capacity Trajectory:** Brian notes Tesla is already building capacity to produce 1 million Optimus units, with a longer-term target of 10 million, and emphasizes that Tesla's ability to construct factories quickly gives it a structural advantage over any new entrant trying to scale humanoid robot manufacturing.

## Energy
1. **Megapack Deployment Timing and Revenue Recognition Complexity:** Randy explains that Megapack revenue is recognized not when units leave the factory or arrive at a site, but when they are electrified and paid in full (or substantially completed to the point where only permitting remains). He calls the Q1 2026 energy deployment figure of 8.8 GWh a "low lump" and expects Q2 2026 to see a significant rebound — with Grok projecting a minimum of 16–17 GWh and a potential range of 21–23 GWh or higher if deferred deployments from prior quarters are counted.

2. **Cortex Compute Capacity May Be Available for Rent:** Randy raises what he describes as underreported news: Tesla's Cortex facility — designed for real-world AI training rather than standard LLM workloads — likely has surplus capacity that may be offered for rent. He contrasts this with Colossus, which was mentioned in an S1 filing update on the day of recording and is more suited to typical LLM use cases. Randy suggests this could become a meaningful revenue stream for Tesla, drawing an analogy to Amazon Web Services' success in renting out cloud and AI compute.

## Financials
1. **Tesla's Historical Stock Return and Current Valuation:** The article cited notes Tesla shares have risen 22,250% over 15 years, meaning a $4,500 investment in May 2011 would be worth $1 million today. Tesla's current market cap is stated at $1.4 trillion, with a trailing P/E of 373x. Brian and Randy push back on the implied conclusion that the stock can't repeat strong performance, with Randy suggesting most Tesla bulls expect an 8–10x return over the next 10 years, meaning a $100,000 investment today could reach $1 million.

2. **Q1 2026 Automotive Revenue and Inventory:** The article notes Tesla posted a 16% year-over-year gain in automotive revenue in Q1 but that revenue was 19% below the same period in 2023. Deliveries were up 6%. Inventory rose from what Brian characterizes as "historic lows" — which he describes as unsustainable — to a higher but still industry-low level measured in days of supply. Brian frames the inventory build as strategically sensible given oil price volatility.
