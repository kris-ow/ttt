&nbsp;
Let s start with a basic timeline. In&nbsp;
January, Eivissa on X reported that&nbsp;&nbsp;
Tesla was introducing a European Model&nbsp;
Y with a new battery pack code YB8LR,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which people in the Tesla community have been&nbsp;
referring to as the 8L battery pack for short.
&nbsp;
Eivissa has proven to be a reliable source&nbsp;
of information in the past because it sources&nbsp;&nbsp;
battery pack information from European regulatory&nbsp;
filings. The account is worth a follow if you re&nbsp;&nbsp;
on X and interested in batteries.
3 days after Eivissa made this post,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Tesla confirmed that they were in fact launching&nbsp;
a new version of the Model Y called the Standard&nbsp;&nbsp;
Long Range Rear Wheel Drive, which matched&nbsp;
the details that Eivissa provided.
&nbsp;
Then, later in January, Tesla confirmed in the Q4&nbsp;
earnings call that they were starting to use the&nbsp;&nbsp;
4680 in non-structural battery packs, which is the&nbsp;
type of battery pack that Tesla currently uses in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Model Y. That, combined with the regulatory&nbsp;
information led Eivissa to the conclusion&nbsp;&nbsp;
that the 8L battery pack must be using the same&nbsp;
Generation 2 Cybercells used in the Cybertruck.
&nbsp;
That was mostly confirmed in April in Tesla&nbsp;
s Q1 earnings call where they said that in&nbsp;&nbsp;
Berlin, they started launching a Model Y&nbsp;
battery pack with their in house 4680.
&nbsp;
The part that was unclear was whether&nbsp;
the 4680 cell used in that pack is the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Cybercell or the new, fully dry version&nbsp;
of the cell. More on that later.
&nbsp;
Next, in late February and early March,&nbsp;
reviews of the Model Y that uses the 8L&nbsp;&nbsp;
battery pack started showing up online.
Before we get into those, it s worth noting&nbsp;&nbsp;
that there appears to be two versions of the Model&nbsp;
Y that have the 8L battery pack. The first is of&nbsp;&nbsp;
course the one that Tesla advertised in January.&nbsp;
But that version was seemingly discontinued in&nbsp;&nbsp;
early spring and replaced with the Premium Long&nbsp;
Range Rear Wheel Drive Model Y with an 8L battery&nbsp;&nbsp;
pack. That is, the battery pack appears to be&nbsp;
the same, they just upgraded other aspects of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the trim, like the entertainment system.
I have to say sometime in early spring here&nbsp;&nbsp;
because the Wayback Internet Archive failed to&nbsp;
capture those changes on Tesla s webpages.
&nbsp;
Either way, that trim change&nbsp;
complicates the story in two ways:
&nbsp;
First, as far as I m aware, Tesla could change&nbsp;
the version of the 4680 cell used in the battery&nbsp;&nbsp;
pack and still keep the pack designation number&nbsp;
8L, which could affect the charging test results.&nbsp;&nbsp;
I don t think that s what happened, but it&nbsp;
s worth keeping in mind as a possibility.
&nbsp;
Second, there were several different ranges listed&nbsp;
for those vehicles during that time period which,&nbsp;&nbsp;
adjusting for wheel and tire options, ranged&nbsp;
anywhere from 657 to 609 kilometres of WLTP&nbsp;&nbsp;
range. I m not sure what drove those changes,&nbsp;
but I don t think it s relevant because we&nbsp;&nbsp;
ve seen changes like that in the past with no&nbsp;
corresponding changes to the underlying specs&nbsp;&nbsp;
and real world performance of the vehicle.
With that in mind, let s take a look at the&nbsp;&nbsp;
specs of the 8L battery pack, and then two&nbsp;
sets of real world charging test results.
&nbsp;
First, just like the original 4680 battery&nbsp;
pack produced in Austin that was deployed&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the Model Y in 2022 with the Gen 1&nbsp;
cell, it appears to have 828 cells.
&nbsp;
Second, while the energy capacity of the Gen&nbsp;
1 4680 pack had only 67.5 kWh useable and&nbsp;&nbsp;
71.6 kWh total, the new version has&nbsp;
74 kWh useable and 79 kWh total.
&nbsp;
Third, the Gen 1 pack weighed 452 kilograms&nbsp;
and the new version weighs 447 kilograms.
&nbsp;
Fourth, that led to a pack level energy density&nbsp;
increase from 149 Wh/kg useable and 158 Wh/kg&nbsp;&nbsp;
total to 166 Wh/kg useable and 177 Wh/kg total.&nbsp;
Roughly an 11-12% increase in energy density.
&nbsp;
That s in line with some of the battery packs&nbsp;
that Tesla currently uses in their vehicles,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but not quite up to scratch with the best&nbsp;
and recently upgraded 2170 battery packs from&nbsp;&nbsp;
Panasonic and LG Chem, which reach about&nbsp;
177 Wh/kg useable and 188 Wh/kg total.
&nbsp;
That is, in terms of energy density, the 8L&nbsp;
battery is a step down from the best performing&nbsp;&nbsp;
packs, with around a 6% energy capacity and&nbsp;
therefore 6% range decrease, but it s still&nbsp;&nbsp;
a solid performer and globally competitive.
As for the energy density of the cells themselves,&nbsp;&nbsp;
if the 8L pack is 79kWh and contains 828 cells,&nbsp;
that means the cells are 95.4 watt hours each.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The cells in the Cybertruck were measured at 95.2&nbsp;
watt hours which, given variances in cells and&nbsp;&nbsp;
testing conditions means the cells are effectively&nbsp;
identical in terms of energy density.
&nbsp;
With all that in mind, let s move on to&nbsp;
the sticking point and the number one&nbsp;&nbsp;
criticism from commentators on the&nbsp;
8L battery pack charging speed.
&nbsp;
The most common video cited with regards&nbsp;
to charging speed was by Elektro Ronny,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which was posted on the 24th of February. Ronny&nbsp;
saw an abysmal peak charge rate of 130 kW,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is about half the advertised 250&nbsp;
kW charging rate of the Model Y.
&nbsp;
Due to comments encouraging Ronny to do the&nbsp;
test again with a better protocol, he posted&nbsp;&nbsp;
another test about a week later with a properly&nbsp;
preconditioned battery. However, the charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
test started at 31% state of charge rather than&nbsp;
the less than 10% state of charge that would&nbsp;&nbsp;
be required to see the peak charging rate.
Despite that, in this test, the battery still&nbsp;&nbsp;
performed better than in the first test, and&nbsp;
achieved a 155 kilowatt charge rate at a 32%&nbsp;&nbsp;
state of charge. 155 kilowatts at 32% state&nbsp;
of charge isn t as good as most Model 3 and&nbsp;&nbsp;
Model Y vehicles, but it s much better&nbsp;
than the first version of the 4680.
&nbsp;
So which Model Y 8L vehicle was this - the&nbsp;
Standard trim that was originally advertised&nbsp;&nbsp;
or the Premium that subsequently replaced it?
It wasn t clear to me so I watched some of Ronny&nbsp;&nbsp;
s other videos and it appears to be the standard,&nbsp;
which makes sense given the original video was&nbsp;&nbsp;
posted in late February and the premium trim&nbsp;
supposedly didn t show up until March.
&nbsp;
The next charging test was done&nbsp;
by Fabrizio, who runs the YouTube&nbsp;&nbsp;
and X accounts called Lampi di Tesla.
Fabrizio is an automotive content creator who&nbsp;&nbsp;
s been very active in the Tesla community over the&nbsp;
years and was an FSD beta tester. He was given a&nbsp;&nbsp;
press vehicle to test by Tesla Italy and provided&nbsp;
me with all the receipts for his charging test.
&nbsp;
First, on screen is the registration showing the&nbsp;
YB8LR battery pack along with licence plate number&nbsp;&nbsp;
HC007PY. That licence plate number matches&nbsp;
the one on the vehicle that he tested.
&nbsp;
After picking up the test vehicle at 26% state&nbsp;
of charge, he drove it until the battery drained&nbsp;&nbsp;
to 7%, and pre-conditioned the battery on&nbsp;
the way to the supercharger. The charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
session started on March 1st 2026 at 8:22PM and&nbsp;
the ambient temperature was 13 C, or 55 F.
&nbsp;
He provided me with the full, unedited video,&nbsp;
which I ve accelerated. It shows the charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
power quickly ramping to 250kW at 7% state of&nbsp;
charge and the video closes with the charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
session ending at 63kW at 82% state of charge.&nbsp;
On screen is the 10-80% portion of the 4680 Model&nbsp;&nbsp;
Y charging curve in yellow, plotted against the&nbsp;
Cybertruck in red, and as you can see the lines&nbsp;&nbsp;
had a similar slope. But, the Cybertruck pack is&nbsp;
obviously much larger so the power is higher.
&nbsp;
So, Fabrizio did exactly what I would have done&nbsp;
and reformulated the graph, normalizing for&nbsp;&nbsp;
battery pack capacity by using the c-rate instead&nbsp;
of total charging power. And, as you can see, the&nbsp;&nbsp;
two lines have a surprisingly similar fingerprint,&nbsp;
indicating the two battery packs likely use the&nbsp;&nbsp;
same battery cell. More on that in moment.
So how does the 8L 4680 battery pack compare to&nbsp;&nbsp;
other Tesla battery packs? Fabrizio provided me&nbsp;
with a spreadsheet that compared the 8L battery&nbsp;&nbsp;
pack to three other battery packs used by&nbsp;
the Model Y in Europe: Two 2170 battery&nbsp;&nbsp;
packs from LG and a CATL LFP battery pack.
I went ahead and also roughly plotted in Elektro&nbsp;&nbsp;
Ronny s tests and the result was illuminating:
First, Ronny s first charge test in red&nbsp;&nbsp;
plateaus early, as if the battery hasn t been&nbsp;
preconditioned, but then later in the charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
session tightly aligns with the test that Fabrizio&nbsp;
did in yellow. That is, it appears to be the same&nbsp;&nbsp;
battery pack with different testing setups.
Second, Ronny s second charge test in orange&nbsp;&nbsp;
overshoots the other 4680 charge tests in&nbsp;
yellow and red as if it s playing catch-up,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which it is. That s because the charging&nbsp;
session started at 32% instead of 10% and it&nbsp;&nbsp;
was properly pre-conditioned. So the battery has&nbsp;
more thermal headroom to play with, for example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and can charge more aggressively because it s only&nbsp;
getting a partial charge. Thanks to that, for that&nbsp;&nbsp;
brief charging session, it performs roughly the&nbsp;
same as the LG battery packs in purple and blue.
&nbsp;
Third, Fabrizio s full charge test of the 8L&nbsp;
battery pack that was properly preconditioned&nbsp;&nbsp;
performs much better than Ronny s first full&nbsp;
charge test that likely wasn t preconditioned.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It didn t charge as fast as the LG battery&nbsp;
packs, but it in my view it s within the&nbsp;&nbsp;
acceptable limits for most EV buyers.
Some people might argue that it should&nbsp;&nbsp;
perform much better, but that usually&nbsp;
comes down to two core arguments:
&nbsp;
First, Tesla vehicles are falling behind other&nbsp;
vehicles in charging performance, so Tesla needs&nbsp;&nbsp;
to up its game with the 4680. However, I ve found&nbsp;
that argument really comes down to a matter of&nbsp;&nbsp;
projecting personal preference rather than fact.&nbsp;
That is, there s a small group of people that are&nbsp;&nbsp;
passionate about faster charge rates and they&nbsp;
assume it s a top priority for everyone. But,&nbsp;&nbsp;
that s not the case. Most EV buyers place greater&nbsp;
emphasis on things like value for money, safety,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the reliability of the charging network, and&nbsp;
connectivity which Tesla s vehicles excel at.
&nbsp;
As a side note, it s also worth noting that thanks&nbsp;
to their efficiency, Tesla s are still some of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
best EV s on the market when it comes to miles&nbsp;
of range added per minute instead of brute force&nbsp;&nbsp;
charging rate, which is what actually matters.
I do think Tesla will increase their charge&nbsp;&nbsp;
rates over time so that they continue&nbsp;
to compare favourably to other EVs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it s better to sell the best value for money&nbsp;
vehicle rather than the best charging vehicle.
&nbsp;
Second, some people expected the 4680 to&nbsp;
perform much better because they say that&nbsp;&nbsp;
s something Tesla promised for the 4680 with&nbsp;
the tabless electrode, which they didn t.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The reason Tesla put the Tabless electrode in&nbsp;
the 4680 was to a) allow the cell to be made&nbsp;&nbsp;
larger with the same thermal characteristics&nbsp;
and charging speed, and b) to reduce costs&nbsp;&nbsp;
by simplifying the manufacturing process.
Before I close out the video, if Fabrizio s&nbsp;&nbsp;
information was useful to you, follow him on&nbsp;
YouTube and X. It certainly was to me and it&nbsp;&nbsp;

&nbsp;
In summary, the 8L 4680 battery pack&nbsp;
used in Model Y s in Europe appears to&nbsp;&nbsp;
have a much better charging rate than&nbsp;
many commentators are indicating.
&nbsp;
As far as I can tell, the 4680 cells that are used&nbsp;
in that battery pack are the same as what s used&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the Cybertruck because they have the same fast&nbsp;
charging fingerprint and the same energy density.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It s not the fastest charging battery cell on&nbsp;
the market, but it s a good all-around performer,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and will improve because it s still only the&nbsp;
2nd version of Tesla s 4680 battery cell.
&nbsp;
Based on the charging curve data, it looks&nbsp;
like that cell was used in the battery packs&nbsp;&nbsp;
tested by both Ronny and Fabrizio.&nbsp;
So charging methodology is likely the&nbsp;&nbsp;
key difference between their results.
Yes, there is a slight chance Ronny and&nbsp;&nbsp;
Fabrizio s vehicles used a different software&nbsp;
version or a different battery cell, like the&nbsp;&nbsp;
new fully dry 4680 cell instead of the Cybercell,&nbsp;
but those explanations seem much less likely.
