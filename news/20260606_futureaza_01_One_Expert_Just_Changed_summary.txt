This video from FutureAzA features host Brian interviewing Luca Greco, described as a leading independent expert on giga casting technology, in a deep-dive conversation on the history, mechanics, and global adoption of large structural die casting in the automotive industry. The core thesis is that giga casting — pioneered by Tesla — is rapidly becoming a global manufacturing standard, with China leading aggressive adoption and Western OEMs beginning to follow. A second part covering the future of casting technology is teased for the following day.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Tesla's Giga Casting Pioneer Status and Alloy Innovation:** Tesla was among the first automakers to use large structural die castings in passenger vehicles, initially deploying a two-piece rear casting on the Model Y before consolidating it into a single large piece within roughly a year. Tesla uses 6,000-ton machines for its Model Y castings, while the Chinese industry standard has moved to 9,000-ton machines to apply greater pressure and compensate for metal shrinkage during cooling. Luca Greco notes that Tesla achieves comparable results with less clamping force due to proprietary engineering in their alloy and process design — described as a "secret recipe." Tesla has also iterated its aluminum alloy multiple times to accommodate higher impurity content, enabling on-site recycling of scrap aluminum (including wheels and stamping offcuts) back into the casting process, with in-house alloying happening in large on-site melting furnaces at Giga Texas.

2. **Cybercab Casting Design — Simpler, Skeletal Structure:** Greco highlights that the latest rear casting iteration seen on the Cybercab represents a notable design shift toward a skeletal structural frame, eliminating large flat areas that add weight without contributing structural integrity. This approach is described as feasible specifically for the Cybercab because it is not a conventional consumer vehicle — the design priority is simplicity and reduction of manufacturing steps rather than meeting the same consumer-facing requirements as the Model Y. Greco explicitly states this skeletal casting approach could not be directly applied to the Model Y.

3. **Break-Even Volume for Giga Casting Is Lower Than Expected:** Greco states that common estimates suggest giga casting becomes economically viable at around 50,000 units per year, but his assessment puts the real break-even closer to approximately 10,000 castings per year. For companies like Tesla or Rivian with few models, the practical break-even when spreading machine costs across a fleet is around 80,000–100,000 castings per year. Chinese OEMs with many models often use the same casting die across multiple vehicle platforms — in some cases up to eight or nine models on a single machine — which dramatically reduces per-unit tooling cost and makes retooling faster (approximately one day to swap dies) compared to reprogramming and retooling hundreds of robots in a traditional stamped-steel body shop.

## Market & Competition

1. **Global Giga Casting Adoption — US and Japan Lagging:** In the United States, Ford has publicly announced plans to use front and rear giga castings (which they call "uni-castings") in their upcoming universal EV platform. Honda installed five 6,000-ton machines in Ohio intended for battery tray production using friction stir welding, but subsequently cancelled those EV plans. In Japan, Nissan has announced intentions to introduce giga casting by 2027, though Greco notes there has been relative silence from them recently. Toyota had planned to introduce giga casting alongside its "unbox" manufacturing process, and its first Lexus EV — which was to feature the technology — was cancelled just days before the interview was recorded. However, Greco believes Toyota's Shanghai factory is still proceeding with machine installation and that Toyota remains committed to giga casting directionally.

2. **Europe: Volvo Leading Western OEMs:** Volvo is described as the most advanced European OEM in giga casting adoption. The company began production in 2025 at its historic Torslanda factory in Sweden using two 8,000-ton machines, making it the first European OEM to reach production-level giga casting. Volvo was already investing in the technology as early as 2022. Buyers of the Volvo EX60 receive a vehicle with European-made giga castings. Volvo is also expanding to a new factory in Slovakia where 9,000-ton machines will be installed, with the Polestar 7 announced as the first vehicle for that facility.

3. **China's Overwhelming Giga Casting Dominance and Oversupply:** Virtually all Chinese OEMs are currently implementing or imminently planning giga casting, with Great Wall Motors and a few startups cited as notable exceptions — and even Great Wall faced public pushback from industry peers on Chinese social media for skepticism toward the technology. The more striking dynamic is a significant oversupply of giga casting machines in China: suppliers invested billions in equipment that now sits unused, with demand unlikely to absorb the excess capacity for at least the next five years. Greco suggests Western OEMs could potentially acquire brand-new, fully commissioned but unused machines from Chinese suppliers at a significant discount.

4. **Magnesium Castings — China's Next Frontier:** China controls over 95% of global magnesium refining, creating a strategic dependency for Western industries. Historically, Chinese vehicles contained less than 1 kg of magnesium while Western vehicles used 5–15 kg, but China is now rapidly increasing magnesium content driven by a state directive targeting 45 kg of magnesium per vehicle by 2030. New applications include magnesium electric motor shells, which are approximately 30% lighter than aluminum equivalents. The growth in smaller castings across Chinese vehicles is partially driven by this magnesium push, as magnesium cannot be stamped like aluminum sheet and must be cast. Safety concerns around magnesium — particularly fire risk when fully molten magnesium contacts oxygen — remain a significant deterrent for foundries, though semi-solid processing techniques like thixomolding mitigate this risk.
