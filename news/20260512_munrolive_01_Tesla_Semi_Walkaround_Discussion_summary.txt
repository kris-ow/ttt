This video features a walkthrough and technical discussion of the Tesla Semi at the ACT Expo, conducted by Munro Live with Dan Priestley, Tesla's Director of Engineering for the Semi truck. The conversation covers the engineering philosophy behind the Semi's design — particularly aerodynamics, drivetrain architecture, battery integration, and total cost of ownership — and how Tesla's experience from its passenger car lineup informed the Semi's development. The overarching thesis is that Tesla's integrated, efficiency-first approach gives the Semi a meaningful structural and economic advantage over both traditional diesel trucks and competing electric heavy-duty vehicles.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Aerodynamics as the primary efficiency lever:** Dan Priestley explains that aerodynamic drag accounts for approximately 50% of the energy required to move the truck down the road. Tesla's approach to minimizing drag compounds into significant savings: less drag means less battery needed, which means less mass, which in turn requires even less structural support — a cascading efficiency benefit. This philosophy drove decisions from the ground up, borrowing lessons from Tesla's passenger car side, including smooth underbody treatment, careful study of airflow around wheels and tires, and extensive wind tunnel testing.

2. **Hard-mounted cab eliminates gap-related drag:** Unlike traditional trucks where the cab is independently suspended and creates large gaps relative to the chassis — gaps that are aerodynamically costly — the Tesla Semi uses a hard-mounted cab integrated directly with the frame structure. Priestley explains this was made possible in part because the electric drivetrain eliminates the longitudinal driveshaft found in diesel trucks, which causes the whole chassis to twist under torque. By treating the battery structure and chassis as a unified system — analogous to a unibody — Tesla was able to lock down the integrated "skateboard" (chassis, drivetrain, battery, front subframe) and eliminate the need for cab compliance, enabling a cleaner aerodynamic envelope.

3. **Two variants targeting different operational needs:** The long-range variant offers 500 miles on a single charge and can haul a 45,000 lb payload with a typical drive van, achieving both time parity and payload parity with diesel operations. Charging is designed to occur during mandatory driver rest periods (per hours-of-service regulations), meaning no dedicated charge downtime. The standard-range variant is positioned for operators who are more mass-sensitive or don't require the extended range, offering a lighter and potentially more cost-effective option.

4. **Dual e-axle drivetrain architecture:** Rather than a longitudinal driveshaft, the Semi uses directly mounted motors on the drive axles. The mid (fourth) axle is the "torque axle," equipped with two independently driven motors — one per wheel — optimized for high-power events like takeoffs and steep-grade overtaking. The rear axle is the "efficiency axle." The two axles are geared differently but share many common components including oil pumps, rotor/stator assemblies, and inverters. At highway cruise speeds, the torque axle disconnects entirely — wheels continue to spin freely with no internal windage losses — delivering the traction benefits of a 6x4 configuration with the efficiency of a 6x2.

5. **Cross-platform component sharing from Tesla's broader lineup:** Priestley highlights significant parts commonality across Tesla's vehicle family as a key enabler of reliability and cost control. The drive inverter and stator are shared with the Cybertruck. The HVAC compressor is shared with the Cybercab. Light modules are shared from other Tesla vehicles. Screens are also carried over. This commonization reduces bespoke component costs and leverages proven reliability from high-volume production.

6. **Total cost of ownership as the central design ethos:** Priestley frames TCO as the unifying objective that benefits all three key stakeholders: the driver, the fleet owner, and the shipper. For drivers, Tesla has developed an in-house seat architecture and focused on comfort. For fleet managers, the emphasis is on day-in, day-out reliability. For shippers, the benefit is reduced freight costs through lower energy throughput. Priestley stresses that the product must work for all three simultaneously to be commercially viable.

7. **Charging infrastructure buildout underway:** Tesla currently has approximately 50 charging sites in various stages of development for the Semi, with some expected to open within the next month or two (from the time of filming). Priestley states that Tesla's philosophy is that every truck delivered must have a charging solution already identified and available on day one — charging deployment must match truck delivery. Tesla is also now selling charging hardware directly online: a V4 fast charger with 1.2 megawatt capability and a base charger rated at 125 kilowatts, available for fleet operators or third parties building out charging infrastructure. The broader Tesla Supercharger team has deployed more than 80,000 DC fast charging posts worldwide, providing institutional expertise in utility coordination and rapid deployment.
