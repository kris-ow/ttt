This video is a presentation by Larry Paul, a lifelong EV enthusiast and early EV adopter, tracing the history of the modern electric vehicle from GM's participation in the 1987 World Solar Challenge through the California ZEV mandate era (1996–2003) and the post-mandate "dark period," up to recent developments. The core thesis is that the modern EV era was made possible by a combination of California regulation, a passionate grassroots EV community, key entrepreneurs, and foundational technology developed during the GM EV1 era — and that GM's deliberate destruction of its own EV leadership was a cautionary tale.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **GM's Solar Racing Origin and the Impact Concept:** The modern EV traces its roots to GM's 1987 World Solar Challenge victory in Australia with the Sunraer, built in collaboration with Aero Environment in just 10 months. The Sunraer finished nearly two days ahead of its second-place competitor, the Ford Sunchaser. This achievement inspired the GM Impact concept car, announced by GM Chairman Roger Smith, which could accelerate from 0–60 mph in 8 seconds and was described as the first production-oriented EV in 10 years. The Impact proved that a fully functional, mass-producible EV was achievable, prompting California's Air Resources Board (CARB) to establish the Zero Emission Vehicle (ZEV) mandate.

2. **The ZEV Mandate and the MOA Era Fleet:** CARB's ZEV program culminated in a binding Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) with the six major automakers, requiring EVs on California roads by 1998, with a target of 800,000 EVs on California roads by 2010. Between 1996 and 2003, all six manufacturers produced thousands of EVs. Nearly 5,000 fully capable EVs were manufactured during this era, including the GM EV1, the Chevy S10 Electric, the Honda EV Plus (~300 built, with about 10 going to New York for cold-weather testing), the Ford Ranger EV (1,312 units, fleet-only), the Dodge/Plymouth EPIC vans (the first vehicles to use DC fast charging), the Toyota RAV4 EV (949 fleet units 1997–2001, then 339 public-sale units 2000–2003), and the Nissan Hypermini/Ultra EV (130 units, the only MOA-era car powered by lithium-ion batteries).

3. **The EV1's Unique Engineering:** The EV1 was the only MOA-era vehicle purpose-built from the ground up as an EV platform, sharing no major parts with other GM vehicles. It achieved a drag coefficient of 0.195 — still the record for the most aerodynamic production car — and offered up to 140+ miles of range. GM also developed variants in its R&D lab including a series hybrid using a turbine engine connected to a generator, a parallel hybrid with a diesel engine powering the rear wheels, and compressed natural gas and fuel cell versions. In March 1994, a slightly modified GM Impact set a land speed record for electric vehicles at 183 mph on a test track.

4. **Inadequate and Counterproductive Marketing:** The EV1 and other MOA-era EVs suffered from strikingly poor or even discouraging marketing. GM's EV1 commercials used imagery of post-nuclear apocalypse, power outages, and "rise of the machines" themes, conveying fear rather than desire. Toyota's RAV4 EV ad explicitly stated the car would not impress co-workers or fix a receding hairline, framing it as a dutiful sacrifice rather than a desirable product. Paul argues the marketing actively discouraged rather than attracted buyers.

5. **Charging Infrastructure Challenges:** Four incompatible charging standards existed during the MOA era, making public charging unreliable. Most vehicles charged at roughly 20 miles of range per hour of charging (Level 2), suitable for urban use but impractical for road trips. DC fast charging standards (CCS1 and CHAdeMO) were not established until 2011. In a remarkable exception, in May 1998, EV1 driver Chris Trexler completed a nearly 3,000-mile road trip from Los Angeles to GM's Detroit headquarters over three weeks, driving ~60 miles at a time and stopping for three-hour Level 2 charging sessions — with no public charging available along the route.

6. **Destruction of the EV Fleet and Community Resistance:** Following litigation led by General Motors, CARB in January 2003 amended the ZEV mandate to eliminate the battery EV requirement, shifting focus to hybrids and fuel cells. All six manufacturers subsequently repossessed and destroyed their lease EVs. The last EV1s were crushed after being gathered at the former GM Van Nuys plant and transported to Mesa, Arizona. The EV community staged high-profile protests including a public "funeral for the EV." In early 2005, 78 EV1s were discovered at a GM training facility in Burbank; a 24-hour vigil began February 16, 2005, accompanied by a $1.9 million no-liability purchase offer, but GM rejected it and transported the cars for destruction. Two activists, Alexandra Paul and Colette Divine, were arrested on March 14, 2005, attempting to block the removal trucks.

7. **Ford Ranger EV Salvation and the RAV4 EV "Don't Crush" Movement:** In contrast to GM, Ford reversed its destruction policy after a week-long sit-in at a Sacramento dealership beginning January 14, 2005, led by Heather Bernoff and David Ravoy. Ford offered to sell remaining Ranger EVs to their lease drivers for $1 each, saving approximately 400 trucks. Toyota also partially reversed course due to the "Don't Crush" movement led by Linda Nichols, saving an estimated 200–220 RAV4 EVs from destruction. Paul and his wife are the original owners of one of 101 RAV4 EVs ever sold new to the public.

8. **AC Propulsion and the T0 — Foundation of the Tesla Roadster:** AC Propulsion, founded in 1992 by Alan Kakonei, Wally Repel, and Paul Krosa, developed AC motor and controller technology foundational to the modern EV. Their T0 sports car achieved 0–60 mph in 3.6 seconds, over 300 miles of range, and outperformed Ferraris and Lamborghinis on the drag strip. It used 6,800 lithium-ion cells (100 modules of 68 cells each), recharged in 3 hours, and featured a single drive shaft spinning at up to 13,000 RPM. Tom Hanks was a primary investor in AC Propulsion and took delivery of the first EBox in 2008. Paul credits the T0 as the single most influential vehicle that inspired Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning to found Tesla in 2003, and set the stage for Elon Musk to participate in Tesla's first funding round in February 2004.

9. **EV1 VIN 212 Restoration — Breaking News:** On October 31, 2025, EV1 VIN 212 was legally purchased at an Atlanta, Georgia impound lot auction after being declared abandoned following damage at Clark Atlanta University. It sold for over $100,000 and is the only EV1 now legally and privately owned. Billy Caruso and his team are meticulously restoring it, with a target completion date of November 14, 2026 — the 30th anniversary of the first EV1 customer delivery. GM, in a reversal of its historical stance, actively supported the restoration by authorizing a team to harvest parts from a donor EV1, including a rare brake control system and a replacement windshield. The restoration is documented on YouTube under the channel "Questionable Garage" (searching "V212").

10. **Five Factors Enabling the Modern EV Era:** Paul identifies five pillars that made today's EV era possible: (1) the federal Clean Air Act of 1970 granting California authority to set stricter emissions standards; (2) the passionate grassroots EV community that fought for these vehicles; (3) Plug In America, formed in August 2005; (4) the documentary *Who Killed the Electric Car?*, released June 28, 2006, written and directed by former EV1 driver Chris Payne, which inspired tens of thousands to write Congress demanding EVs; and (5) entrepreneurs, particularly AC Propulsion and the founders of Tesla.
