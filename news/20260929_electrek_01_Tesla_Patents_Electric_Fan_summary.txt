Tesla was granted a patent for an "Electric Fan Car" system using four electric ducted fans in the rear diffuser to generate downforce, drawn on a Model S, a model Tesla stopped building in April. The article ties this to a forthcoming Roadster demo and other recent aero-related patents.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Patent details:** The USPTO granted Tesla patent US 12,746,987 B1, filed in January 2024 with a provisional application dating to January 17, 2023, listing eight inventors mostly based near Tesla's Hawthorne design studio. The design routes air from an underbody inlet between the rear wheels through a duct to four axial fans in the rear diffuser, separated by three vertical strakes, creating a "suction region" (vacuum) under the floor; a second version adds two smaller side inlets to reduce backwash that Tesla says otherwise "wastes power." Tesla's filing argues the fans can produce downforce independent of vehicle speed, unlike passive diffusers, and the fans draw power from the vehicle's main high-voltage battery pack with no separate motor, activated via a button or automatically at certain speeds.
2. **Drawn on a discontinued model, pointing to the Roadster:** The patent drawings depict a Model S, a car Tesla ended production of in April as the Fremont line shifts toward Optimus, leading the article to conclude the design's only realistic future application is the Roadster. This follows a patent reported last week for a hidden active rear wing (up to 700 kg of downforce) drawn on a car resembling the new Roadster, and an August 2025 Tesla patent for underbody fans with deployable skirts — together suggesting, per the article, a track aero package aimed at the Roadster.
3. **Context — fan cars aren't new:** The article notes historical precedents (Chaparral 2J in 1970, Brabham BT46B in 1978, Gordon Murray's T.50) and highlights McMurtry, whose electric Spéirling set the Goodwood hill climb record in June 2022 and produces 2,000 kg of downforce at a standstill using skirts and battery-powered fans; McMurtry began production of a $1.36 million Spéirling PURE earlier this year. Tesla's design differs by avoiding skirts, packaging fans into a normal-looking diffuser.

## Editorial Stance
The author (Fred Lambert) argues the patent is a neat packaging idea but not novel given McMurtry's existing production fan car, and is skeptical Tesla's skirt-less design can approach McMurtry's downforce levels. He views the pattern of recent fan and wing patents as evidence Tesla has built a genuine track aero toolkit for the Roadster, but cautions that patents often don't translate into shipped products, noting the Roadster was unveiled in 2017, promised for 2020, and is now slated for "2027 or 2028" with a demo pushed to October 15; he says he'd prefer real aero hardware over a "hover" thruster stunt.
