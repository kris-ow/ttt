This video features FutureAzA host Brian in conversation with guest John ("Dr. Know It All"), a tech commentator with ~95,000 YouTube subscribers, discussing Tesla's emerging strategy to control its entire AI compute stack — from data centers and chip design to in-house fabrication via Terafab. The core thesis is that Tesla's vertical integration across training infrastructure (Cortex), chip development (AI5), and a proposed domestic chip fab (Terafab, in partnership with Intel) positions it uniquely against competitors who rely on fragmented, globally distributed supply chains. The hosts also assess rivals like Rivian and the broader compute demand picture driven by humanoid robots.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **Cortex 1 and 2: Tesla's AI training backbone in Austin:** John explains that Cortex 1 in Austin is continuously crunching FSD training data to improve the self-driving stack, with the challenge growing harder as the system approaches perfection — the marginal improvement becomes increasingly difficult to identify and train. Cortex 2 is described as already partially operational, and the hosts believe it will serve primarily as "Optimus University," the training ground for Tesla's humanoid robot. Both systems ingest real-world disengagement data from Tesla drivers as well as synthetic simulation data, allowing Tesla to generate and run one-in-a-billion edge-case scenarios (e.g., a moose crossing a road in Los Angeles) repeatedly until the system handles them correctly.

2. **Rivian's FSD ambitions assessed skeptically:** The hosts discuss Rivian's claim that it will have a Full Self-Driving-level capability on the road by the end of 2027 (i.e., "next year" relative to the video's publish date of 2026-06-21). John characterizes Rivian's current driver-assistance system as roughly equivalent to Tesla's Autopilot from four or five years ago — capable of lane-keeping, following a lead vehicle, and highway lane changes, but unable to navigate off-highway, stop at traffic signals, or turn. John says he "seriously doubts" Rivian can close that gap in a year, citing insufficient training data (far fewer cars on the road than Tesla), and no apparent large-scale compute infrastructure comparable to Cortex. He notes that if Rivian were renting compute, it would be ironic if they turned to xAI, which reportedly has spare capacity.

3. **The scope of the self-driving challenge:** John emphasizes that the gap between basic highway Autopilot-style capability and genuinely safe Full Self-Driving is enormous and took Tesla itself years to traverse — during which time even Tesla insiders believed full autonomy was "right around the corner." He cites the famous Waymo example from roughly ten years ago of a woman on a mobility scooter chasing ducks — a scenario not in Waymo's simulator — as evidence that edge cases are effectively infinite. He references Elon Musk's framing that FSD must be able to handle even a UFO crashing in the road.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Cortex 2 as Optimus training infrastructure:** The hosts characterize Cortex 2 as the likely dedicated compute cluster for training Optimus, Tesla's humanoid robot. John states that both FSD and Optimus are being actively trained on the Cortex systems, making these data centers central to both the autonomous driving and robotics programs simultaneously.

2. **Humanoid robots as a driver of infinite chip demand:** John argues that if the humanoid robot market scales as broadly predicted, chip demand will be effectively infinite for "decades and decades." This framing supports his view that Tesla's Terafab initiative is not merely a competitive chess move against TSMC or Samsung — it is a genuine necessity, because even if existing foundries maximize output and build new factories, the world will still need more chips.

## Market & Competition

1. **Terafab: Tesla's vertical chip fabrication strategy:** The hosts discuss Tesla's announced plan to build Terafab, a domestic chip fabrication facility in Texas, designed to handle the entire chip lifecycle in one location — from raw materials ingestion through wafer fabrication, etching, testing, and packaging. John frames this as a deliberate shortcircuiting of the traditional model (e.g., Nvidia designing chips, shipping designs to TSMC in Taiwan, packaging elsewhere), which requires at least a year of iteration cycles. By co-locating everything, Tesla aims to dramatically accelerate chip design iteration. Brian notes that SpaceX, which has reportedly raised an additional $85 billion, is sharing costs with Tesla on this initiative.

2. **Intel partnership as the "missing piece":** Brian describes his initial extreme skepticism about Tesla building cutting-edge sub-5-nanometer chips, noting that China has spent roughly half a trillion dollars and still has not caught up to the frontier. His skepticism shifted when the Intel partnership was announced. John elaborates that Intel is pursuing a non-ASML, non-EUV lithography approach targeting one-to-two nanometer process nodes — a "Hail Mary" that, if successful, could be transformative. He acknowledges Tesla is "in bed" with Intel on this because next-generation chips like AI5 require advanced process nodes for energy efficiency and transistor density. In the near term, John notes that Samsung and TSMC will still be building Tesla's chips while Terafab is constructed — and he is skeptical the fab will be operational in anything close to one year, even if the physical building could be completed that quickly.

3. **Dojo's cautionary tale for chip iteration speed:** Brian briefly references Dojo as an example of what happens when chip development moves too slowly — older chips become stale and lose competitive relevance before they are deployed at scale. This underpins the urgency behind Terafab's fast-iteration design philosophy.

4. **Supply chain derisking as the strategic rationale:** The hosts draw a parallel to Tesla's 4680 battery strategy, citing a reference to a video by Jordan Gasey arguing that the 4680's real value is supply chain derisking rather than pure performance gain. The argument: if China were to become commercially or politically unavailable as a supplier, Tesla would be the only Western automaker still capable of operating. The same logic applies to chips — vertical integration via Terafab is framed not as a pure performance bet but as an insurance policy.
