Recorded on July 1, 2026 (Canada Day), this episode of the Cern Basher podcast features host Cern Basher and guest Phil Beisel reviewing the past two weeks of Tesla, SpaceX, and broader AI/tech developments. The central thesis is that Elon Musk is systematically building a vertically integrated "full stack" — spanning FSD software, robotaxi operations, Optimus manufacturing, chip fabrication (Terafab), orbital compute (Star Mine), and wireless spectrum (Starlink/Echostar) — and that this positions him to win the AI race not just at the model level but at the infrastructure and inference-at-scale level. The hosts argue that the coming wave of embodied AI and autonomous deployment will be far larger and more disruptive than most observers appreciate, and that a Tesla–SpaceX merger is increasingly logical given the deepening operational and financial entanglement between the two companies.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus production line opens at Fremont:** Elon shared an image walking the Optimus production line at the Fremont factory, confirming that the Model S/X line has been cleared from that space to make room. Lars Moravy mentioned in a separate interview with Herbert and Jeff Lutz that one line has been assembled at Fremont for Optimus. A Tesla post cited by the hosts (attributed to Tesla Zoa) indicates that Optimus is being built and validated in Germany with approximately 40 sublines to be constructed. A Tesla video released roughly one to two months prior depicted 40 lines with multiple stations for producing various robot sub-components; that same video stated 12,000 human jobs will be created in the process.

2. **Manufacturing philosophy — more like a car than a phone:** Lars Moravy pushed back on the criticism that Tesla lacks consumer-product manufacturing expertise, arguing that building Optimus is far more analogous to building a car than a cell phone, given the three-dimensional nature of assembly and the safety requirements. The hosts agreed, noting that Optimus — like a car — has a large number of moving parts and dense electronics, whereas a phone is largely static. Phil Beisel expects Optimus manufacturing to begin human-in-the-loop, with automation introduced progressively as processes are understood.

3. **Scale trajectory — Fremont as prototype, Texas as mass production:** The hosts characterize Fremont as effectively a prototype factory regardless of unit count, with initial thousands of units likely absorbed internally by Tesla and SpaceX before reaching external customers. The hosts believe a 10-million-unit annualized production factory in Texas is the eventual destination. Early Optimus units are expected to be deployed first in manufacturing, then in service industries (retail, restaurants), and eventually in the home — though the hosts note that home deployment faces unique consumer-acceptance and safety hurdles.

4. **Societal integration — novelty to normalcy:** The hosts discuss a two-stage transition: first, the novelty phase where Optimus in public will be a head-turning spectacle (analogous to early Waymo reactions in San Francisco), and second, a normalization phase where robots are simply background fixtures of daily life. They also flag an anticipated period of public misbehavior toward Optimus units, noting that the robots are expected to be trained not to fight back or self-protect, drawing parallels to people harassing Waymo vehicles in San Francisco.

## Robotaxi

1. **Austin unsupervised expansion imminent:** Phil Beisel states with conviction that Austin unsupervised robotaxi operation is close, acknowledging he had previously (and incorrectly) predicted a May–June 2026 launch. He cites the growing number of Cybercabs observed running in Austin, testing on public roads without a driver, steering wheel, or pedals, and what he interprets as a manufacturing build-up — including images of Cybercabs accumulating in parking lots — as evidence of an imminent fleet release. He estimates that within two weeks to two months, Tesla will announce a fleet of approximately 150 cars operating as a commercial service in Austin.

2. **Lars Moravy: Cybercab production ahead of expectations:** The hosts reference Lars Moravy's interview with Herbert and Jeff Lutz in which Lars indicated that Cybercab production is going better than he expected, while also emphasizing that high volumes must be produced simply to learn how to produce at high volumes — a point the hosts interpret as Tesla making many more Cybercabs than the publicly known service numbers suggest.

3. **Operational complexity is the hidden bottleneck:** Phil Beisel argues that the broader investment community and media are overly focused on FSD/AI as the gating factor for robotaxi scale, when in fact a large set of non-AI operational challenges — customer service systems, fleet maintenance (tire changes, cleaning), navigation edge cases, and incident response automation — are equally or more constraining. He draws an analogy: if Waymo handles N incidents per day at its fleet size, Tesla cannot simply hire a proportionally larger call center when running 10x the fleet; the entire operational loop must itself be autonomous. He believes this is what is occupying significant Tesla engineering bandwidth beyond the FSD team.

4. **Cybercab spotted near Silicon Valley suburbs:** The host (Cern Basher) recounts personally seeing a Cybercab driving on Yerba Buena Road in a remote southeast corner of San Jose — approximately one mile from his home and roughly 20 minutes outside of Palo Alto — describing it as a striking surprise even for someone deeply familiar with the vehicle. Testing on public roads without driver controls is confirmed by both hosts.

5. **Fleet size context:** The hosts reference Lars Moravy having commented in the Herbert/Jeff Lutz interview that roughly 150 Cybercabs may be in existence (Herbert's estimate), with the hosts speculating the true production number could be substantially higher (e.g., 450+), reflecting manufacturing learning rather than service deployment intent alone.

6. **Mixed fleet model — Cybercab plus Model Y:** The hosts note that a functioning robotaxi network will still require a significant proportion of Model Y vehicles (perhaps ~20% of fleet) to accommodate rides with more than two passengers, and that a larger Model Y rollout alongside Cybercabs has not yet been publicly visible.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD V14 "light" for Hardware 3 — an impressive feat with caveats:** Phil Beisel characterizes the delivery of a supervised FSD experience on Hardware 3 as a remarkable engineering achievement, given that Hardware 3 has roughly one-eighth the memory bandwidth of Hardware 4, and Hardware 4 is three to five times faster for overall FSD workloads. The higher-resolution cameras on Hardware 4 add further capability gaps. He believes this effort stems partly from honoring Elon Musk's prior commitments to Hardware 3 owners (estimated 3–4 million vehicles), partly from a genuine engineering drive to understand system constraints, and that the optimization work likely benefited from pipeline work already done in a prior FSD version (referenced as an intermediate release in the v14.x series that involved a full-stack rewrite of data processing and movement). He does not expect Hardware 3 vehicles to ever achieve unsupervised FSD.

2. **Constraint-driven innovation:** Phil Beisel frames the Hardware 3 FSD work as exemplifying a broader engineering philosophy: working within severe constraints forces teams to deeply understand where compute cycles and data-movement bandwidth are actually spent, producing insights that benefit the broader FSD stack. He draws an analogy to biological analysis — observing a creature in granular detail reveals optimization opportunities invisible at a higher level.

3. **FSD breadth is a stretch on engineering resources:** Phil Beisel expresses concern — flagged as his personal opinion without internal data — that the FSD team may be spread thin across an unusually large number of simultaneous workstreams: supervised FSD on Hardware 3 and Hardware 4, FSD in Europe, left- and right-hand drive variants, Tesla Semi FSD, Cybertruck-specific features (e.g., summon), unsupervised FSD for robotaxi, and all associated operational systems. He notes it has been approximately one year since the robotaxi launch, which he interprets as evidence of more problems than were anticipated.

4. **Katy, Texas crash — media misreporting:** A Tesla crash in Katy, Texas prompted investigations from NHTSA and NTSB and was widely reported with implications of FSD involvement. Elon Musk responded publicly that FSD drives slowly through neighborhood streets and this was a high-speed crash. Phil Beisel elaborates that data showed FSD was manually overridden — the accelerator was pressed to 100% and the vehicle reached 73 mph — making driver fault clear. The hosts criticize media reporting as sensationalized and biased, while also noting an inherent irony: as FSD improves and human interventions become rarer, drivers become less prepared to intervene correctly when they do attempt to take control.

5. **"Full self-hearing" — in-factory audio diagnostics:** Lars Moravy described in the Herbert/Jeff Lutz interview a system (jokingly named "full self-hearing" by Lars) that uses the car's built-in microphones during end-of-line production testing to detect and localize rattles and other anomalies via AI analysis. The hosts note this technology has potential consumer-facing applications (e.g., helping service centers diagnose rattles without relying on driver descriptions) and fleet applications (e.g., autonomous vehicles self-diagnosing in the absence of a consistent human occupant).

## Energy

1. **Major European Megapack order — 25 GWh, phase one of 100+ GWh program:** Tesla secured a deal in Europe for 25 gigawatt-hours of Megapack energy storage systems, described as the first phase of a broader program targeting over 100 gigawatt-hours in total. For context, the hosts note Tesla sold nearly 47 gigawatt-hours of Megapack globally in 2025, making the 100 GWh target more than twice that figure. The deal also includes Autobidder trading software with long-term revenue warranties — the hosts flag this as a notable reemergence of a product Tesla has not publicly discussed in some time, describing it as AI-powered software to optimize Megapack dispatch and maximize revenue from grid arbitrage.

2. **Powerwall lease program in Massachusetts and Connecticut:** Tesla launched a discounted Powerwall lease program in Massachusetts and Connecticut tied to virtual power plant participation.

3. **Sunrun/Renew Home virtual power plant:** Tesla announced a partnership with Sunrun and Renew Home to aggregate home batteries, smart thermostats, and other distributed devices into what they describe as the largest distributed virtual power plant in the US, targeting more than 16 gigawatts of flexible capacity for hyperscalers, data centers, and utilities. The hosts flag this as flying under the radar despite being a significant strategic move.

4. **Megapod trademark — supercharger sites as distributed AI compute nodes:** Tesla filed a trademark application for "Megapod." The hosts theorize this refers to deploying Megapack-backed AI compute clusters at Tesla Supercharger sites across the US (noting approximately 7 gigawatt-hours of provisioned capacity at Supercharger locations). The concept is to use overprovisioned grid power at underutilized Supercharger sites to run inference workloads — potentially leased to third-party AI consumers similar to how Anthropic leases cloud compute — with Megapacks buffering cheap off-peak power for use during peak compute demand. The hosts emphasize this is inference compute, not training.

## Market & Competition

1. **Elon exercises options early:** Elon Musk exercised stock options ahead of an August deadline. The hosts note this has fueled speculation about a potential Tesla–SpaceX merger timeline but decline to add further detail.

2. **Tesla–SpaceX merger logic is growing:** The hosts make an extended case that a Tesla–SpaceX merger is increasingly inevitable, driven by: (a) deepening cross-company engineering collaboration (Lars Moravy on a first-name basis with SpaceX counterparts, shared materials and hardware between Starship and Cybertruck programs); (b) the impracticality of managing related-party transactions at scale (e.g., SpaceX purchasing millions of Optimus units, or Tesla using Grok in its vehicles, would generate taxable inter-company revenue); (c) Elon's desire for frictionless decision-making without independent board approval of cross-entity deals; and (d) the fact that Tesla — not SpaceX — hired the new director of the Terafab project (an 18-year Intel semiconductor manufacturing veteran), signaling Tesla's material stake in what might superficially appear to be a SpaceX project.

3. **The "Beisel Curve" — AI compute supply-demand gap:** Phil Beisel presents a proprietary chart (logarithmic scale, described as the "Beisel Curve") showing AI compute demand growing at approximately 3.4x per year since 2017, compared to the prior CPU-era growth rate of roughly 60% per year. Even after applying an efficiency discount (reflecting algorithmic and hardware improvements), the adjusted demand curve vastly outstrips the projected supply from existing fabs (primarily TSMC, with Samsung and smaller players). Terafab, if it achieves one terawatt of compute output and comes online around 2030 (which the hosts consider aggressive), makes a meaningful but still insufficient dent. The hosts argue this gap will drive compute prices significantly higher, benefiting Nvidia, OpenAI, Anthropic, and especially SpaceX as Cursor's new owner. Tim Cook and Apple are cited as a cautionary tale: Apple's late and weak AI investments caused a blind spot to GPU demand, resulting in Apple raising MacBook prices by $100–$300 due to memory cost pressure.

4. **Waymo competition framing:** The hosts use Waymo as a benchmark for robotaxi incident rates and operational scale, arguing Tesla must architect its entire operational loop — not just the vehicle AI — to be autonomous in order to run a fleet an order of magnitude larger than Waymo's without proportionally scaling human support staff.

5. **SpaceX Starfall — potential military application:** On June 23, 2026, SpaceX launched "Starfall," described as a temporary satellite capable of staying in orbit from one hour to a couple of weeks before returning to Earth via parachute (similar to Dragon). The hosts identify three potential use cases: rapid cargo delivery anywhere on Earth, in-orbit manufacturing or experiments with return capability, and future retrieval of cargo from other space objects. Both hosts believe the primary driver is military, though they acknowledge this is speculation.

6. **SpaceX / Echostar spectrum deal — direct-to-cell testing underway:** Last summer, SpaceX completed a deal with Echostar worth approximately $19 billion (paid in a mix of cash and SpaceX stock) to acquire wireless spectrum. Echostar received 261.8 million SpaceX shares, worth approximately $45 billion at current prices. The deal has cleared regulatory hurdles but is not scheduled to close financially until November 2027 unless SpaceX accelerates payments. A letter dated June 15, 2026 confirmed SpaceX is conducting interim period testing of next-generation direct-to-cell satellites using the acquired Echostar spectrum. Phil Beisel believes this testing likely involves prototype handset hardware built by SpaceX to validate antenna design and signal performance — and that this (rather than a consumer phone launch) is the most likely explanation for the Wall Street Journal's report that Elon Musk is developing a new AI device using Qualcomm Snapdragon chips, a report Elon publicly called "utterly false."

7. **AI device / "Starphone" speculation:** The hosts decline to fully dismiss the Wall Street Journal report about an Elon Musk AI phone, with Phil Beisel arguing that large organizations routinely build prototypes of adjacent hardware, and that a Snapdragon-based prototype device would be a logical tool for testing Starlink direct-to-cell capabilities. He sketches a longer-term vision of an AI-native device (no app ecosystem, pure information delivery via on-device and Starlink-offloaded inference through Star Mine) that could disrupt both carriers (Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile) and handset makers (Apple). He notes SpaceX's acquisition of Cursor AI as further evidence of an integrated AI stack ambition.

8. **Starlink in New Zealand — 27% rural broadband market share:** Elon Musk highlighted that Starlink has become New Zealand's largest rural internet provider, with 27% of the rural broadband market (up from 19% the prior year), 85,000 total connections (72,000 rural residential fixed), and download speeds averaging 226 Mbps — approximately four times faster than competing services. New Zealand now has the highest satellite broadband uptake per capita in the developed world.

9. **SpaceX IPO float and lockup schedule:** Per an analysis by Dillon Loomis, SpaceX's current public float is approximately 4.9% of total shares. Key lockup release milestones: ~20% of an early employee/investor block releases approximately two days after Q2 earnings (with an additional conditional 10% if the stock closes above $175.50 on more than five of ten days, bringing the potential float to ~15%); further tranches release between August 20 and October 24, 2026, bringing cumulative float to ~27–28%; Q3 earnings in November trigger another ~28% release (the largest single date); the 180-day lockup ends December 8, 2026, at which point ~40% of shares will be tradable. Elon Musk's shares carry a 366-day lockup — the longest of any individual — with his block not beginning to release until mid-2027. Full float (~100%) is expected by August 2027. NASDAQ-100 inclusion is scheduled for July 6, 2026; S&P 500 inclusion is expected mid-2027 or later, potentially coinciding with a Tesla–SpaceX merger.

10. **Terafab land acquisition near Austin:** Tesla (or an affiliated entity) has reportedly acquired approximately 2,700 acres of land east/northeast of Austin — comparable in size to the current Giga Texas footprint — as a likely site for Terafab. The hosts expect further land purchases. Separately, they reference a prior Elon Musk slide showing plans for 11 million square feet of manufacturing space near Bastrop, Texas (roughly 15–20 minutes from Giga Texas) for AI1 satellites (Star Mine).
