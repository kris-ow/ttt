This video from FutureAzA features host Brian in conversation with casting expert Luca Greco, exploring the current state and future trajectory of giga casting technology in the automotive industry. The core thesis is that Tesla's in-house, vertically integrated casting process gives it a durable competitive advantage through rapid iteration, process control, and continuous improvement — advantages that suppliers and most Western OEMs cannot easily replicate.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Tesla's casting strategy evolved away from mega-machines:** Tesla originally ordered a casting machine in the 15,000–16,000 ton range to produce a full underbody in one piece (front section, battery tray, and rear), but cancelled the order. This was widely misreported as Tesla "retreating" on giga casting. In reality, Tesla pivoted to its "unbox" manufacturing process, which uses smaller castings producible on 6,000-ton machines or smaller. The structural battery pack made a single-piece full underbody redundant, as adding a second structure through the middle of the car would have introduced complexity, new tolerances, and higher scrap rates.

2. **Front casting removal from Model Y was contextual, not a reversal:** The front underbody casting was never fully rolled out across Giga Texas, Giga Berlin, or Giga Shanghai — it remained on a separate line and was dependent on the structural 4680 battery pack. In Giga Berlin, it was tied to BYD blade pack integration, which was cancelled (possibly over battery longevity concerns). In Giga Texas, cell supply was redirected toward the Cybertruck. Additionally, thousands of robots had already been installed for a traditional front underbody, and the existing front casting design was suboptimal — reportedly weighing around 85 kg, at least 10 kg heavier than ideal per Munro Live's assessment. Importantly, both the Cybertruck and the Cybercab do use front underbody castings, confirming the technology remains valid.

3. **Vertical integration is Tesla's most underrated casting advantage:** Lars Moravi told Munro Live at the Cybertruck delivery event that Tesla's casting engineers, body-in-white engineers, and die designers all work together in the same room daily. This level of integration enables rapid iteration impossible with a supplier relationship. A key example is conformal cooling — 3D-printed inserts placed inside dies with channels that mirror the casting's shape — which dramatically improved thermal control and reduced cycle times from over 170 seconds down to 80 seconds or less. Giga Berlin recently released a video discussing this. Suppliers lack the financial capacity and incentive to pursue such innovations on behalf of OEMs.

4. **Chinese OEMs are adopting a hybrid model:** Rather than fully outsourcing casting or investing in full in-house capability, some Chinese OEMs are inviting die casting suppliers to set up operations inside their factories or industrial parks. This allows the OEM to benefit from rapid iteration and supplier expertise simultaneously. One unnamed Chinese supplier is reportedly running a machine at over 200,000 parts per year — believed to exceed Tesla's Fremont peak output. Chinese company Chery (a state-owned OEM) is also noted for using a 5,000-ton machine to produce a rear underbody casting with strong structural properties, though they also continue using traditional 9,000-ton supplier machines.

5. **Near-term casting improvements will be incremental and mostly invisible:** Over the next one to five years, visible changes to castings are unlikely. Improvements will focus on yield rates, material properties, reduced scrap, weight reduction, and lower machining cycle times. The most exciting frontier is magnesium castings, which could reduce weight by approximately 18–20 kg compared to aluminum equivalents (e.g., replacing Tesla's rear casting). Aluminum-magnesium alloys (~60% aluminum, 40% magnesium) are being studied in China to balance weight savings with corrosion resistance and flammability concerns during die casting. No consumer vehicle applications exist yet. Tesla is not expected to pursue magnesium castings in the near term.

6. **Western OEMs and newer entrants face structural limits:** Ford, Volvo, and Volkswagen are highlighted as capable of reaching good casting technology levels, partly by following Tesla's lead; Volkswagen is doing interesting work on battery trays. Rivian's R2 integrates multiple rear underbody castings sourced from suppliers — a pragmatic strategy given the investment required for in-house capability. Lucid similarly uses castings from suppliers. Lucid's plan to build a factory in Saudi Arabia is flagged as logistically difficult given the absence of a local supply chain. The broader point is that supplier-dependent OEMs cannot achieve the rapid iteration that defines Tesla's manufacturing edge.

## Market & Competition

1. **Large casting applications may have reached their practical ceiling:** Beyond battery trays (largely a China-only development) and C-pillar castings on large SUVs (seen from Huawei, NIO, and others), Luca Greco sees few new compelling large-casting applications on the horizon. The benefits of consolidation — fewer parts, fewer process steps — diminish as castings grow beyond a certain size, and integration challenges begin to outweigh savings.

2. **China leads on large and exotic casting innovation:** All current large-scale casting applications, including battery tray castings, are being developed in China. The largest casting ever made — the one-piece full underbody originally conceived by Tesla — was produced in China approximately two years before the video's publication. Multi-material innovations (plastic injection molding combined with aluminum die casting in a single shot, from a 5,000-ton machine) have been announced and built in China, though no consumer product output has been confirmed.
