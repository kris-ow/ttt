Electrek reports that a fatal December 2025 Alabama crash involving a 2021 Tesla Model Y — initially treated by state troopers as a routine single-vehicle wreck — was confirmed via Tesla's own NHTSA filing to have had a driver-assist system engaged at the time, though Tesla redacted key details explaining what happened.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Crash details:** On December 7, 2025, around 7:40 p.m., a 2021 Tesla Model Y Long Range driven by Kayleigh Page, 29, left Alabama Highway 157 near Battleground, struck two trees, and caught fire. Page died at the scene; her 7-month-old daughter survived after a passerby pulled her from the burning car. State troopers' reports made no mention of Autopilot or FSD.
2. **Tesla's NHTSA filing:** Under NHTSA's Standing General Order, Tesla filed a crash report identifying the vehicle as a 2021 Model Y with automation "Engagement Status" marked "Verified Engaged," a pre-crash speed of 55 mph, and a fatality logged. Tesla redacted the narrative, software version, and whether the road was within the system's approved operating area as "confidential business information" — redactions the article says Tesla applies to 99.9% of its reports. The article states it cannot determine whether basic Autopilot or FSD was engaged because Tesla redacted the software version.
3. **Matching methodology:** Electrek says it matched the NHTSA record to this crash based on unredacted location (Vinemont/Morgan County), state, vehicle type, fatality, and month, plus Tesla's logged incident time (~8:09 p.m. local) falling within the trooper-reported window (roughly 7:40–8:46 p.m.), calling it the only fatal Tesla crash in that area that month. Electrek says it has asked the Alabama Law Enforcement Agency for the crash report and whether investigators received Tesla's raw telemetry or only Tesla's interpretation of it.
4. **Broader pattern claim:** The article states that, per its own analysis of the full NHTSA dataset, road/lane departure is the single largest disclosed pre-crash movement category among Tesla driver-assist crashes, ahead of rear-end and turn scenarios.

## Editorial Stance
The author argues Tesla aggressively markets Level 2 driver-assistance as if it drives itself (citing Musk's suggestion that FSD drivers could look at their phones) while consumer FSD still legally requires supervision, and contends that Tesla's redaction of crash narratives and software versions hides evidence of how the systems behave when they fail, worsening the gap between marketing and reality.
