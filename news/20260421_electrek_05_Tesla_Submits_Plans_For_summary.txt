Tesla has submitted permit applications for two private, fleet-only Supercharger stations in Chandler and Mesa, Arizona — marking the first dedicated charging infrastructure planned specifically for its Robotaxi operations.

## Robotaxi
1. **Arizona fleet charging hubs:** The two filings, discovered in municipal records by permit tracker Marco RP, call for V4 Supercharger stalls that will not be open to the public. The Chandler site on South Roosevelt Avenue is planned for 56 V4 stalls; a second site is filed at 5349 E Main St in Mesa. Both applications were submitted simultaneously last week.

2. **Strategic location — Waymo's home territory:** The Phoenix East Valley is where Waymo launched its autonomous ride-hailing service in 2018. Waymo currently operates roughly 3,000 robotaxis across 10 US cities, completing approximately 500,000 paid rides per week, with a manufacturing hub also in Mesa. Tesla's infrastructure filings in the same region carry a clear competitive signal.

3. **Second attempt at dedicated Robotaxi charging:** In February, Tesla abandoned a planned robo-charging site at 825 Sansome Street in San Francisco — which would have accommodated 150+ vehicles at 600 amps of power — on the day of its planning commission hearing, following Teamsters union opposition.

4. **Current Robotaxi scale remains limited:** Tesla launched service in Dallas and Houston on April 18, expanding beyond Austin for the first time with unsupervised rides, though Houston's service area covers only roughly 25 square miles and the total fleet across all three Texas cities consists of only a few vehicles, most still carrying safety monitors. In Austin, where service launched in June 2025, fewer than 10 truly unsupervised vehicles are operating.

5. **Infrastructure serves Model Y fleet, not Cybercab:** These V4 plug-in stations are designed for Tesla's current Model Y Robotaxi fleet. The purpose-built Cybercab — which rolled its first unit off the production line in February — uses wireless inductive charging and would require separate infrastructure.

## Editorial Stance
The author argues the downside risk is low: if Tesla fails to scale autonomy, the stations can be converted to public Superchargers, making these permit applications a reasonable hedge regardless of the Robotaxi program's outcome. However, the author also notes Tesla has fewer than 10 truly driverless vehicles after nearly a year in Austin, a crash rate NHTSA data suggests is several times worse than human drivers, and a service that remains weather-dependent.
