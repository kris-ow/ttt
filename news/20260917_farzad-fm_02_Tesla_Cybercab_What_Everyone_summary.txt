This is a debate-style podcast between two Tesla-focused commentators (one identified as Dave Lee) about the Cybercab rollout and what it signals about Tesla's broader vehicle strategy. The core disagreement: one host holds a long-standing thesis that Tesla will eventually build a steering-wheel-and-pedal variant on the Cybercab production line to soak up excess capacity if regulation caps robotaxi deployment; the other argues Tesla will never do this, and that removing all driver controls is a deliberate regulatory strategy that clears the way for an entirely new generation of cheaper, steering-wheel-less consumer vehicles.

## Robotaxi
1. **Cybercab public rollout came faster and more low-key than expected:** Both hosts note Tesla's Cybercab reveal event was low-key (no livestream, Elon Musk not present), but within days the robotaxi app was opened to the public in Austin, letting anyone hail a driverless Cybercab — faster than either expected, though light on details about scaling plans.
2. **Production capacity thesis vs. regulatory cap:** One host's thesis is that Tesla's Cybercab factory could eventually produce on the order of millions of units per year, but he cites a figure from Tesla's latest earnings capping production at 125,000 vehicles per year, and argues regulatory/deployment limits (not manufacturing) will be the binding constraint — leading him to predict Tesla will build a steering-wheel, four-seat variant sharing 80–90%+ of parts to keep the line running. He states he believes this "100%."
3. **Counter-thesis: no steering wheel ever, geofencing is the safety strategy:** The other host argues a driver-control variant is a near-impossible ("0%," "less than 1% chance") pivot because removing all driver controls (mirrors, airbags, steering stalk, UI) is what exempts Tesla's cars from a large body of NHTSA driver-control regulations; adding controls back would reclassify the car and reimpose ~hundreds of parts/regulations, effectively requiring a different vehicle. He frames Tesla's tightly controlled geofencing, hours of operation, and vehicle counts as a deliberate strategy to build an airtight safety data case for NHTSA, and predicts most regulatory hurdles clear within roughly 6–9 months to a year.
4. **Cybercab's real purpose framed as setting up next-gen consumer vehicles:** The second host's overall argument is that Cybercab volume/production targets don't matter in the near term — its purpose is to establish safety data and large geofenced areas that pave the way for a "stage two": a broader lineup of steering-wheel-less consumer vehicles (four/six/seven-seaters), potentially priced under $30,000, that Tesla may already be a year into designing (with Tesla's typical car program cited as roughly a three-year development cycle), possibly revealed within about two to two-and-a-half years.
5. **Cybertruck cited as precedent for missed volume targets not being fatal:** Cybertruck's original target of at least 250,000 units, which it hasn't come close to reaching, is cited as evidence that missing a production target doesn't derail Tesla — used to argue Tesla doesn't need Cybercab to hit any specific volume for the strategy to be "working."

## Autonomous Driving
1. **FSD version progression tied to steering-wheel obsolescence argument:** One host argues that as FSD advances from the current version toward future versions (referenced as V15 through V17), rider comfort with having no steering wheel will increase to the point that within roughly three years demand for a steering wheel becomes "ridiculous," with in-car AI (referenced as Grok) handling requests in place of manual control.
2. **Rapid qualitative improvement noted between Model Y robotaxi and Cybercab rides:** Both hosts recall that roughly a year prior, Tesla's Model Y robotaxi in Austin had rough behavior (e.g., slowing awkwardly at speed bumps), whereas their recent Cybercab ride felt near-flawless — used as evidence supporting expectations of continued rapid safety/comfort improvement.
