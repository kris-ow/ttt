West Coast Customs has finished its first electric Chevy Bel Air, and there’s not much Chevy left under the paint. The Burbank shop cut the body off a Tesla Model 3, kept the entire rolling platform, and set a 1955 Bel Air shell on top of it.

The result, called Project B-Zero, looks like a ’55 Chevy from the curb and like a Model 3 as soon as you open the door. And it charges at Tesla Superchargers.

## An old body on a new platform

In the shop’s new video, “The First Ever Electric Bel Air,” founder and CEO Ryan Friedlinghaus explains that a longtime client, Troy Swope, wants to convert an entire car collection to electric and picked the Bel Air to go first. WCC had previously built the same client a Chevelle with a big gas engine.

Friedlinghaus, who drives a Tesla himself, decided against pulling the Model 3’s motors and battery out and fitting them into the old Chevy. Instead, the Tesla stays whole and the Chevy becomes the body kit.

 
 
 

That’s a formula WCC has used for years with gas cars. The shop calls it “old school meets new school”: an old GTO body on a new GTO, an old Camaro on a new Camaro, an old Mustang on a new Mustang. B-Zero is the first time it did it with a Tesla underneath.

“It sounds easy, but that process was tedious,” Friedlinghaus said.

## The wheelbase problem

Before any Chevy sheet metal went on, the crew took the bare Model 3 skateboard for a spin on the streets around the shop, with no doors, no lights and no seatbelts. Friedlinghaus joked about how many safety violations they were racking up. But it drove fine.

The real headache was the wheelbase. A 1955 Chevy rides on a 115-inch wheelbase, while the Model 3 sits at 113.2 inches. On its gas builds, WCC can cut, shorten or lengthen the newer car’s frame to match the old body. You can’t do that with a Tesla, since the floor is the battery pack.

So the shop changed the body instead, stretching the Bel Air’s rear wheel wells so the wheels sit where they look like they belong.

## Bel Air outside, Model 3 inside

The client wanted the exterior to read as a mostly stock Bel Air. The original car was red, so WCC kept it red, with a satin finish and gloss black accents.

Inside, it’s a Model 3. The shop kept the Tesla’s interior, including the steering wheel and center touchscreen, and modified it to fit the Chevy body. The seats got black suede with white stitching in an octagon pattern that Friedlinghaus said was borrowed from the Lamborghini Urus, plus red accents and a “B-Zero” script on the dash.

Friedlinghaus said the goal was for people to see “a really nice Bel Air” from the outside, then open the door and find a Tesla. He also expects some double takes when it pulls into a Supercharger station.

He admits the reaction has been split. Plenty of people are mad that the shop electrified a Bel Air, and plenty of others think it came out great. The client is clearly in the second camp, describing the car as easy to drive and saying every trip in it feels like an event.

## Electrek’s Take

This is sick. I know Tesla doesn’t care about personal car ownership anymore, but they should really sell their platform so shops can do these conversions without using salvage vehicles.

Don’t get me wrong, if you can recycle a salvage, that’s good, but it can’t always be done.

Compare it to the 1966 Mustang that a Sacramento Tesla parts shop converted earlier this year. That project took about $40,000 and two years, with the team grafting sections of a Model 3 floor into the Mustang. It’s an amazing build, but it’s a lot of custom engineering. WCC’s approach skips most of that. You keep Tesla’s crash structure, battery thermal management, software, over-the-air updates and Supercharger access, because you never took the car apart in the first place. It’s closer to what S-Klub did with its Model 3-based Mercedes 300SL than a traditional drivetrain swap.

The purists will hate it. That’s fine. This Bel Air will get driven more in a year than most restored ones get driven in a decade.
