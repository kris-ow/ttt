The video examines why Google's longtime board representative, Don Harrison, resigned from SpaceX's board after roughly a decade, even though Google (Alphabet) holds a large and growing stake in SpaceX. The host argues this is not a sign Google opposes the rumored SpaceX-Tesla merger, but rather a conflict-of-interest cleanup — Google now competes with and does business with SpaceX across AI compute, cloud, satellites, and (via Waymo) robotaxis — ahead of independent committees reviewing the merger.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation
1. **Don Harrison steps down from SpaceX board:** SpaceX filed an 8-K disclosing that Don Harrison — Google's President of Global Partnerships and Corporate Development and a SpaceX director since February 2015 — is stepping down effective November 4, 2026. The filing stated his departure was not due to any disagreement over SpaceX's operations, policies, or practices; both SpaceX's statement and Google's statement to CNBC were warm, and Elon Musk publicly said Harrison "was a great board member" but cited "too many conflicts of interest with Alphabet" as the reason for his resignation.
2. **Google's SpaceX stake and recent buying:** Per a 13F filing, Alphabet added about 3.85 million more SpaceX shares in the third quarter and held roughly 555 million shares worth about $83.7 billion as of September 30 — SpaceX was nearly 95% of Alphabet's reported stock portfolio. At Friday's close the host calculates this stake at roughly $74 billion, up from Google's original ~$900 million investment in 2015 when SpaceX was valued around $12 billion.
3. **Alphabet's unrealized SpaceX loss hit earnings:** Alphabet's CFO reported "other income" of negative $8.2 billion in Q3, mostly attributed to an unrealized (paper) loss on its SpaceX shares, which the host says was a significant factor in Alphabet missing earnings that quarter.
4. **Host's theory on timing tied to merger review:** The host argues Harrison's exit is about removing the most conflicted voice from the room as SpaceX's and Tesla's special committees evaluate a potential merger — since Google, via Waymo, would be the most interested party in how a combined Tesla-SpaceX plans robotaxi competition. He notes Texas's business-court mechanism allowing companies to pre-certify special-committee independence, reducing later litigation risk from directors with outside conflicts.
5. **Remaining SpaceX board skews Musk-aligned:** The host notes that after Harrison's departure, SpaceX's remaining independent directors (Ira Ehrenpreis, Randy Glimp, Steve Jurvetson, Luke Nosek, Rolf Vatter) have long histories with Musk's companies, and cites a Bloomberg report from last month suggesting SpaceX's and Tesla's boards are shifting toward directors loyal to Musk.
6. **Merger process status and odds:** Elon Musk said both companies' boards formed special committees at shareholder request and that he, as an interested party, isn't negotiating; there's no guarantee of a deal. Reuters reported both sides have hired advisers and talks are in early stages; Bloomberg suggested an announcement could come in the first half of next year. A prediction-market figure cited by the host (via a market participant on Polymarket) showed the implied probability of an announcement this year falling from roughly 20% in late October to about 8.5% currently. Tesla's December 3 annual shareholder meeting has no merger vote on the agenda, and Musk has said any merger would need 50% of all outstanding Tesla shares to approve — including non-voting shares.
7. **Lock-up/share-sale speculation (unconfirmed):** A theory circulating (attributed to commentators "Herman and Axe") suggests Google needed to vacate the board seat ahead of SpaceX's post-IPO lock-up expiration on December 8 to freely sell or rebalance shares. The host explicitly flags he could not confirm Google's shares are subject to this lock-up, and that Google's continued buying and stated support cut against the theory; he says this will only be clarified by Google's next 13F filing in February.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Google–SpaceX compute deal creates direct conflict:** SpaceX disclosed in June a deal where Google pays up to $920 million per month (roughly $11 billion/year, a figure the host says Deutsche Bank corroborated) for compute capacity at SpaceX data centers near Memphis, running from October through mid-2029, which Google describes as short-term capacity for Gemini demand. The host argues Harrison effectively sat on both sides of this negotiation as SpaceX director and Google's chief deal-maker.
2. **Anthropic as shared conflict point:** Google has committed up to $40 billion to Anthropic, which the host says is reportedly SpaceX's largest compute customer, leasing the original Colossus facility in Memphis for about $1.25 billion/month — creating another case where Google has financial exposure to a company that is itself SpaceX's major customer.
3. **Pricing dynamics discussed (Chamath/Elon exchange):** A commentator on the All-In podcast suggested SpaceX could terminate its Anthropic compute deal with 90 days' notice if data centers become scarce and re-lease capacity at higher prices; Elon Musk responded publicly that pricing works both ways — if compute becomes cheap and available, SpaceX's revenue could fall — calling it "no tears in the casino" for either side.
4. **Gemini vs. Grok and orbital compute race:** The video frames Google's Gemini and cloud business as a direct competitor to SpaceX/xAI's Grok and SpaceX's own cloud compute ambitions (SpaceX acquired xAI in February per the host). Google's "Suncatcher" project launched a test satellite with four AI chips on October 1 aboard a SpaceX rocket; SpaceX is developing a competing orbital compute initiative, with Elon Musk stating further details would be revealed next year and saying launches could begin in the third quarter of next year.
5. **Satellite-to-phone competition:** Google holds a stake (reported as about 9 million shares) in AST SpaceMobile, a competitor to SpaceX's Starlink direct-to-phone service; SpaceX separately selected Qualcomm to help build a satellite/5G network, per the host.

## Robotaxi / Humanoid Bots
1. **Waymo as the core Tesla-side conflict:** Because Alphabet owns Waymo — Tesla's main robotaxi competitor — the host argues a Google-affiliated director sitting on a merged Tesla-SpaceX board would be structurally conflicted when robotaxi strategy is discussed. He cites his own recent data point that Tesla's Texas vehicle registrations (1,282, including 38 new Model Ys) are now within three of Waymo's Texas registrations (1,285), attributing the underlying data to the Texas AV Tracker team.
2. **Potential Google stake in Tesla robotaxi business via merger:** The host raises a scenario in which, if the rumored SpaceX-Tesla merger occurs in a share-for-share structure (which the host says Barclays expects), Google's existing ~555 million SpaceX shares could convert into shares of the combined entity — giving the company that owns Waymo an eventual (diluted) stake in Tesla's robotaxi business. He stresses this is conditional on the merger happening and on its terms.
3. **Optimus vs. Atlas:** The host notes Google DeepMind's AI is being integrated into Boston Dynamics' Atlas humanoid robot (announced in January, per the host), positioning Atlas as a competitor to Tesla's Optimus — another area of Google-SpaceX/Tesla overlap relevant to board-conflict concerns.
