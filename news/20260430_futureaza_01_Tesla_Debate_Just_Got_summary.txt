This video is a conversational debate between host Brian (Futurazza) and Herbert (from Brighter), addressing widespread investor frustration with Tesla across multiple fronts — FSD/Hardware 3 upgrades, robotaxi delays, the Tesla Semi and Roadster, and AI focus. The core argument is that while delays are real and Elon Musk has repeatedly missed self-imposed milestones, most of these frustrations were already priced into reasonable expectations well before the Q1 2026 earnings call, and the long-term thesis centered on autonomy and robotaxi economics remains intact.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **Hardware 3 upgrade path announced:** Herbert, himself a Hardware 3 owner, describes the Q1 2026 earnings call announcement as positive news for the roughly 200,000 remaining Hardware 3 owners in the US (out of an estimated 400,000 original one-time FSD purchasers, with at least half already having upgraded to Hardware 4 cars). Tesla announced two paths: a discount to trade in and purchase a new car, or a direct hardware upgrade of the existing car — new cameras and a new computer — at what Herbert assumes will be Tesla's expense, given that owners paid $8,000–$15,000 for FSD. Tesla said it would need to create "mini factories" in major cities to perform these upgrades, requiring owners to drop off their vehicles for approximately a week.

2. **V14 Light for Hardware 3:** Herbert states that a software update called "V14 light" is expected to drop in June 2026 or sometime later in Q2 2026. He describes it as covering most driving scenarios from parking to highway, and expresses enthusiasm that it will make the car feel like it's "driving itself." He notes his personal experience using the current FSD version in Los Angeles for a week and finding it "flawless."

3. **Unsupervised FSD — region-by-region rollout:** The hosts clarify that Hardware 3 vehicles will not achieve unsupervised FSD capability under the current hardware. However, Herbert notes that if a Hardware 3 car is upgraded to the new hardware and is located in a geography where unsupervised FSD has been approved, it could qualify — since the unsupervised rollout is expected to be region-by-region.

4. **Tesla's AI and compute investment:** Herbert describes Tesla's AI infrastructure as centered on Cortex (described as the second-largest supercomputer in the world after XAI's Colossus) and the under-construction Cortex 2, which he says will be among the largest supercomputers in the world. He frames Tesla's vision-based AI as the "holy grail" — physics-aware, context-aware, and camera-only — and argues no competitor has replicated it at scale. He also mentions Terafab (a joint Tesla/SpaceX chip project) as a major development, allowing Tesla to produce its own GPUs rather than relying entirely on Nvidia, and notes Elon Musk has spent "every single weekend" for the past seven to eight months focused on the AI5 chip before it was "taped out."

5. **Elon's missed predictions — misled but not lied:** Both hosts agree that Elon Musk has repeatedly set FSD and robotaxi milestones that did not materialize — Herbert calls him "the boy who cried FSD" — but both resist calling it dishonest lying, arguing instead that Musk genuinely believed the timelines and always included verbal caveats. They draw comparisons to Toyota, GM, and Waymo executives who made similarly overconfident predictions between 2012 and 2016 about autonomous driving timelines.

## Robotaxi

1. **Earnings call guidance shift — not material in 2026:** Herbert recounts that on the Q1 2026 earnings call, Elon Musk walked back prior guidance suggesting robotaxi would be "meaningfully impacting the bottom line" in the second half of 2026, instead saying it would not be "super material" by end of 2026 but would be material in 2027. Herbert argues this was not surprising — he had already adjusted his expectations in December 2025 after Tesla missed its stated goal of 50% US market access to robotaxi by end of 2025, and after the doubling-every-month cadence Elon cited in January 2026 did not materialize.

2. **Herbert's personal Cybercab fleet estimate for 2026:** Herbert states his own estimate for Cybercab production/deployment by end of 2026 is approximately 15,000 units — a figure he says he has been communicating for months. He contrasts this with more bullish community estimates of 50,000, and uses it to define what "not super material" means in practical terms.

3. **Robotaxi unit economics framing:** Herbert sketches a high-level contrast between Tesla's current car business — generating approximately $5,000–$7,000 in one-time profit per vehicle sale — versus a robotaxi generating approximately $30,000 per year in revenue, recurring for at least three to five years. He frames this transition as the central investment thesis.

4. **Tesla Semi delayed for autonomy integration:** Both hosts converge on the view that the Tesla Semi has been deliberately delayed not due to incompetence but because Tesla is waiting to redesign it for autonomous (robo-trucking) capability. Herbert argues that FSD for commercial trucking will be priced at a substantially higher commercial rate than consumer FSD — analogous to commercial vs. personal Starlink pricing — making the wait worthwhile. He also notes that battery supply constraints in 2020 would have made a full Semi launch impossible regardless.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **20 million unit target abandoned:** The hosts briefly acknowledge that Tesla's previously stated goal of 20 million vehicles per year is no longer in play, citing it among the list of investor grievances without extensive discussion.

2. **Tesla Semi and Roadster as non-priorities:** Herbert frames both the Semi and the Roadster as products that have been de-prioritized in favor of solving autonomy first. He is explicit that the Roadster "shouldn't be the priority" and that the Semi makes far more strategic sense once robo-trucking is ready.

## Market & Competition

1. **Compute constraints hitting AI competitors:** Herbert notes that Anthropic's Claude (referencing "Opus 4.7") has been "nerfed" and "dumbed down" due to compute constraints, while Tesla and XAI are building Cortex 2, Colossus 2, and Colossus 3 — and eventually plan space-based compute infrastructure through a Tesla/SpaceX collaboration. He argues this trajectory will put Tesla/XAI beyond what any Earth-bound competitor can match in AI compute.

2. **Elon distraction narrative dismissed:** Both hosts push back on media narratives that Elon Musk is distracted by SpaceX, Neurolink, or the Boring Company. Herbert argues his role at Neurolink and the Boring Company is likely advisory (an hour or two per week), that Gwen Shotwell runs SpaceX day-to-day effectively, and that Musk's recent intensive focus has been specifically on Tesla's AI5 chip. Brian jokes that even if "distracted," Musk is still working more hours on Tesla than most CEOs work total.

## Financials

1. **Investor frustration is time-horizon dependent:** Herbert segments the Tesla investor base into distinct groups — recent buyers (weeks to months), options traders who expected a post-earnings pop, and long-term holders — and argues that frustration is rational for those who bought in the past two to three years and are sitting on flat or negative returns, but less so for long-term holders. He frames Tesla as a "startup-like" investment where the valuation is predominantly built on promises of future capability, not current earnings.
