This video from FutureAzA features host Brian and guest Mark discussing the growing trend of U.S. states and now the federal government imposing additional registration fees on EV owners to compensate for lost gas tax revenue. The core argument is that while road funding is a legitimate concern, the proposed flat-fee approach is blunt, potentially unfair, and represents a broader "war on EVs" that disproportionately burdens certain drivers. The hosts analyze the proposed federal Build America 250 Act and Oregon's existing per-mile program as case studies in well-intentioned but poorly executed policy.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **40 States Already Charging Extra EV Fees:** As of the video's recording, 40 states have applied additional registration fees for EV owners on the rationale that EVs do not pay the federal gas tax that funds road infrastructure. The federal gas tax, which has not been adjusted since 1993 and is not indexed to inflation, is charged on a per-gallon basis, while state registration revenue (via license plate stickers) stays entirely at the state level and does not flow back to the federal government.

2. **The Build America 250 Act — Federal Flat Fee Proposal:** U.S. House lawmakers have introduced a bipartisan 5-year surface transportation bill called the Build America 250 Act, which runs over 11,000 pages. Tucked inside as Section 1129 is a provision that would require state DMVs to collect an annual $130 registration fee from EV owners and $35 from some plug-in hybrid owners. The bill is expected to cost more than $500 billion and is being developed ahead of the current transportation law's expiration in September 2026. Tesla and EV industry watcher Sawyer Merritt is noted as viewing the fee as fair, given that road funding must come from somewhere as gas vehicle adoption declines.

3. **Built-In Fee Escalator with a Legislative Cap:** The proposed federal fee is not static. The Federal Highway Administration would be required to increase the registration fees by $5 biennially (every two years). This escalation would continue until hitting a hard legislative cap of $150 per year for EVs and $50 per year for plug-in hybrids. The fees are nominally slated to terminate in 2036, though the hosts are deeply skeptical that any such tax would actually sunset.

4. **Plug-In Hybrid Owners as the "Biggest Losers":** The hosts argue that plug-in hybrid owners — particularly recent adopters who are apartment dwellers and never actually plug in their vehicles — are the most poorly served by a flat fee. These owners derive no meaningful EV benefit and would simply face a higher tax bill with no corresponding offset of gasoline purchases. The flat fee structure also fails to account for the wide disparity between efficient vehicles like a Prius and high-consumption pickup trucks, meaning drivers game the system in opposite directions with similar tax outcomes.

5. **Fairness and Regressive Impact Concerns:** The hosts raise concerns that a flat per-year fee disproportionately burdens lower-income drivers who tend to log more miles because they cannot afford to live near their workplaces. These high-mileage drivers would effectively pay the same flat amount as a wealthy commuter who drives far less, making the fee regressive in practice.

6. **Oregon's Per-Mile Program — A Cautionary Example:** Oregon already operates a voluntary per-mile road use program as an alternative to flat fees. Participants can submit odometer photos periodically or use an OBD-II dongle or GPS device to report mileage. The hosts walk through Oregon's online road use calculator and find that even at very low annual mileage (e.g., 1,200 miles per year), the EV fee still exceeds what the equivalent state fuel tax would have been — and this charge is on top of, not a replacement for, standard vehicle registration. Out-of-state miles can be excluded on an honor system by submitting a form, which the hosts find absurd and easily gamed.

7. **Federal Per-Mile Proposal Also Mentioned:** Separately from the flat fee, the federal government is also exploring a per-mile charge of approximately 1 to 3 cents per mile for EVs. The hosts note this could make EV driving more expensive than gas vehicle driving in some scenarios, and that very fuel-efficient gas vehicle owners (not hybrid owners) might paradoxically come out ahead under such a scheme.

## Market & Competition

1. **Advocacy as the Recommended Response:** The hosts encourage EV owners who oppose these fees to join local EV advocacy groups rather than acting as individuals, arguing that organized groups are far more effective at influencing legislators. They also suggest contacting U.S. House representatives directly, though they express cynicism about the responsiveness of elected officials.
