This video from Jo Bhakdi focuses on the economics of SpaceX's Starship program ahead of a launch attempt, framing it as critical context for SpaceX's anticipated IPO. The core thesis is that while reusability of the Starship vehicle gets the most public attention, **reliability** — specifically, not losing the rocket and its cargo at launch — is the single most important economic driver for making space commercially viable. A quantitative cost-per-kilogram model is walked through to illustrate how launch failures dramatically inflate effective costs through insurance and cargo loss.

## Market & Competition

1. **Starship Version 3 launch attempt and delay:** A launch attempt of Starship version 3 was delayed due to a hydraulic pin holding the tower arm in place failing to retract. Elon Musk tweeted about this, and a rescheduled attempt was set for approximately 5:30 p.m. CT on the video's publish date (2026-05-22). The launch is framed as highly consequential for the SpaceX IPO and the broader commercial space industry.

2. **Current launch cost baseline:** The existing cost to bring cargo to orbit using SpaceX's Falcon and Falcon Heavy systems is cited at between $2,500 and $5,000 per kilogram, with other providers ranging up to $25,000 per kilogram. Starship's promise is to dramatically reduce this figure.

3. **Starship system production costs at a run rate of 10 units:** At a production run of 10 units in 2026, the Starship upper stage is estimated to cost approximately $41 million per unit, and the booster approximately $82 million, totaling roughly $120 million per system. SpaceX is said to be targeting production of approximately 10 systems in 2026 and scaling toward approximately 100 per year in 2027–2028.

4. **Cost per kilogram under various reuse scenarios:** Without any reusability but with successful cargo delivery, Starship's 150-ton-to-orbit capacity already implies an internal cost of approximately $892 per kilogram. With the booster reused 10 times, this drops to approximately $397 per kilogram. With both the booster and Starship reused 10 times, the cost falls to approximately $148 per kilogram. Booster reusability is described as more or less already achieved through the "chopstick" Mechazilla catch system; Starship reusability remains unsolved.

## Financials

1. **The reliability problem and insurance economics:** The central financial risk identified is not reusability failure but launch failure — i.e., the rocket exploding before delivering cargo. With 10 planned launches in 2026 and one catastrophic failure (losing cargo), the effective insurance cost burden is modeled at approximately $100 million per launch, pushing the cost per kilogram from roughly $400 to approximately $1,000. With two failures out of 10 launches, the cost per kilogram rises to approximately $3,000 — roughly 9–10x the baseline — because only eight successful flights must absorb the losses from two.

2. **Space AI cargo value and the disruption threshold:** A full 150-ton Starship cargo load filled with GPUs and AI hardware is estimated at roughly $900 million to $1 billion in cargo value. The presenter calculates that if cost per kilogram falls below approximately $200, 1-gigawatt data centers in space would be approximately 60% cheaper over a 10-year operating period than equivalent ground-based infrastructure — with no permitting constraints. The disruption threshold for AI-in-space economics is estimated at approximately $500 per kilogram, a level the presenter argues is already within reach even without Starship reusability.

3. **Priority ranking of economic drivers:** The presenter explicitly ranks the three key cost drivers: (1) **Reliability** — not losing the rocket at launch — is by far the most important factor; (2) **Booster reusability** — already largely achieved, driving cost from ~$900 to ~$370 per kilogram; (3) **Starship reusability** — the most technically complex but economically the least critical of the three, capable of driving costs from ~$370 down to ~$100 per kilogram if achieved.
