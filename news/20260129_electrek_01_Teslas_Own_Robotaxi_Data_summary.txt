The article analyzes NHTSA crash reports and Tesla's own disclosed robotaxi mileage to argue that Tesla's Austin robotaxi fleet is crashing far more often than human drivers, despite having a safety monitor in every vehicle.

## Autonomous Driving / Robotaxi

1. **Crash data and calculated rate:** NHTSA's Standing General Order database lists 9 crashes involving Tesla's robotaxi fleet in Austin between July and November 2025, including collisions with an SUV, a cyclist, an animal, a fixed object causing minor injury, and others at low speeds. Tesla's Q4 2025 earnings report chart shows the fleet had traveled approximately 500,000 cumulative miles as of November 2025, which the article calculates as roughly one crash every 55,000 miles.

2. **Comparison to human drivers:** The article cites NHTSA data that human drivers in the US average about one police-reported crash every 500,000 miles, putting Tesla's robotaxi crash rate at roughly 9 times higher by that measure. It notes that when unreported incidents are estimated, human crash frequency is closer to one every 200,000 miles — still better than Tesla's robotaxi figure. (The headline figure of "3x worse" appears to reference a different comparison than the 9x figure derived in the article's own math; the two are not reconciled in the text.)

3. **Safety monitor caveat:** All reported mileage involved vehicles with a human safety monitor able to intervene, meaning the crash rate occurred despite backup supervision, which the article argues makes the comparison more damning. A top reader comment (43 likes, from user "Nx") adds that the data doesn't capture how many crashes were prevented by monitor interventions, arguing the real gap versus unsupervised performance would be worse than the comparison suggests.

4. **Comparison to Waymo:** The article states Waymo operates a fully driverless fleet (no safety monitor) and has logged over 125 million autonomous miles with a crash rate it describes as well below human averages. It also describes a recent Waymo incident in Santa Monica where a child ran into the street from behind an SUV; Waymo's vehicle detected the child and reduced speed from 17 mph to under 6 mph before contact, and the child was uninjured — details the article says Waymo disclosed in full.

5. **Transparency gap:** Every Tesla crash narrative in the NHTSA database is redacted with "[REDACTED, MAY CONTAIN CONFIDENTIAL BUSINESS INFORMATION]," leaving details of the cyclist collision, the animal strike, and the minor-injury incident unknown. The article contrasts this with Waymo, which it says provides full narrative descriptions for incidents in the same database, quoting an example Waymo report describing a rear-end collision caused by another vehicle.

## Editorial Stance
The author argues the data is "sobering," acknowledging a possible improving trend (only one crash each in October and November) but concluding that a crash every 55,000 miles with a safety monitor present is not robotaxi-ready, and that Tesla's redaction practices prevent confidence that it is learning from incidents. The author calls on Tesla to both improve its safety record and increase transparency about crashes in Austin, stating it is currently failing at both.
