This video features futurist blogger Brian Wang (Next Big Future) outlining a thesis that SpaceX and Tesla are quietly building a global telecom and AI-device network using existing hardware — Starlink satellites, superchargers, Powerwalls, Cybercabs, and Optimus bots — as small-cell communication nodes, funded in part by profitable AI ventures including Cursor (now being acquired by SpaceX/xAI). A secondary segment covers Starship's recovered first stage and its implications for reusability economics.

## SpaceX — Starlink
1. **Small-cell network built on existing Tesla/SpaceX hardware:** Wang describes a plan, which he says Elon Musk referenced on a recent earnings call, to use Starlink-equipped Cybercabs as communication nodes, alongside superchargers, Powerwalls, and Optimus robots acting as small cells — using roughly 50 watts at supercharger/Powerwall sites (~1,000 meters range) and about 1 watt in Cybercabs (~400 meters range) — to build a mesh network layered under the Starlink satellite constellation.
2. **Cost advantage over traditional telecom infrastructure:** Wang estimates this approach could be 10–20 times cheaper than traditional cell tower buildouts, since Tesla/SpaceX would avoid leasing fees paid to tower companies, and could replace fiber backhaul with Wband/Dband/Eband satellite links at roughly $3,000 per endpoint versus roughly $200,000 for a traditional fiber endpoint. He also suggests hardware could be built directly into vehicles and Powerwalls at manufacturing time (he cites roughly $100 added per car and $500 per Powerwall) rather than retrofitted — noting Tesla has historically not retrofitted older hardware generations.
3. **Direct-to-cell subscriber capacity estimates:** Wang states current Starlink version 3 satellites have on the order of 2,000 beams for high-speed internet and, per his understanding, roughly 1,000 beams for direct-to-cell, and believes this could support up to around 100 million U.S. subscribers and 300 million or more globally, potentially more if service is stepped down to 4G-equivalent speeds in some markets.
4. **Timeline: competitive rollout over three-plus years:** Wang believes Starlink direct-to-cell (already live for voice/text/low-grade video via partners like T-Mobile) will get "far stronger" by around 2028 and highly competitive by around 2029, with satellite phone-focused hardware discussed for around mid-2027; he frames the full urban/dense-coverage vision as a three-plus-year endgame.
5. **Strategic goal is ecosystem lock-in, not just carrier replacement:** Wang argues the bigger prize than displacing a cellphone plan is controlling the device default — bundling Grok, X, and X Money on a low-cost (he floats a $100) AI device to capture engagement value that Big Tech currently pays billions for (he cites Google paying to be default on phones), monetizing via subscriptions rather than hardware margin.
6. **Gateway station and satellite capacity scaling:** Wang notes SpaceX has first-generation gateway (ground relay) stations and is developing second-generation gateways using Wband/Dband for greater capacity; he also suggests that AI-focused satellites drawing far more power (citing figures of 250 kilowatts up to a megawatt) could, if repurposed for communications, multiply bandwidth roughly 30-fold versus current satellites.
7. **Third-party estimate: Starlink revenue scale comparable to Tesla robotaxi:** Referencing a separate conversation with analysts from Mach 33 (Larry Goldberg and Aaron Bernett), the host relays their (unconfirmed) view that Starlink's revenue/earnings could be comparable in scale to Tesla's robotaxi business in the same future years — a claim the host frames as contrary to consensus that SpaceX's big payoffs are far off.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Cursor developing its own LLM ("Vega") and Composer 3:** Wang cites leaks/reports that Cursor is testing a new coding model, Composer 3, which may include Cursor's own large language model reportedly code-named Vega — claimed by Cursor's CEO per Wang to potentially outperform rival frontier models, with public release possibly within roughly a month of the interview and pricing around 10x cheaper than a comparable rival model.
2. **Cursor's revenue and profitability:** Wang states Cursor has surpassed $4 billion in annualized recurring revenue with about 2.1 million paying seats (average revenue per seat around $2,000), is clearly tracking toward $6 billion+ ARR, and — unlike OpenAI and Anthropic, which he says are still losing money at scale (OpenAI cited as losing $10–20 billion/year on ~$40 billion revenue) — is now profitable after cutting infrastructure costs via access to xAI's compute.
3. **SpaceX/xAI acquisition of Cursor pending close:** Wang states the SpaceX/xAI acquisition of Cursor has been agreed but not yet formally closed, with closing expected around late August or September.
4. **Cursor's "planner vs. worker" model routing strategy:** Wang describes Cursor's orchestration approach — using a top-tier model for planning and cheaper models (naming Kimi, Grok 4.5, and others) for execution — which he says can cut costs on complex coding tasks dramatically (citing a database-build example dropping from roughly $20,000 to roughly $500–$1,300) while auto-optimizing model selection to save users money and increase Cursor's margin.
5. **Growth strategy targeting global developer volume:** Cursor is pursuing volume growth (Wang cites a total addressable market of roughly 30 million developers worldwide, with Cursor currently around 2 million) including a discounted plan for Indian developers, prioritizing developer count growth over near-term revenue-per-seat increases.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation
1. **Cursor acquisition reframed as SpaceX AI asset:** The host and Wang clarify that Cursor is being acquired by SpaceX (not xAI independently), meaning SpaceX would own both Cursor's coding-agent business and its in-development LLM, positioned as a separate profit engine from Grok/Macrohard.

## SpaceX — Starship
1. **Starship reuse potential even without a repaired heat shield:** Citing an analysis from Ark Invest's Brett Winton, Wang argues that Starship's ocean-recovered upper stage shows reuse is possible even with heat-shield damage, since launch itself doesn't damage the heat shield — allowing SpaceX to skip repairs (estimated by Wang at roughly $4 million out of a $40 million stage), remove grid fins to add payload (raising payload from roughly 100–150 tons to as much as 250 tons on a one-way/expendable-style mission), or repurpose a recovered stage as a space station. Wang estimates getting to five or more reuses of the upper stage could push launch costs toward $50–$100 per kilogram.
