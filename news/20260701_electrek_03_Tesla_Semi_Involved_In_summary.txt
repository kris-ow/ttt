A Tesla Semi was involved in its first known fatal crash on June 28, 2026, on U.S. 50 in Dayton, Nevada, killing two people and critically injuring a third after the truck's driver reportedly fell asleep and rear-ended two stopped passenger vehicles.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **First known fatal crash involving a Tesla Semi:** At approximately 7:20 a.m. on June 28, a Tesla Semi struck two passenger vehicles stopped at a red light at the intersection of U.S. 50 and Traditions Parkway in Dayton, Nevada. The couple killed were identified as Sergio "Boo" and Jennifer Villanueva; a third person was airlifted with life-threatening injuries. The Lyon County Sheriff's Office stated that preliminary evidence suggests the driver fell asleep. The Nevada State Police Highway Patrol is investigating. The truck's make was not officially confirmed by the Nevada Highway Patrol, but scene images clearly identified it as a Tesla Semi, first flagged by FreightWaves' Timothy Dooner.

2. **Tesla Semi program context:** Tesla only began ramping customer deliveries of the Semi in 2026 after years of delays, with early fleets including DHL and California port drayage operators. There are still only a few hundred units on the road. Tesla builds the Semi at Gigafactory Nevada near Sparks — roughly an hour from the crash site — and operates its own fleet from that facility, though the operator of the truck involved has not been confirmed.

## Autonomous Driving
3. **No FSD involved; AEB performance is the central question:** Tesla does not offer Full Self-Driving on the Semi. A Tesla Semi was spotted in California running FSD test hardware just three days before the crash, but that is a separate test-fleet program; the crashed truck's driver was in full manual control. The article raises whether the Semi's automatic emergency braking (AEB) engaged before impact. Tesla originally stated the Semi ships with Enhanced Autopilot and uses the same camera hardware as its passenger vehicles — which run standard AEB — but has never published a Semi-specific active-safety specification, leaving it unclear whether forward-collision braking functions equivalently or whether it activated in this incident.

4. **Drowsiness detection gap:** Tesla introduced a "Driver Drowsiness Warning" feature for its cars in 2023, using the cabin-facing camera to detect yawns and blinks above 40 mph when Autopilot is disengaged. Tesla has not confirmed whether the Semi has a cabin-facing camera or an equivalent fatigue-detection feature — a notable gap given the reported cause of this crash. Commercial fatigue-detection systems are increasingly common in trucking but remain optional rather than federally required.

## Editorial Stance
The author emphasizes this was a human-fatigue failure, not an autonomous driving incident, and urges against conflating it with FSD-related concerns. The primary focus, per the author, should be on whether the Semi's AEB system engaged — framing it as a standard active-safety question that should be answered as the investigation proceeds.
