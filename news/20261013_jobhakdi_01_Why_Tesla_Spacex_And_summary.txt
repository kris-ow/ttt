## Market & Competition

He frames Micron's earnings surge as driven mainly by price expansion (demand outstripping supply) rather than volume, and argues the stock is undervalued, trading at a PE around 12–13 (forward PE roughly 6) despite the CEO reportedly having already locked in about 35% of planned 2031 revenue via long-term contracts; he holds a Micron position (~12% of his portfolio, via deep-in-the-money short puts) and took losses this week but remains fundamentally bullish, speculating the stock could reach $2,000 within 12 months while cautioning it could also fall back toward levels seen in a prior crash (he cites ~$710 from a "July" selloff he attributes to the current year).

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Terrestrial vs. orbital compute buildout framing:** The host says SpaceX added about 1.5 gigawatts of compute this year and expects to add another three-plus gigawatts next year (he separately floats 3–5, or "five to six," as a stretch case), arguing this terrestrial buildout alone could drive SpaceX toward roughly $200 billion in annual profit run-rate within the next year to 18 months — which he says would dwarf even Micron's profit growth. He frames Starlink, including the newly announced 800 MHz direct-to-cell spectrum deal covering T-Mobile/AT&T-type markets, as a "nice to have" strategic asset but comparatively minor (he estimates the mobile opportunity at roughly $500 billion market cap, call it an order of magnitude smaller) next to compute.
2. **Why Cybercab should get a higher multiple than SpaceX terrestrial compute:** The host's central thesis is that $40 billion in Cybercab profit would earn a materially higher market multiple (he estimates roughly 10x higher) than the same $40 billion from terrestrial AI compute, because Cybercab deployment is scalable (going from 1 million to 10–100 million units is plausible) whereas terrestrial compute faces hard physical, political, and regulatory ceilings (data-center siting resistance, 2028 election-cycle backlash) that make each additional gigawatt harder to add than the last. He illustrates this with a rough equivalence that one gigawatt of SpaceX compute generates profit comparable to about 1 million deployed Cybercabs (he cites a conservative $30–50 billion profit range), but argues Tesla doesn't need to match that scale to match SpaceX's stock impact, because Cybercab profit multiples (he guesses around 100x in a bull case) would far exceed the multiple (he guesses 10–20x) the market would assign to terrestrial compute earnings.
3. **Orbital compute timeline:** The host expects orbital AI compute to begin meaningfully scaling toward the end of 2028, eventually (in his view) coming to dominate overall compute first within the solar system and later, over a longer horizon, more broadly — a scenario he says would earn SpaceX a much higher valuation multiple than its terrestrial compute business.
4. **Starship launch-cadence ceiling as a key constraint:** The host says a model he's building shows combined launch permits at Kennedy Space Center, Cape Canaveral, and Starbase, Texas cap Starship launches at roughly 145 per year, a ceiling he says would be hit around 2030 given projected flight-count growth (he cites figures of 30 Starship flights needed in 2028, 68 in 2029, and 152 in 2029/2030 timeframe) — making this cadence limit, in his view, the binding constraint on scaling orbital compute deployment. He argues this makes a planned SpaceX Louisiana launch site (criticized by a commenter as an unnecessary cost) actually the most important project for SpaceX's 2030s trajectory, alongside Starship reusability, since without added launch capacity SpaceX cannot manufacture or fly enough Starships to keep scaling.
