The article reports that Obrist, an Austrian engineering firm known for retrofitting a Tesla Model 3 with a gasoline range-extender engine, has gone bankrupt, with three group companies owing a combined roughly €31.8 million (about $37 million), and never having brought a production vehicle to market.

## Market & Competition
1. **Obrist Group insolvency details:** The Feldkirch Regional Court opened bankruptcy proceedings in late September for Obrist Powertrain GmbH (owes about €18.85 million to 34 creditors, down to nine employees) and Obrist Technologies GmbH (owes about €6.08 million to 10 creditors, no remaining staff). A third entity, Obrist DE, is also insolvent with about €6.8 million in liabilities per ORF Vorarlberg. Both Austrian units told the court restructuring was impossible and agreed to shut down immediately; the parent company, Obrist Engineering (air-conditioning/thermal parts, ~30 employees), is not part of the filings. Founder Frank Obrist cited high development costs, failed investor talks, and the European auto industry crisis; creditor-protection group AKV also pointed to automakers' shift to full electrification.

2. **History of unfulfilled claims:** Obrist first showed a Model 3-based "Mark II" in 2020 with a shrunk 17.3 kWh battery and a 40 kW two-cylinder gas generator in the frunk, claiming 1,000 km range, a licensing deal with an unnamed "international partner," production by 2023, and a system cost of about €4,500 at volume. None of this materialized — the partner was never named and no car ever shipped. In 2022 it pivoted to a methanol-fueled "aFuel" Model Y prototype claiming a per-kilometer CO2 reduction, then in 2024 announced an Abu Dhabi fund alliance with a claimed "double-digit billion-euro" investment for methanol plants in several countries, and pitched $18.6 billion "gigaplants," asserting roughly 2,700 would be needed (about $50 trillion total) to replace fossil fuels. Obrist also lobbied the EU to reconsider its 2035 combustion-car ban, claiming its HyperHybrid would cut CO2 by 85% versus a comparable EV.

## Editorial Stance
Electrek's Fred Lambert argues Obrist was never a credible range-extender company but a "licensing shop with no customer" that kept escalating its claims (from 2023 production to a $50 trillion fossil-fuel-replacement plan) each time prior promises failed, while battery costs fell and EV range improved, undercutting its core premise; the piece notes Obrist's own bankruptcy filing cites automakers' shift to full EVs as a cause of its failure.
