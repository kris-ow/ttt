Tesla announced it will end upfront one-time sales of Full Self-Driving (FSD), moving to a subscription-only model starting February 14, 2026 — the same day a California court ruling takes effect requiring Tesla to stop deceptively marketing FSD and Autopilot. The author, Jameson Dow, examines whether the timing is coincidental or a deliberate response to the ruling, and concludes the connection is unclear.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **California ruling on FSD/Autopilot marketing:** A California judge ruled on December 17th that Tesla deceptively marketed both "Full Self-Driving" and "Autopilot," calling the FSD name "actually, unambiguously false and counterfactual." The ruling specifically orders Tesla to stop using the "Autopilot" name for ADAS features that don't achieve SAE Level 3/4/5 autonomy, but — the author notes — does not explicitly order Tesla to stop using the "Full Self-Driving" name despite finding it misleading. The ruling takes effect February 14, 2026, giving Tesla 60 days from the December 17th decision to comply; Tesla's deadline to file a petition for reconsideration was January 15th, the day after Tesla's subscription announcement, and the article states it has not confirmed whether Tesla filed such a petition.
2. **Why the subscription switch likely doesn't satisfy the ruling:** The author argues the shift from upfront sales to subscription doesn't change Tesla's marketing, naming, or description of FSD/Autopilot — the court's concern was deceptive marketing, not the sales model — so this move alone probably doesn't address the court's or California DMV's objections. The author speculates Tesla may roll out additional changes to how it distinguishes Autopilot from FSD around the same date.
3. **Theory: tied to Musk's compensation package:** Some have theorized the change aims to help Musk hit a tranche of his roughly $1 trillion pay package requiring 10 million+ FSD subscriptions. The author argues this is unlikely since the tranche counts both one-time purchases and active subscriptions (and purchases can't be cancelled, so Musk would still prefer them), and because Tesla's installed base is far short of that number — citing roughly 4 million cars lacking the hardware for current FSD and a low current take rate.
4. **Theory: resolving FSD transfer/hardware disputes:** The author suggests the subscription-only model could permanently resolve Tesla's recurring, "temporary" accommodations allowing customers to transfer paid FSD licenses to new vehicles — an issue arising from years of selling FSD (for as much as $15,000) without delivering promised capabilities, which has led to customer complaints and class-action lawsuits. A subscription model would have customers pay only for current capabilities rather than a promise of future features, which the author views favorably for reducing Tesla's liabilities and improving revenue predictability.

## Editorial Stance
The author is skeptical that ending upfront FSD sales actually addresses the California ruling's concerns, since the ruling targets deceptive marketing/naming rather than the sales model itself. The author views a subscription model as beneficial for Tesla (reducing liability, enabling more predictable SaaS-style revenue) and for consumer transparency, but remains uncertain whether the February 14 timing is a deliberate response to the court deadline or coincidental, speculating that further changes to FSD/Autopilot structure may be announced around the same date.
