Electrek reports that Tesla began offering a small number of Austin Robotaxi rides without an in-car safety monitor, but an article update cites video evidence that these "unsupervised" vehicles are being trailed by separate Tesla vehicles containing safety monitors, meaning human oversight was relocated rather than removed. The author, Fred Lambert, frames the rollout skeptically as an attempt to manufacture a "win" after years of missed Musk deadlines, rather than evidence that unsupervised self-driving is solved.

## Robotaxi
1. **Gradual unsupervised rollout announced:** Musk posted video of a rider in an Austin Robotaxi without a front-seat safety monitor; Tesla VP of Self-Driving Ashok Elluswamy said the rollout starts with "a few unsupervised vehicles mixed in with the broader robotaxi fleet with safety monitors," with the ratio increasing over time.
2. **Trailing safety-monitor vehicles:** An update to the article cites new video evidence that the supposedly unsupervised Robotaxis are closely followed by black Tesla vehicles carrying safety monitors, which the author says shows Tesla moved the monitor rather than eliminating it.
3. **Missed prior promises:** The article notes Musk repeatedly said in September, October, November, and December that safety monitors would be removed "by the end of the year," and that this was only true for employee/internal rides — paying customers are still getting safety monitors as of the article's writing.
4. **Crash data concerns:** The author states Tesla's Robotaxi fleet has had at least 8 crashes reported to NHTSA since June (with safety monitors present), and an unknown number of unreported incidents. Based on Tesla's own disclosure of roughly 250,000 fleet miles through early November, the author calculates a crash rate of about once per 60,000 miles, which the article contrasts with an average human crash rate of about once per 500,000 miles and Waymo's rate of about once per 98,600 miles over 125 million+ fully driverless miles. The author also asserts Tesla redacts narrative sections of NHTSA crash reports, unlike Waymo.
5. **Fleet size disputed:** The article cites a Texas A&M engineering student, Ethan McKanna, who reverse-engineered Tesla's Robotaxi app and found only 32 distinct Model Ys in the Austin network at the time, with typically fewer than 10 operating simultaneously — far below Musk's prediction of 500 vehicles by year-end 2025, and short of his "1,000 within a few months" and "half the US population" coverage claims, none of which the article says materialized.
6. **Remote monitoring reliance:** The author reiterates earlier reporting that Tesla's fleet relies heavily on teleoperation, citing a Tesla-released photo of a control room with visible steering wheels, and Musk's past statement that "we can't screw up."
7. **Waymo comparison:** The article states Waymo is running about 450,000 weekly driverless rides across Austin, Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Atlanta, and Miami — an 80% increase from six months earlier — with plans to expand to 10 more U.S. cities by end of 2026, contrasted against Tesla's small, mostly supervised Austin fleet.

## Editorial Stance
The author argues this is not a genuine solving of unsupervised driving but an optics-driven move to claim a "win" after a decade of missed deadlines; he says the gradual ratio approach implicitly admits Tesla isn't confident in full unsupervised operation, contradicting Musk's claim that "unsupervised is pretty much solved." He leans toward a cynical interpretation while acknowledging a more charitable reading (cautious, responsible data-gathering) is possible, and concludes Tesla is "playing catch-up while trying to look like you're winning" relative to Waymo.
