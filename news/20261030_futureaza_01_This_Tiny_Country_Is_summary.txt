This episode of futureaza travels to Nepal to examine the country's electric bus program, covering both commercially purchased Chinese buses and a locally engineered diesel-to-electric conversion project. The core thesis is that Nepal's abundant, renewable (hydropower) but non-storable electricity supply makes electric buses an economically and strategically sound choice over imported diesel, despite much higher upfront capital costs. There is no Tesla or SpaceX content; the piece is entirely about a foreign public transit operator and a domestic bus-manufacturing/conversion ecosystem unrelated to either company's thesis.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Nepal's bus fleet economics:** A Nepalese public transit official explains the agency holds roughly 99% share of government bus procurement and bought its initial electric buses from CHTC (a Chinese manufacturer) via competitive bidding roughly a decade ago; buses were priced around $87,000 each (corrected from an initially cited $93,000, attributed to a later exchange-rate mismatch), versus the host's claim that a comparable BYD bus in the US costs $400,000–$500,000.
2. **Real-world range and charging:** The buses carry a 161 kWh battery with a manufacturer-claimed WLTP-style range of 200 km (at 100%-to-0% discharge), but since the fleet is never drained below 15–20% remaining charge, real-world daily range is about 120–150 km, sufficient for six to eight trips per route on a single overnight charge using 98 kW chargers with 45 kW nozzles per side (four to five hour charge time).
3. **Durability and maintenance uncertainty:** After roughly four years of operation, major electrical components (controller, motor, battery) have not yet needed replacement, but the operator says Chinese manufacturers follow a "replace, not repair" policy for electrical modules, and post-warranty replacement costs remain unknown since the fleet hasn't yet aged past warranty coverage.
4. **Design shortcomings and next-order plans:** Current 8.5m buses have insufficient ground clearance for Nepal's pothole-heavy roads, causing bumper damage; the operator plans to extend future bus length (cited as "2 meter" in context implying a larger bus class, not literal 2m) to raise passenger capacity within the same fixed cost base, and is also considering articulated buses for high-traffic routes to ease Kathmandu Valley congestion.
5. **Cost gap vs. diesel buses:** Diesel buses cost roughly 10 million Nepalese rupees while comparable electric buses cost at least double that, mainly due to battery costs and financing; this capital cost gap is cited as the main reason operators hesitate to switch, despite electric buses paying back the cost difference in three to five years and producing zero tailpipe emissions.
6. **Macroeconomic rationale:** The official argues that even if electricity and diesel costs were equal, electric buses would still be preferable because diesel imports drain Nepal's foreign currency reserves (as a landlocked country reliant on trucked-in fuel), whereas electricity spending circulates within the domestic economy; the organization has committed to procuring only electric buses going forward, with plans to expand to intercity routes connecting provinces and sub-metro cities.
7. **DIY diesel-to-electric bus conversion:** An engineer demonstrates a locally converted 16-year-old diesel bus retrofitted with a 56 kWh battery pack and an electric motor (requiring a calculated nominal motor power around 120–122 kW to meet a 50 km/h speed target and handle grades of 14–15°), reusing the original manual transmission's first gear, differential, and propeller shaft, with an electric pump replacing the engine-driven vacuum pump for hydraulic brakes; the bus seats 32 and carries 32 standing, has already carried 60 passengers in a demonstration, and awaits government regulatory approval (expected to take roughly a year or two post-approval) before conversions can be legalized and scaled.
