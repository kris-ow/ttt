This episode of "brighterwithherbert" covers four major Tesla news stories with guest Brian White (FutureAzA), discussing FSD international expansion, Cybercab field sightings, Tesla Semi early market reception, and Tesla's newly identified solar panel gigafactory site in Texas. The hosts argue these developments collectively signal Tesla is in a rapid, broad-based expansion phase across autonomous driving, commercial vehicles, and clean energy manufacturing. Brian White provides on-the-ground context from his reporting on factory buildouts and fleet infrastructure.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD Supervised Approved in Lithuania, Now Live in ~10 Countries:** Tesla announced that FSD Supervised has been approved in Lithuania, making it the second European country after the Netherlands (which approved on April 11th) to grant this authorization. The hosts note FSD Supervised is now available in approximately 10 countries worldwide, though Brian White clarifies the count may be closer to 8 or 9 depending on whether territories like Puerto Rico are counted separately. The Lithuanian Ministry of Transport and Communications stated that "these technologies can already make a real contribution to safer and more comfortable driving" and emphasized that regulation should not lag behind technological developments.

2. **Greece Poised to Be Next in Europe:** The Secretary General of Transport for Greece publicly announced that Greece intends to become the second EU country to approve FSD, stating they will accept the Netherlands' type approval outright without waiting for a broader EU vote. Greece had expected to be second but was beaten to the announcement by Lithuania. The hosts expect Greece's approval to come in June 2026. The Secretary General cited data from North America showing "a very significant reduction in accidents" under supervised FSD.

3. **Ireland FSD Approval Underway; Tesla Proactively Engaging EU Members:** Brian White reports that FSD approval in Ireland is actively underway. He notes Tesla has been reaching out individually to EU member countries, offering test vehicles and data to expedite national approvals rather than waiting passively for EU-wide action. The hosts suggest more country approvals beyond Lithuania are imminent and that neighboring countries will demand FSD access once they observe its performance in approved markets.

4. **China FSD Hiring Surge Signals Acceleration:** The hosts discuss a wave of urgent job listings in China for FSD-related data labelers and local supervisors, suggesting Tesla is aggressively ramping its China FSD program. Tesla's data center in China appears to have been operational for roughly two to three months. The hosts note China requires locally processed training data and has its own approval scope, and speculate that broader China FSD approval could come in Q3 2026 rather than sooner.

5. **FSD's Technological Differentiation vs. Competitors:** Brian White draws a sharp contrast between Tesla FSD and systems like GM Super Cruise, noting Super Cruise operates only on approximately 400,000 pre-mapped road miles worldwide, while FSD works on every road in North America — including unmapped terrain like fields. The hosts assert FSD Supervised is "eight to ten times better" than human driving alone based on published safety statistics.

## Robotaxi

1. **Cybercab Spotted in Dallas for the First Time:** A Cybercab was observed entering a service center in Dallas, marking the first time a Cybercab has been publicly spotted in that city. Separately, a production-version Cybercab at the Houston robotaxi hub was photographed with a license plate — a development Brian White interprets as a strong signal of imminent operational deployment, noting that vehicles are not plated unless they are close to being put into use.

2. **Cybercab Charging at Austin Supercharger:** A Cybercab was filmed charging at a Supercharger in Austin, with a Tesla employee present to plug in the vehicle. The hosts discuss wireless charging for the Cybercab: the ultra-wideband positioning system for pad alignment received regulatory approval in February 2026, but full wireless charging system approval and production-scale deployment have not yet been confirmed. The hosts agree wireless charging is not a rate-limiting factor for near-term robotaxi operations.

3. **Robotaxi Hub Infrastructure Buildout Identified in Multiple Cities:** Brian White describes a newly reported robotaxi maintenance and car wash hub permitted in Las Vegas, featuring approximately 35,000 square feet, 17 DC fast chargers, and 200+ parking spots. A similar facility exists in Dallas. The host estimates that a 200-spot hub could support roughly 1,000 active vehicles in service, assuming only ~20% of the fleet is off-road at any given time. Brian White notes the cars do not need to return to a home base nightly and can park anywhere legally, with strategic overnight positioning near anticipated morning demand.

4. **Manual Charging Scalability vs. Waymo:** The hosts discuss Waymo's fleet of approximately 3,700 vehicles across five cities (expanding to ten) and note that all Waymo vehicles currently require manual charging. They argue this is manageable at small scale but becomes a logistical challenge around 10,000 vehicles. The hosts also note Tesla's structural cost advantage: Tesla charges at its own Supercharger network at internal cost, while Waymo pays retail or near-retail rates to third-party networks like Electrify America, compounded by the fact that Waymo's vehicles are less energy-efficient per mile.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Tesla Semi Receives Rave Early Reviews, Strong California Demand:** The New York Times published a positive early review of the Tesla Semi, suggesting it could significantly disrupt the trucking industry. California trucking firms have submitted applications for state subsidies to purchase more than 1,200 Tesla Semi trucks — more applications than all other electric truck models combined since California's incentive program began in 2019. At $290,000 per long-range unit, 1,200 trucks would represent approximately $350 million in Tesla Semi revenue.

2. **Tesla Semi Priced Well Below Competitors:** The Tesla Semi's long-range version is priced at $290,000, compared to electric trucks from Daimler, Volvo, and others that typically start at $400,000 or more. Brian White notes that $300,000 has long been the economic threshold at which electric semis make financial sense for fleet operators even without elevated diesel prices. With diesel prices having risen approximately 50% recently, the economic case becomes even more compelling, potentially justifying purchase prices up to $400,000–$450,000.

3. **California Incentives Could Make Tesla Semi Cheaper Than Diesel Upfront:** Brian White explains that California's previous incentive structure offered $120,000 per rig for general buyers and $150,000 for small businesses or veteran-owned companies, with drayage trucks qualifying for $150,000. At those levels, a $290,000 Tesla Semi could land at $140,000 or less — potentially at or below the typical upfront cost of a diesel semi ($150,000 range), making every subsequent mile of operation pure savings.

4. **Tesla Semi Uptime and Reliability:** A recent survey cited by Brian White showed the Tesla Semi achieving 95% uptime, compared to diesel trucks which typically operate at 82–87% uptime, with best-in-class diesel reaching 92–95%. Brian White characterizes this as remarkable for a brand-new vehicle platform and expects reliability to improve further as the product matures.

5. **50,000-Unit Annual Run Rate Expected by 2028:** When asked about production ramp timelines, Brian White said he would be "very surprised" if Tesla hits a 50,000-unit-per-year run rate by end of 2027, projecting that 2028 is more likely — contingent on 4680 battery cell production scaling appropriately. He estimates 50,000 semis would require approximately 30–40 gigawatt-hours of batteries annually and notes that while Tesla has indicated the battery production challenge is "solved," he is waiting for confirmation before fully believing it given the history of 4680 delays.

## Energy

1. **Tesla Solar Gigafactory Located Near Houston, Texas:** Tesla's large-scale solar panel factory has been identified as sited in Bastrop County (Bergstshire area), Texas, near Houston, co-located with Tesla's Megapack gigafactory already under construction at the same site. The facility will integrate the full manufacturing stack: ingot growth, wafer slicing, photovoltaic cell production, and finished panel assembly — all under one roof. Tesla has earmarked $250 million in capex for the project.

2. **100 Gigawatt Annual Manufacturing Target — Vastly Exceeds Current US Market:** Tesla's stated target is 100 gigawatts of annual solar manufacturing capacity. For comparison, First Solar — currently the largest domestic solar manufacturer — projects 17.7 gigawatts by 2027, meaning Tesla's target is nearly six times First Solar's. Total US solar installations in all of 2023 were approximately 32 gigawatts, making Tesla's target roughly three times that entire annual market. Brian White notes that $250 million is highly capital-efficient for a project of this scale, citing that other companies have spent over $1 billion on similar efforts unsuccessfully, largely because they had to build a company from scratch in addition to the factory.

3. **Strategic Rationale: Tariffs, Shipping, and Captive Demand:** Brian White outlines several advantages of domestic solar manufacturing for Tesla: avoiding import tariffs, eliminating 1–2 month shipping delays and associated costs, and reducing in-transit damage risk. He notes that solar panels are a commodity product and that lights-out (fully automated) solar factories already exist in China, suggesting labor cost is not a meaningful disadvantage for US manufacturing. Tesla's captive demand — Superchargers, Megapack installations, AI data centers, and residential customers — provides a structural customer base.

## Market & Competition

1. **Waymo Fleet and Cost Structure Contrasted with Tesla Robotaxi:** The hosts reference Waymo's current fleet of approximately 3,700 vehicles operating across five cities with plans to expand to ten cities. They highlight Waymo's structural cost disadvantages relative to Tesla's robotaxi model: higher energy costs per mile due to less efficient vehicles, dependence on third-party charging networks at retail or near-retail prices, and manual charging labor requirements that become increasingly burdensome at scale.
