This video examines why SpaceX is building an in-house capability to cast gas turbine blades and veins, arguing that this obscure manufacturing effort — not chip supply — is the key bottleneck determining how fast SpaceX can scale its AI data center (Colossus) revenue. It walks through the metallurgy and casting process in detail, then evaluates Elon Musk's claim of a 12-18 month speed advantage against outside analyst skepticism and competitors' alternative strategies of buying power through established suppliers.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation / Financials
1. **Aggressive revenue growth targets tied to AI compute:** SpaceX CFO Brett Johnson said the company is on track for roughly $100 billion in annual recurring revenue (one month's pace annualized) by the end of 2026; Elon Musk's own projection reaches as high as $1 trillion in annual recurring revenue by the end of 2027 — a roughly 10x increase from the 2026 pace.
2. **Compute capacity targets:** SpaceX had 1.7 gigawatts of computing capacity at its Colossus data centers (Memphis and South Haven) as of the end of September 2026, with Elon Musk targeting 13 gigawatts by the end of 2027. Musk says the binding constraint on this growth is power, cooling, and infrastructure rather than chip supply.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute / Energy
1. **SpaceX's turbine blade casting bet:** To accelerate power delivery for Colossus, SpaceX is developing in-house casting of gas turbine blades and veins (the hot-section airfoils that extract energy from combustion gas), rather than buying complete turbines and waiting on third-party suppliers. A SpaceX executive stated that turbine suppliers report blades and veins are 12-18 months behind the rest of a finished turbine, and that SpaceX casting its own parts could bring power online 12-18 months faster than competitors — a claim the video stresses is unproven, with no publicly verified SpaceX-cast industrial turbine blade shipped to date.
2. **Independent analyst skepticism on timeline:** Jefferies and other sector analysts, as cited by the Wall Street Journal, estimate that building a single-crystal casting line from scratch takes four years or more — at least four times longer than the revenue growth window SpaceX is targeting (through end of 2027).
3. **SpaceX's existing materials expertise:** SpaceX has operated an in-house super alloy foundry since at least 2019 per Elon Musk's public statements, originally developed for Raptor engine turbopumps, which Musk has said use a SpaceX alloy called SX500 for hot oxygen-rich environments. However, SX500's chemistry is undisclosed and there's no verified evidence it is the alloy being used for the industrial power turbine blades.
4. **Tesla connection via shared personnel and energy storage:** Per a Foundry Management and Technology report, Charles Kuhman was recruited to coordinate alloy development across both Tesla and SpaceX. Separately, Tesla Megapack batteries have been installed at the Colossus site to help manage demand/supply timing alongside turbine generation — a more direct Tesla link than the casting work itself, since Tesla's aluminum high-pressure die casting process differs from the ceramic-shell single-crystal blade casting SpaceX is pursuing.
5. **Industry backlog context:** Siemens Energy says about 70% of its turbine blades and veins go toward servicing already-installed machines rather than new builds. GE Vernova says hitting its end-of-2026 bookings target would leave it mostly sold out through 2030, illustrating the supply constraint SpaceX is trying to route around.
6. **Competing hyperscaler approaches:** Rather than building in-house casting, other AI infrastructure players are securing power through partners — Meta's Hyperion project is working with an energy company building combined-cycle power plants, and Crusoe has ordered gas turbines from GE Vernova for data center power.
