This futureaza video is a solo opinion piece raising skepticism about SpaceX's Starmind concept — launching large-scale AI compute into orbit. The host argues that while the idea is technically exciting, it may be a poor decision on cost, obsolescence, and de-orbiting grounds, and prefers SpaceX's stated plan to build terrestrial data centers first as a proof of concept.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Terrestrial-first approach preferred over orbital compute:** The host argues data centers should be built on the ground first, citing that ground-based sites are repairable, upgradable, and can use closed-loop water cooling and behind-the-meter power (solar plus batteries, or turbines) to avoid grid strain and local pushback. He notes SpaceX has said it will build compute on the ground first, which he calls the right move.
2. **Cost comparison favors ground-based compute:** The host argues ground data centers need more solar panels than orbital ones (due to night, weather, and orientation limits) but panels are cheaper, replaceable, and don't require the heavy shielding, radiation hardening, and micrometeorite protection that space-based satellites need. He speculates that Megapack costs could be lower than launch costs, potentially making terrestrial deployment cheaper overall, though he stresses actual cost figures are something only SpaceX's own accounting can determine.
3. **De-orbiting orbital compute is a major, likely permanent problem:** At the high altitudes being considered (a geosynchronous-type band), the host says there is virtually no atmospheric drag, so satellites could remain in orbit for hundreds to thousands of years versus the 3–7 year decay of Starlink satellites. He notes de-orbiting from that altitude takes roughly 20 times more energy than reaching a "graveyard" orbit, making active de-orbiting impractical, so obsolete compute satellites would likely be stranded in orbit rather than removed — his core objection, framed as risking an "antique mall" of dead, unusable, unrepairable hardware in space that also crowds out future use of that orbital band.
4. **Re-entry of valuable/hazardous materials framed as a risk:** The host says these satellites would contain large quantities of valuable elements (he lists gallium, palladium, zinc, lithium, iron, phosphate, manganese, cobalt) that, unlike ground-based hardware, cannot be recycled if lost in orbit; if satellites are ever de-orbited or fall, hundreds of tons of these materials could burn up in the atmosphere, which he characterizes as a potential environmental and (rarely) ground-collision hazard since the racks aren't designed to survive re-entry.
5. **Broader AI data-center pushback framed as organic, not orchestrated:** The host pushes back on claims that opposition to AI data centers is an "orchestrated hate campaign," describing his own experience near a Google data center in Moses Lake, Washington, where promised economic benefits (jobs, investment) largely didn't materialize locally — most spending went to imported equipment, construction jobs were temporary and non-local, and only a handful of permanent jobs were created — while the facility drew discounted power and water. He argues public frustration over rising power bills and water usage is a natural, understandable reaction, not evidence of an orchestrated campaign.
