This video, from the channel "jobhakdi" (Jo Bhakdi), argues that a SpaceX-Tesla merger is inevitable and imminent, and focuses almost entirely on the mechanics of how such a deal would be structured, priced, and voted on. The core thesis is that the acquisition offer will be denominated in absolute dollars (not as a percentage of SpaceX), that SpaceX's post-IPO volatility creates dangerous uncertainty for Tesla shareholders, and that a "ratchet" mechanism must be built into the deal to protect Tesla shareholders and ensure a clean vote.

## Market & Competition

1. **Merger inevitability and timing:** Jo Bhakdi argues the SpaceX-Tesla merger is certain to happen, citing Elon Musk's need to consolidate companies into a singular entity (possibly renamed X Corp) to eliminate governance friction and maximize management focus. He references Chamath Palihapitiya as sharing this view. He believes the merger will happen sooner rather than later, citing upcoming U.S. midterm elections and current political momentum as reasons not to delay. He speculatively suggests an offer could come within one to three months of the video's publication.

2. **Structural mechanics — SpaceX acquires Tesla, not vice versa:** Bhakdi states clearly that SpaceX must be the acquiring entity (the surviving legal entity) because Elon Musk holds majority 10x supervoting shares in SpaceX. He uses the Cursor acquisition — reportedly done at a firm price of $60 billion (equivalent to roughly 3% of SpaceX at the time) — as a precedent to argue that all acquisitions are denominated in absolute dollar amounts, not percentages of the acquirer. Therefore, the Tesla deal will be announced as a specific dollar market cap offer, payable in SpaceX shares, with the exact number of SpaceX shares determined only at the moment the vote closes, based on SpaceX's market price at that time.

3. **SpaceX share structure enabling the deal:** Bhakdi notes that SpaceX has 36 billion authorized shares versus only 13 billion outstanding — meaning authorized shares are approximately 300% of outstanding shares. He argues this ratio is highly unusual (far beyond a typical employee options pool reserve of 10–20%) and signals deliberate pre-authorization for a large acquisition. He says SpaceX would not need additional board approval to issue shares for a Tesla acquisition.

4. **SpaceX post-IPO volatility as a deal risk:** Bhakdi describes SpaceX's post-IPO price action as extremely volatile — IPO'd at $161, ran to $218 (roughly +35%), then fell back to approximately $185 (about +14% from IPO, net). He argues the current float is only about 5% of total shares, which could expand to 20% soon as lockups expire, creating significant selling pressure starting around August 2026. He expects SpaceX to retreat toward or below IPO price in that window, which he says is nearly inevitable regardless of the company's long-term value. He explicitly states he is not buying SpaceX shares currently, expecting a cheaper entry point.

5. **Scenario analysis — deal without a ratchet:** Bhakdi presents a model assuming a 50% premium acquisition offer at $600/share for Tesla (implying a $1.88 trillion Tesla market cap), based on a then-current Tesla share price of approximately $400. He models the outcome for Tesla shareholders depending on where SpaceX trades at the vote date: if SpaceX is flat at $183 (market cap $2.43T), Tesla shareholders own 44% of the new entity; if SpaceX rises 20% to $220 (market cap ~$2.9T), Tesla shareholders own only 39%; if SpaceX falls 20% to $146 (market cap ~$1.94T), Tesla shareholders own nearly 50%; if SpaceX falls 50% to $92 (market cap ~$1.22T), Tesla shareholders own 61%. He argues this variability creates perverse incentives — Tesla shareholders would want SpaceX to fall, SpaceX shareholders would want to push the price up to minimize dilution — opening the door to manipulation, resentment, and vote failure.

6. **The ratchet proposal:** Bhakdi proposes a one-line addendum to the deal: Tesla shareholders will receive the greater of either $1.88 trillion (the 50%-premium fixed offer) or the full SpaceX market cap at the moment of the vote (a one-to-one merger of equals). Under this structure, if SpaceX stays flat ($2.43T market cap), Tesla is acquired for $2.43T at ~$778/share and owns 50% of the combined entity. If SpaceX rises 20% to $2.9T, Tesla is acquired for $2.9T at ~$933/share and still owns 50%. If SpaceX falls 20% to $1.94T, Tesla is acquired for $1.94T at ~$622/share and still owns 50%. Only if SpaceX falls below the $1.88T floor does the fixed minimum kick in — e.g., if SpaceX falls 50% to $1.22T, Tesla is still acquired at $1.88T ($600/share) and owns 61% of the new entity. He argues this ratchet eliminates manipulation risk, stabilizes the vote, and ensures Tesla shareholders are never worse off if SpaceX rises in value.

7. **Fiduciary and legal risk of a bad deal:** Bhakdi argues that if institutional Tesla shareholders (including those like Ron Baron who also hold SpaceX) vote to approve a deal that disadvantages Tesla shareholders in order to benefit their SpaceX holdings, this creates a fiduciary breach and exposes the Tesla board to significant legal liability. He contends the deal must be genuinely attractive to Tesla shareholders or the vote will fail, and believes all parties — including Elon Musk — have strong incentives to structure it cleanly.
