This is a live-broadcast style recap of a SpaceX Starship test flight that, for the first time, attempted (and ultimately achieved) an orbital insertion, deployed a batch of new-generation Starlink V3 satellites, and completed a multi-hour orbital coast before reentry and splashdown. The narrative follows the mission from booster liftoff through hot-staging, booster splashdown near Starbase, an in-flight engine anomaly that briefly forced a fallback to a suborbital plan, a subsequent go-ahead for orbit, satellite deployment, and finally reentry and splashdown in the North Pacific.

## SpaceX — Starship
1. **First-ever Starship orbital insertion:** The flight launched on a Super Heavy booster with 33 Raptor engines and a Starship upper stage with six Raptor engines, using hot-staging separation; after ascent the vehicle initially followed a passively safe suborbital trajectory (by design, so it would fall into the Indian Ocean if nothing further happened) before a planned single-Raptor burn was used to raise it into a genuine orbit — described by commentators as the first time Starship reached orbit.
2. **Engine anomaly forced a decision point:** During ascent, one of Starship's Raptor Vacuum (RVAC) engines shut down prematurely (engine "number six"), reducing the ship to five of six engines; commentators noted a similar RVAC loss occurred on a prior V3 flight. Mission control initially announced it would NOT attempt the orbital burn as a result and would let Starship proceed on its safe suborbital path to the Indian Ocean, but after further review of engine health (with all three sea-level Raptor "core" engines confirmed nominal) the team reversed course and authorized the orbital insertion burn, which was successfully executed using a single Raptor firing off auxiliary nose-cone fuel tanks.
3. **Booster landing and reentry/splashdown:** The Super Heavy booster performed a boostback burn and landing burn (stepping down from 13 to 5 to 3 engines), successfully splashing down near Starbase, described as a soft landing. Starship itself later performed a deorbit retrograde burn (~8–10 seconds on one Raptor) roughly 2 hours 12 minutes into the flight, reentered the atmosphere reaching peak speeds around Mach 12, used its flaps to decelerate through transonic and subsonic phases, and splashed down in the North Pacific just over 3 hours after launch — over three times longer than any prior Starship mission, per the commentary. The splashdown was described as less soft than a previous mission's but the ship remained intact in the water. Commentators also noted a separate ship ("ship 40") was concurrently returning to Starbase, putting "one and a half Starships" in the water/in transit at once.
4. **Heat shield and tile experimentation continued:** As on previous flights, some heat shield tiles were deliberately removed or tested with different attachment methods on the leeward flap side (lower risk zone) to gather thermal protection data, and some tiles were painted white to serve as visual reference targets; Starlink satellites equipped with cameras again captured imagery of the heat shield during flight for engineering analysis.
5. **Reusability and cadence ambition:** SpaceX framed this flight as opening the path to full Starship reusability (return for catch and reflight) and stated an ambition for "a couple thousand more flights" in the near future, alongside plans to eventually fly with larger satellite payloads (from 26 up to 60 Starlink units per launch).

## SpaceX — Starlink
1. **First orbital deployment of Starlink V3 satellites:** The mission carried 26 Starlink V3 satellites and, following successful orbital insertion, deployed all 26 over roughly 30 minutes into what was described as their intended operational orbital shells — the first time V3 satellites were sent for actual constellation integration rather than a suborbital test. SpaceX said it established two-way communication (radio frequency and laser) with all 26 satellites shortly after deployment.
2. **V3 satellite capability gains over V2:** Each Starlink V3 satellite adds about 1 terabit per second of network capacity, so the 26-satellite batch added roughly 26 terabits of bandwidth — described as about 10x the capacity added by a single Falcon 9 launch of V2 Mini satellites. Additional stated improvements versus V2: roughly 10x higher upload bandwidth, roughly 22x higher uplink, and roughly 8x higher throughput (attributed to new quad-band radio antennas covering multiple frequency bands). Each V3 satellite also carries six 400-gigabit inter-satellite laser links. At a future 60-satellites-per-launch cadence, one Starship launch was said to add about 20x the network capacity of a single Falcon 9 launch.
3. **Manufacturing location:** All Starlink satellites, including V3, are built at SpaceX's Starlink facility in Redmond, Washington, which the speakers said is now producing more satellites than any other facility in the world.
