Tesla's Cybercab has received an EPA-certified efficiency rating of 165 Wh/mi, making it the most efficient electric vehicle ever rated — outpacing the next closest competitor, the Lucid Air Pure, by 28%. The article contextualizes why this matters for robotaxi economics while noting significant caveats around both the comparison and Tesla's autonomous driving readiness.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Cybercab efficiency rating and competitive context:** The Cybercab's 165 Wh/mi EPA-certified figure was confirmed by Tesla VP of Vehicle Engineering Lars Moravy. For comparison: Lucid Air Pure RWD (230 Wh/mi, +28%), Tesla Model 3 RWD (240 Wh/mi, +31%), Tesla Model Y RWD (240 Wh/mi, +31%), Hyundai Ioniq 6 (241 Wh/mi, +32%), Toyota bZ3X (260 Wh/mi, +37%), Tesla Model S AWD (270 Wh/mi, +39%). The article attributes the record to the Cybercab's purpose-built design: two seats, no steering wheel or pedals, teardrop aerodynamic body, and a sub-50 kWh battery pack. Tesla claims the pack can deliver close to 300 miles of range.

## Robotaxi
2. **Fleet economics advantage from efficiency:** At average US electricity rates of ~$0.16/kWh, the Cybercab's energy cost is approximately $0.026/mile, versus ~$0.038/mile for a Model 3 and ~$0.048/mile for a Hyundai Ioniq 5. The article notes the small battery pack also enables faster charging and lower per-vehicle battery costs. Tesla has stated a $30,000 unit price target for the Cybercab, with the efficient powertrain cited as central to hitting that figure.

3. **Production status and autonomy concerns:** Cybercab production began at Giga Texas in April 2026, though the ramp is described as initially slow. The article notes Tesla has not yet solved unsupervised autonomous driving — the first steer-wheel-less unit came off the line in February 2026 — and states that Tesla's supervised robotaxi fleet "currently crashes at roughly four times the rate of human drivers." The program has also seen a leadership exodus, with three senior leaders departing since February 2026.

## Editorial Stance
The author acknowledges the 165 Wh/mi rating as genuine engineering achievement but argues the efficiency record is "meaningless" if Tesla cannot solve the autonomy problem at scale, and cautions that comparing the Cybercab to conventional passenger cars is an unfair framing — akin to comparing motorcycle fuel economy to a sedan's.
