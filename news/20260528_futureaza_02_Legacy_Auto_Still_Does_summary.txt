This video from FutureAzA features host Brian and recurring guest Randy Kirk discussing why legacy automakers — particularly GM, Ford, and Stellantis — fundamentally misunderstand what Tesla is building and why their current investment strategies signal long-term decline. The core thesis is that legacy auto is doubling down on internal combustion technology (transmissions, V8 engines) while retreating from EVs, effectively forfeiting global markets and leaving themselves wholly unprepared for the autonomous vehicle disruption ahead.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **GM's EV retreat masked as domestic investment:** GM is investing heavily in transmission production — $300 million to increase capacity near Detroit, another $300 million the prior year (2025), $40 million for transmission production in Ohio, and over half a billion for propulsion plants in Ontario building sixth-generation V8 engines for the Corvette and full-size pickup trucks. GM noted total investments of more than $6 billion since 2025, largely geared toward the redesigned Silverado. Brian and Randy frame this as analogous to investing in steam engines — profitable in the short term but strategically backwards.

2. **GM and Samsung pause $3.5 billion battery plant:** GM and Samsung have confirmed a full pause on construction of their jointly planned $3.5 billion battery plant dubbed "Synergy Cells," with county officials citing economic factors and the end of EV incentives. The companies still nominally aim to meet a "substantial completion" deadline by end of 2027, but Brian clarifies this only means finishing the building shell — no equipment will be installed. He interprets this as a complete retreat from serious EV ambitions and a forfeiture of international markets where GM has competed for over 70 years.

3. **Ford's "all in" on EVs rings hollow:** Ford has written down $6 billion in EV-related charges and is effectively retreating to its ICE roots. Jim Farley's pivot back to gas was framed not as a cost-saving move but as a profitability move. Randy notes Ford leads all automakers in recalls by a wide margin with no apparent fix in sight. Brian jokes that claiming to be "all in" on EVs while still maintaining multiple combustion commitments and writing down billions doesn't reflect the meaning of the phrase.

4. **Stellantis ranked worst-positioned of the Big Three:** Both hosts agree Stellantis is in the worst strategic position — described as "rudderless," with weak EV offerings in the US market despite having better EV products in Europe that never make it stateside. Stellantis is reportedly spending $7 billion on retrofits and new vehicles but without a coherent direction.

## Market & Competition
1. **Legacy auto's failure to partner with Tesla:** Randy argues that any of the Big Three could have simply called Elon Musk, offered a royalty arrangement, and licensed Tesla's drivetrain or skateboard platform rather than spending billions developing flawed proprietary systems like GM's Ultium. Brian extends the analogy — a single proven skateboard platform on which to build multiple vehicles would have been far more efficient than what GM attempted with Ultium, which he describes as "one size fits all, except it's all size fits none."

2. **Autonomous vehicles as the deeper existential threat:** Randy argues the real long-term threat to legacy automakers isn't just electrification — it's autonomy. He predicts robotaxis could offer rides at 30 cents to $1 per mile (citing Elon Musk's public predictions at the low end), eliminating the need for personal car ownership for many consumers. Randy notes his own household has already reduced to one Tesla, relying on Uber for some trips despite the higher per-mile cost. He expects robotaxis to become massive within the next one to two years (i.e., by 2027–2028) and to begin cutting into all car sales broadly.

3. **Blockbuster/Nokia/Xerox analogies for legacy auto:** Randy repeatedly draws parallels between GM's trajectory and famous corporate failures — Blockbuster (still profitable as Netflix disrupted it), Nokia (dominant until Apple's iPhone arrived), and Xerox (invented the personal computer mouse but failed to develop it). The point in each case: being profitable today does not protect against rapid disruption, and legacy automakers may not see the future clearly enough to adapt.

4. **Global market forfeiture:** Brian argues that legacy automakers are retreating to their domestic strengths — high-margin trucks in the US — while surrendering international markets. Developed markets abroad demand competitive EVs that GM and Ford cannot provide, and developing markets want cheaper cars than legacy OEMs can profitably build. The pivot back to ICE is framed as an irreversible forfeiture of global automotive market share.
