Two commentators discuss why Tesla's Robotaxi expansion appears slow, arguing it reflects a deliberate strategy of accumulating driving data cautiously before scaling, while also covering cost economics, customer experience friction points, and how society might judge robotaxi safety statistics once fleets scale.

## Robotaxi
1. **Slow rollout as deliberate data-gathering strategy:** One speaker argues Tesla is intentionally moving slowly and staying within geo-fenced areas and limited times of day so it can accumulate enough driving miles that any future accident can be contextualized statistically (e.g., "we've driven a million miles" rather than just a handful), rather than appearing to have caused a disproportionate incident too early.
2. **13.6 billion supervised FSD miles seen as a foundation:** One speaker notes Tesla already has 13.6 billion supervised Full Self-Driving miles, arguing this dataset is valuable for Robotaxi even though it's supervised, since human drivers not intervening on the same roads provides comparable behavioral data.
3. **Learning from human driving patterns:** Using an Austin intersection example, the speakers describe Robotaxi software potentially adapting to human lane-choice patterns (e.g., most drivers taking a particular lane) even without knowing the underlying reason, though one speaker suggests the system should ideally evaluate why humans behave that way.
4. **Anticipating and avoiding others' mistakes and hazards:** A recurring theme is that Robotaxi/FSD doesn't just avoid causing accidents but proactively anticipates dangers — cited examples include avoiding children, pets, deer, and in Australia, kangaroos and wombats — which the speaker calls "astounding," while jokingly noting this could reduce roadkill enough to affect deer populations.
5. **Defensive driving lowers cost per mile:** Avoiding accidents through defensive maneuvers (e.g., not tailgating trailers, merging away from on-ramps) is framed as a direct financial benefit — accident avoidance reduces vehicle downtime and repair costs, which can be expressed in cents-per-mile, a metric one speaker says applies to all Robotaxi/Cybercab economics.
6. **Camera-based sensors cheaper to repair than Waymo's lidar/radar stack:** One speaker contrasts Waymo's protruding lidar/radar hardware, which is costly and time-consuming to repair when damaged, with Tesla's vision-only approach, claiming cameras can be swapped for roughly $25, keeping repair costs and downtime low.
7. **Customer experience as a distinct risk factor:** Beyond crash safety, the speakers highlight non-safety risks tied to rider experience — cars stopping mid-street, confusion at airport pickup lanes, locked doors preventing entry, and drop-off location requests (e.g., wanting to be dropped closer to cover in the rain) — as issues that must be resolved before wider scaling, with one speaker noting Elon Musk has discussed this tradeoff between scaling speed and refining these experience issues on recent calls.
8. **Safety reliability vs. experience reliability are distinct metrics:** One speaker distinguishes "safety reliability" (how often FSD encounters a collision-risk situation) from "experience reliability" (how often it does something frustrating, like poor parking placement), citing their own early Robotaxi ride where the car had to be chased into a parking lot blocks away — an issue they say no longer occurs today.
9. **Framing future accident statistics against a human baseline:** The speakers anticipate headlines highlighting large cumulative Robotaxi accident or fatality counts as fleets scale, but argue the more relevant comparison is against the (much higher) number of deaths/accidents that would have occurred if humans had driven the same miles — with one speaker speculating Tesla could save on the order of 20,000 lives a year if it cut fatalities by half, while questioning what percentage improvement society and regulators would consider acceptable.
