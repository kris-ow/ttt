This episode of the Cern Basher podcast features host Cern Basher and returning guest Phil Beisel covering a wide range of technology and investment topics centered on Tesla's FSD and robotaxi progress, the SpaceX IPO outlook, the explosive growth of AI compute demand, and a live demo of a networking app Phil Beisel built using AI-assisted coding tools. The core thesis is that SpaceX is the indispensable compute infrastructure company of the future — every major AI firm will eventually need SpaceX's orbital data centers and Starlink connectivity — and that Tesla's FSD and robotaxi deployment are quietly accelerating toward a tipping point that the market is not fully pricing in.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD Performance and Personal Usage:** Cern Basher reports actively using FSD and building a usage streak, noting that on a recent drive the odometer read 1,433 miles. He expresses strong personal trust in the system, saying he "doesn't even bother looking at the road much at all." Phil Beisel corroborates this sentiment, stating he feels more confident in the car on the highway than in urban settings, noting the irony that Tesla historically worried about highway incidents due to higher speed consequences, yet the incident rate on divided highways with FSD appears low.

2. **FSD Driving Modes and Edge Cases:** Phil Beisel describes testing FSD in "Mad Max" mode on a mountain road near South San Jose, noting it was too aggressive for that environment and he dialed it back. He recounts FSD performing a hard brake for a bird crossing its path — an event he found slightly over-tuned — as well as braking for a snake on the road. He frames these as evidence of how well-calibrated the safety layer is, while acknowledging there are still preference and operational nuances to refine.

3. **Grok Voice Integration — Hardware Constraint on AI4:** Phil Beisel analyzes the challenge of integrating Grok voice commands natively into robotaxi vehicles. He explains that currently all Grok inference — voice recognition, speech-to-text, AI processing, and text-to-speech — is handled in the cloud, which is unreliable for a robotaxi service that may not have constant connectivity. He argues this workload cannot borrow compute cycles from FSD (running at approximately 36 frames per second) without degrading safety performance. He gives only a 20% probability that full voice integration is achievable on the current AI4 hardware platform, and states his expectation is that it will require the AI5 chip, at which point he believes hardware will be "fully capable."

## Robotaxi

1. **Unsupervised Robotaxi Expansion to Greater Austin Area:** Cern Basher cites Tesla's Ashok (referencing a public post) announcing that unsupervised robotaxi service is now available in the greater Austin area, describing it as "robotaxi the way it was intended starts in earnest today in Austin." Both hosts frame this as a pivotal moment, with Cern Basher predicting the market is "about to transition into what will be an explosion of these things" and that it will "hit everybody like a ton of bricks" given how close the program is to rounding its first year of operation.

2. **Unsupervised Robotaxis Operating on Highways:** A notable detail surfaced in the discussion is that unsupervised robotaxis are now operating on highways within the geofenced Austin area — not just surface streets. Both hosts find this significant given Tesla's historical caution around high-speed autonomous operation, and note the irony that FSD is in many ways more reliable on highway segments than in complex urban environments.

3. **Operational Nuances of Robotaxi Service:** Phil Beisel argues that the safety box for robotaxi is "largely checked" and that the remaining challenges are operational — specifically the seamless passenger experience around pickup/dropoff navigation, dynamic route adjustments, and in-vehicle communication. He uses the analogy of typical Uber driver conversations (e.g., "drop me at door nine, not door six") to illustrate how much ambient human supervision currently compensates for gaps that a driverless system must handle automatically. He frames Grok voice integration as the key unlock for making the in-vehicle robotaxi experience feel complete.

4. **Robotaxi Tracker and Data Density:** Cern Basher briefly references the robotaxi tracker website (RobotaxiTracker.com) as a public tool tracking fleet counts, and speculates about future AI-powered tools that could provide granular, real-time views of Cybercab density by intersection, cross-referenced with Waymo data and other public sources — framing this as an example of what democratized AI-powered analysis tools could deliver.

5. **Validation Coverage of Geofenced Areas:** Phil Beisel theorizes that Tesla has likely run every road segment, intersection, and decision point within its geofenced operating polygon hundreds of times through autonomous systems as part of its validation process, building a comprehensive traversal graph. He notes that situational variability (weather, pedestrians, obstructions) is the remaining challenge beyond static road topology.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus as a World-Model Data Collector:** Phil Beisel frames Optimus as ultimately one of the most important data-collection platforms for building world foundation models. He argues that current LLMs are trained on written text — an extremely condensed and filtered version of reality — whereas what AI truly needs is continuous, real-world visual observation data. He states that when Optimus is deployed at scale, it will be able to observe the world "on a constant, continuous basis" in ways that humans and current AI systems have barely scratched the surface of, enabling far richer world models than text-based training allows.

2. **Jensen Huang's Robot Capability Slide:** Cern Basher references a slide from Jensen Huang's annual presentation plotting robot capability against compute and data volume, progressing from TFLOP-level classical simulation through internet plus synthetic data, to world foundation models. Both hosts note with emphasis that "Tesla vision tech" appears in the top-right quadrant of the chart — the highest capability, highest compute position — validating Tesla's data-driven FSD approach as the leading edge of real-world robotics training data.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX IPO Analysis — Historical IPO Comps:** Phil Beisel walks through a Market Watch table of major tech IPO performance, covering Facebook (down 45% at 3 months), Twitter (roughly flat at 12 months), Zoom (up 142% at 12 months), Uber (down 21% at 12 months), Palantir (up 153% at 12 months with a 53% drawdown), and Robinhood and Rivian (strong IPO period followed by decline). The average one-year return across the list was approximately 14%, with an average peak-to-trough drawdown of approximately 55% in the first year. Both hosts characterize the article's "scary chart" framing as purposefully negative clickbait, and suggest the more relevant question is 3–5 year performance.

2. **SpaceX as the Indispensable Compute Infrastructure Company:** Phil Beisel articulates the central thesis of the episode: every major AI company — Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Oracle, Apple — will ultimately need SpaceX to scale its compute. He argues that terrestrial chip production (including Terafab) will not be sufficient to close the compute supply-demand gap, that power availability on Earth is a binding constraint, and that orbital data centers with space-based solar arrays are not a fantasy but a necessity. He states: "Every single person in that capex chart has to come to SpaceX to get its business done."

3. **AI Capex Trajectory — Big Tech Spending:** Cern Basher presents a capex chart showing that Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Oracle, and Apple combined spent $165 billion in one recent year, rising to $268 billion the following year, then $468 billion in 2025. Projections show $785 billion for 2026 and just under $1 trillion for 2027, with the chart showing a flattening toward 2030 that both hosts dismiss as unrealistic given accelerating demand. Free cash flow for most companies on the list is projected to dip significantly during this period.

4. **Nvidia Vera Rubin — Early Adopters Signal Future Leaders:** Cern Basher highlights a Jensen Huang presentation slide showing early adopters of Nvidia's new Vera CPU (part of the Vera Rubin platform for agentic AI) as OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX. He interprets this as Jensen signaling the three fastest-growing companies of the next several years. Both hosts agree these will likely be the three largest IPOs of the next year. Phil Beisel notes the key distinction: OpenAI and Anthropic do not build data centers, while SpaceX is building both terrestrial and space-based data center infrastructure — meaning the former two must ultimately go through SpaceX for compute at scale.

5. **Anthropic–SpaceX Deal and OpenAI Competitive Risk:** Phil Beisel discusses Anthropic's deal to use SpaceX's Colossus data center, which he frames as strategically smart — effectively buying Anthropic an "e-ticket" into SpaceX's future orbital compute pipeline. He contrasts this with OpenAI, suggesting Sam Altman's company will be "starved out" of compute if it cannot secure a similar infrastructure relationship, drawing a parallel to Yahoo versus Google and Netscape versus Microsoft. He notes that Elon Musk previously referred to Anthropic as "Missanthropic" before the deal was announced, and expresses skepticism that a similar détente with OpenAI is forthcoming.

6. **Cursor AI and SpaceX Acquisition Option:** Phil Beisel mentions in passing that SpaceX holds an option to acquire Cursor AI, the coding platform he used to build his app, with a reported acquisition price of $60 billion and an option to walk away for $10 billion. He connects this to SpaceX/xAI's strategy of training Grok on coding data and techniques.

7. **Starlink as the Access Layer for Orbital Compute:** Phil Beisel argues that Starlink is not merely a consumer internet service but the access layer for future orbital compute infrastructure. He frames low-Earth-orbit satellites — currently around 10,000 and growing — as the connectivity backbone that will link users to space-based data centers, making terrestrial ISPs like Comcast progressively obsolete. He notes Starlink's round-trip ping to ground of approximately 17–18 milliseconds as evidence of the low latency that makes it viable for real-time applications.
