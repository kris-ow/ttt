Tesla has retroactively modified "Full Self-Driving" purchase agreements to add "supervised" language absent from the originals, and in some cases has made the original contracts entirely inaccessible — an issue Electrek confirmed with multiple owners holding HW3 vehicles and FSD purchases from before 2024.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **Contracts altered or inaccessible post-purchase:** Owner Oliver Abcarius discovered that his 2019 FSD purchase agreement (for a 2018 Model 3) and his wife's 2020 Model Y purchase agreement — both from the era when Tesla sold "Full Self-Driving Capability" without "supervised" language — now link to invalid pages. Every other document on both accounts opens normally; only those referencing FSD purchase details are inaccessible.

2. **Timeline of Tesla's FSD language shift:** From 2016 through early 2024, Tesla sold "Full Self-Driving Capability" for up to $15,000 with the promise of unsupervised autonomy via OTA updates. In March 2024, Tesla formally renamed the product "Full Self-Driving (Supervised)" with FSD v12.3.3. By September 2025, Tesla had abandoned the original autonomy promise, and in April 2026, Musk confirmed HW3 vehicles (produced 2016–2023) cannot achieve unsupervised FSD due to hardware limitations — with no concrete retrofit plan in place.

3. **Legal context — evidence relevance:** Tesla faces up to $14.5 billion in lawsuits covering FSD false advertising, Autopilot crash liability, and securities fraud. A certified US class action covers FSD statements made between October 2016 and August 2024. A California administrative ruling found Tesla engaged in false advertising on FSD. An arbitrator ordered Tesla to refund $10,600 to an FSD buyer for breach of contract. FSD fraud suits are also active in China and Europe. The now-inaccessible contracts are central evidence in these cases. Tesla also deleted a 2016 blog post — which promised full self-driving hardware on all vehicles — in August 2024 during the buildup of litigation; it remains accessible via the Wayback Machine.

## Editorial Stance
Author Fred Lambert argues that making executed contracts inaccessible during active litigation is legally distinct from a forward-looking product rename, raising the possibility of evidence spoliation — which courts can treat with adverse inferences or sanctions — and calls the pattern "hard to ignore." He urges pre-2024 FSD owners to check and document the accessibility of their original purchase agreements.
