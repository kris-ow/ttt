This video from the "brighterwithherbert" channel features host Herbert interviewing Larry Goldberg, a serial entrepreneur and venture capital investor, on why Tesla investors should be closely monitoring SpaceX. The core thesis is that Wall Street and many Tesla investors are treating SpaceX and Tesla as two separate companies when they are increasingly operating as a unified stack — sharing engineers, materials, factories, and chips — with their futures deeply intertwined. The conversation covers shared solar manufacturing ambitions, the Terafab joint chip fabrication project, orbital data centers, and the high-confidence prediction that a formal Tesla-SpaceX merger announcement is coming.

## Energy

1. **SpaceX Launching a Solar Cell Factory in Bastrop, Texas:** SpaceX has officially begun construction of what is described as one of the world's most advanced solar cell factories in Bastrop, Texas. The announcement was attributed to Noah Kohl, SpaceX's Director of Solar Production, who posted a job listing for a senior facilities engineer and described hands-on ownership of utilities, process equipment, and clean room systems. SC Robinson is credited with surfacing the information. The facility is being built on the south end of the Starlink terminal factory complex, with a 157,000-square-foot parking garage on the north end. Larry Goldberg noted he had visited the site nearly a year earlier when ground was being cleared.

2. **Elon Musk's 100-Gigawatt Solar Target — Each, Separately:** Elon Musk stated that both Tesla and SpaceX are each independently targeting 100 gigawatts per year of US solar cell manufacturing, with Musk estimating that goal would take approximately three years to achieve. Combined, the two companies would represent 200 gigawatts per year — a figure the host notes is roughly four times the entire US solar industry's installation output of approximately 50 gigawatts in 2024. Musk explicitly said the two teams are working separately, and Goldberg explained why: SpaceX solar cells are designed for space deployment (requiring rollable, compact form factors, different coatings, and materials suited for vacuum and deep cold), while Tesla's cells target earthbound rooftop applications.

3. **Different Solar Technologies, Shared Science:** Goldberg explained that while SpaceX and Tesla are building separate solar factories for distinct use cases, the underlying chemistry and scientific knowledge will be shared. Space-based solar cells must roll into compact bundles for launch, withstand a space environment with no weather or atmospheric attenuation, and handle temperature extremes (as low as 3 Kelvin in shadow). On Earth, Tesla's cells are static and designed for rooftop installation. Despite these differences, Goldberg expects the two teams to share formulations, coatings, and scientific expertise. He noted that Elon mentioned Tesla cells were previously manufactured with six zones and are now moving to 18 zones — representing a near-total redesign compared to past products.

4. **Tesla's Buffalo Solar Factory Already Scaling:** Tesla's solar manufacturing operations in Buffalo, New York are already active. The host noted that Tesla recently added 350 jobs in Buffalo as solar panel production began, invested $350 million in supercomputers on-site, and is scaling the Gigafactory New York facility toward 300 megawatts per year of production capacity. Goldberg expressed some skepticism that Buffalo's current scale would be sufficient for Tesla's ambitions, though he acknowledged the facility has expansion capability.

5. **Space-Based Solar Is Five Times More Effective Than Ground-Based:** Musk and Goldberg both discussed the physics advantage of orbital solar power. In space, solar panels receive approximately 30% more raw power due to the absence of atmospheric attenuation, and they operate continuously without day/night cycles, weather, or seasonality. The net effect, per Musk, is that a given solar panel in space generates approximately five times more energy than the same panel on Earth. The cold of space (3 Kelvin in shadow) also provides a highly efficient passive cooling environment for co-located AI compute hardware. Goldberg noted that beaming energy back to Earth from space does not appear economically viable in the near term.

6. **Orbital AI Data Centers: Five to Six Years Away:** Goldberg argued that orbital data centers — solar-powered AI compute platforms in low Earth orbit — are not a distant prospect but approximately five to six years away. He framed this as a near-term investment thesis rather than a speculative long-term vision, noting that AI data center economics are driven by chip cost (~80% of investment, per Brian Wang) and energy cost, both of which are dramatically improved in space. The remaining bottleneck is launch cost, which Goldberg expects Starship's full reusability to resolve by dropping launch cost from approximately $1,000 per ton to $30–$50 per ton.

## Market & Competition

1. **China Dominates Solar Manufacturing — For Now:** Goldberg and Musk both framed the SpaceX and Tesla solar manufacturing push explicitly as a response to Chinese dominance of global solar cell production. Goldberg noted that China "dramatically" brought down the cost of solar cell manufacturing, which contributed to Tesla's historically poor track record in solar. He argued that new technology — including perovskite cells, automated production, high-efficiency silicon, and the ability to print solar cells — is now shifting the competitive landscape and making US-based manufacturing economically viable again. The goal, Goldberg said, is to "take the leadership away from China."

2. **Starlink Terminal Factory Scale Exceeds Giga Texas in Automation:** Goldberg, who visited the Bastrop, Texas campus nearly a year before the video, described the Starlink terminal manufacturing facility as "gigantic" — larger in physical scale than Giga Texas — but with roughly one-tenth the number of employees per square foot, indicating a dramatically higher level of automation. He called it a pioneer for what highly automated US manufacturing can look like, and suggested that lessons from Bastrop will flow into Tesla's manufacturing roadmap, including potentially the Buffalo solar operation.

3. **SpaceX Launch Cost Advantage Over Competitors:** Goldberg provided a comparative framework for launch costs. He stated that Falcon 9 currently costs approximately 20% of what any other rocket costs (excluding New Glenn), making it roughly one-fifth the cost of alternatives. New Glenn is cheaper than Falcon 9 for very large payloads but is still insufficient for orbital data center economics. Starship with full reusability, he argued, will reduce costs to "fractions" of current Falcon 9 rates — from approximately $1,000 per ton today to $30–$50 per ton — at which point orbital infrastructure becomes cheaper to operate than equivalent ground-based infrastructure.

## Financials

1. **Terafab: The Joint Tesla-SpaceX Chip Fabrication Venture:** The host recapped Elon Musk's March 2026 announcement of Terafab, a joint chip fabrication facility between Tesla and SpaceX. Musk's stated goal is to produce over one terawatt of compute per year — encompassing logic, memory, and packaging all in one location — which he described as unprecedented. The allocation is 80% for space applications and 20% for ground. Goldberg called Terafab the most significant project Elon has ever undertaken, surpassing Starship and Optimus in scale and complexity, and argued it can only succeed if both companies operate as a single integrated unit rather than separate organizations coordinating across corporate lines. He framed the cost-sharing between Tesla and SpaceX on Terafab as effectively cutting the investment burden in half for each company.

2. **SpaceX–Tesla Merger: High-Confidence Prediction with Near-Term Timeline:** Goldberg stated with "101%" confidence that SpaceX and Tesla will formally merge, citing the operational integration already underway across chips, solar, compute, batteries, materials, and motors as making separate corporate structures untenable. He pointed to Dr. Charles Khman serving as Vice President of Materials Engineering at both companies simultaneously as evidence of how intertwined the organizations already are. A third party, identified as "Alexandra" (likely Alexandra Merz), is said to believe a merger announcement could come within 2026. Goldberg agreed an announcement is likely in 2026 but expects the process to extend through mid-2027 to fully resolve.

3. **SpaceX IPO Lockup Structure and Tesla Valuation Implications:** Goldberg offered detailed speculation on the mechanics of a SpaceX IPO lockup. He argued that a standard 3- or 6-month lockup is impractical given SpaceX's estimated valuation of approximately $1.7–$2 trillion, which would be larger than 80–90% of the market and would need to be included in major indices. He proposed that a rolling lockup structure is likely — beginning with 2–5% of shares becoming tradeable two weeks post-IPO, then gradually expanding over six months — to prevent a single-day flood of supply. During this period, he predicted SpaceX's public stock price would be "muted" relative to its ultimate value due to constant selling pressure, but that Tesla shareholders would have time to see Tesla's valuation rerate upward toward SpaceX multiples as the merger becomes inevitable.
