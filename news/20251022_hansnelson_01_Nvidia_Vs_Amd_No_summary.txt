This is an "hansnelson" podcast episode featuring guest Alex (of the YouTube channel "ticker symbol: U") discussing the Nvidia vs. AMD investment debate in AI hardware. The core thesis is that Nvidia and AMD are not really direct competitors — the AI compute market is growing so fast that both are supply-constrained rather than demand-constrained, with Nvidia pursuing an "all-to-all" multimodal strategy across training, post-training, and inference scaling laws, while AMD is focused mainly on capturing share in text-based inference workloads. ## Market & Competition
1. **Nvidia and AMD framed as non-competitors in a fast-growing market:** The guest argues the AI compute market is growing faster than either company can supply, so neither is racing the other on price or share — every chip either makes gets sold, making the "Nvidia vs. AMD" framing largely moot for investors.
2. **Revenue growth disparity cited from a third-party chart:** A chart attributed to Brad Gersonner (All-In podcast) reportedly showed Nvidia and AMD both near $23 billion in annual revenue a few years prior, with AMD now around $33 billion and Nvidia around $220 billion — roughly 10x growth for Nvidia versus 1.5x for AMD.
3. **AMD's OpenAI and Meta deals called disproportionately large:** The AMD-OpenAI deal involves delivering GPUs at a scale the guest estimates as roughly five times AMD's current annual data-center GPU shipments (citing AMD's ~200,000 GPUs/year) for just the first gigawatt milestone of a reported multi-gigawatt deal; he also notes a Meta 1-gigawatt data center announcement likely allocating a significant share to AMD's MI450, plus expected Oracle purchases — though he stresses this is a large, unproven step-up from AMD's current delivery scale.
4. **CUDA ecosystem lock-in as Nvidia's core moat:** The guest, drawing on his own academic/research background, argues Nvidia's advantage isn't raw hardware but the CUDA software ecosystem's switching costs (technical, emotional, and organizational), likening it to iPhone ecosystem lock-in versus Android.
5. **Nvidia vs. AMD strategic philosophy contrast:** The guest characterizes Nvidia (Jensen Huang) as pursuing new zero-to-hundred-billion-dollar markets (robotics, drug discovery, protein folding, materials science, fusion) to avoid disruption, per Clayton Christensen's innovator's dilemma framework, while AMD (Lisa Su) is described as focused on capturing share in existing, more fixed markets like inference and data-center CPUs — a strategy he compares to Steve Jobs' "infinite game" versus competitors chasing incremental parity.
6. **Guest's personal allocation view:** Asked how he'd allocate $1,000, the guest said he would put $900 in Nvidia and $100 in AMD, citing Nvidia's diversification (Nvidia's revenue is roughly 88% data-center per the guest, versus AMD's data-center revenue still smaller than its gaming revenue) and lower perceived risk of a "fatal mistake" analogous to Intel's decline.
7. **Lisa Su's AMD turnaround compared to Intel's self-inflicted decline:** The guest argues AMD did not "kill" Intel but filled a vacuum after Intel failed to invest in next-generation manufacturing technology and lost major clients (he recalls Apple as an example), and cautions against assuming Lisa Su's success against Intel implies she can similarly disrupt Nvidia, since Jensen Huang remains a founder-CEO actively avoiding the innovator's dilemma.
