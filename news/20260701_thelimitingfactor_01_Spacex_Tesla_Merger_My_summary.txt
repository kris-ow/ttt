This video from Jordan Giesige (The Limiting Factor) offers his personal perspective as a Tesla shareholder on the increasingly discussed prospect of a Tesla–SpaceX merger, following SpaceX's completion of its IPO. The core argument is that while a merger makes strategic sense in principle, the timing is poor for Tesla shareholders because Tesla's near-term robotaxi-driven revaluation is likely 1–2 years away, whereas SpaceX's comparable revaluation may be 2–4 years out — meaning a merger of equals now would dilute Tesla holders and delay their expected gains.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX IPO as the merger catalyst:** SpaceX recently completed its IPO, pricing shares at $135 per share, implying a market cap of approximately $1.77 trillion. The S-1 filed with the SEC showed the company is not currently profitable enough to justify a ~$2 trillion valuation on current earnings, but investors are pricing in rapid growth and an extra-planetary total addressable market (TAM) spanning space-based compute, lunar operations, and Mars colonization. Elon Musk's close associates are now describing a merger as imminent, and prediction markets have bets placed that it will occur before spring 2027.

2. **The "Schrödinger's Valuation" framing:** Jordan introduces the concept that stock valuations are inherently observer-dependent — a stock can be simultaneously over- and undervalued depending on the investing framework, risk tolerance, and time horizon applied. He uses this to frame the merger debate: each shareholder must independently decide whether a merger offer is fair and vote accordingly, because there is no single objective answer.

3. **Differing TAMs and timelines:** Jordan argues that SpaceX has a larger TAM due to its extra-planetary ambitions, but that tapping into it meaningfully depends on a fully and rapidly reusable Starship, which he estimates is roughly 1–2 years away if development goes well. After that, ramping launch cadence and converting that into meaningful profit will take additional time, all while SpaceX continues to consume capital for factories and launch infrastructure. He estimates SpaceX's stock could trade sideways for several years. Tesla, by contrast, has an earthbound but nearer-term TAM, with robotaxi revenues appearing on the horizon and a potential stock revaluation he expects within 1–2 years; he considers humanoid robots (Optimus) a longer-dated story, 2–4 years from generating significant revenue.

4. **Merger dilution risk for Tesla shareholders:** If a merger happened now, Jordan argues it would dilute Tesla shareholders by blending Tesla's near-term revaluation potential with SpaceX's longer-dated one, delaying potential gains by 2–3 years or more. He frames the time-value cost at roughly 15–20% annual opportunity cost — what the capital could earn elsewhere if not tied up in a sideways-trading merged entity. A merged company could also be net cash-flow negative if SpaceX's capital expenditure requirements exceed the combined FSD and Starlink revenue streams, potentially meaning the stock "flounders" for multiple years.

5. **Jordan's preferred merger terms:** If a merger does occur, Jordan states he would want either (a) at least $800 per share for Tesla stock, or (b) Tesla to represent at least 60% of the combined company's value. Under scenario (a) combined with the 60% framing, this would imply a ~$3 trillion valuation for Tesla and ~$2 trillion for SpaceX (approximately $150/share for SpaceX). He acknowledges that $800/share would mean forgoing the 2-year standalone gains he anticipates, but views it as a fair premium for accepting merger synergies. He notes the most likely deal is probably a merger of equals, which would imply Tesla shareholders receive roughly a 30–50% premium over the current share price — approximately $500–$600 per share.

6. **Post-merger portfolio strategy:** Jordan says he would hold through the merger period to capture whatever premium is offered, then reassess. His expectation is that he would sell a significant portion of the merged company's stock, redeploying capital into other positions he considers undervalued (he names Upstart and Lemonade as current holdings). He explicitly frames this as a rational, unemotional response — "there's no crying in the casino."

7. **Strategic rationale for a merger:** Jordan lists three reasons he supports a merger in principle: (1) it would reduce bureaucratic and regulatory overhead on Elon Musk; (2) it would eliminate duplicated resources between the two companies; and (3) a combined entity would have greater capacity to attract and retain talent. The overarching goal he attributes to Elon Musk is concentrating more cognitive capacity on execution and problem-solving while cutting costs. He also acknowledges Elon's perspective — that the combined companies could create such abundance that money itself becomes irrelevant — while noting that this vision will take decades regardless, making a 1–2 year delay to let Tesla realize its next revaluation event immaterial to the long-term mission.
