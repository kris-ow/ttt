Electrek published one Tesla-relevant article today, a critique of Tesla's camera-only autonomy approach.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Ex-Autopilot chief blasts vision-only self-driving:** Fred Lambert reports that Sterling Anderson, who led Tesla's Autopilot program in 2015–2016 and is now GM's Chief Product Officer, called vision-only self-driving "a weird religious stance that ignores physics" in an interview with The Verge. Anderson said radar is cheap enough that removing it makes no sense and that cameras can't see through dense fog or measure instantaneous velocity like radar can; he also said lidar helps camera systems improve faster when trained together, and that he avoids "eyes off" marketing language because it pushes drivers into complacency. GM's Cadillac Escalade IQ is due to debut an "eyes-off" system in 2028 using lidar, radar, and cameras. Electrek's article notes Tesla removed radar starting in 2021 and ultrasonic sensors in 2022 over internal engineer objections, cites Musk's past comments defending the vision-only approach, and reports NHTSA upgraded its investigation into FSD in reduced-visibility conditions to an Engineering Analysis covering 3,203,754 vehicles, now counting nine crashes including a pedestrian death in November 2023, with NHTSA finding FSD's camera degradation detection failed to flag conditions like sun glare, fog, and airborne dust before crashes. Fred Lambert's own "Take" argues Anderson is right, calling Tesla's vision-only bet a position rooted in Musk's 2019 dismissal of lidar as a "fool's errand" rather than first-principles engineering, and notes Tesla is now building its own fleet maps and running Robotaxi within geofences despite past criticism of competitors doing the same; he contrasts Waymo's roughly 271 million rider-only miles with Tesla's about 3 million driverless miles since removing safety monitors in January. ## Editorial Stance
Electrek's Fred Lambert is sharply critical of Tesla's vision-only strategy, framing it as an ideological choice by Musk rather than sound engineering, and points to NHTSA's investigation and mileage comparisons with Waymo as evidence the approach is lagging on safety.
