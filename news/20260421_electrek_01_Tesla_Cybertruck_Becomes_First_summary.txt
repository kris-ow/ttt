Tesla's Cybertruck has been approved to sell power back to California's grid through PG&E's residential Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) pilot program — marking the first AC-based vehicle-to-grid system approved for residential use in California. The approval includes up to $4,500 in incentives toward equipment and installation costs.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Cybertruck's AC-based Powershare system distinguishes it from competitors:** Unlike Ford and GM vehicles in the same PG&E pilot — which rely on DC bidirectional chargers costing $6,000–$10,000 — Tesla's Cybertruck uses AC power natively via a Powershare Gateway and Universal Wall Connector. This conventional residential hardware approach reduces cost and complexity. The Cybertruck's 123 kWh battery pack is equivalent to roughly nine Powerwalls, making it one of the largest mobile storage assets available to the grid. Tesla confirmed the new Model Y Performance has bidirectional charging hardware, but Powershare has not been activated on it yet.

## Energy
1. **California program structure and expansion from Texas:** PG&E's V2X pilot covers over 16 million people across Northern and Central California and offers up to 1,000 residential customer spots. Cybertruck owners can enroll in Powershare Home Backup (home outage power) and Powershare Grid Support (grid export during high-demand events), retaining control over discharge limits. This follows Tesla's February 2026 launch of Powershare Grid Support in Texas — its first U.S. V2G offering — covering CenterPoint Energy and Oncor service areas. Tesla's existing California Powerwall virtual power plant paid out nearly $10 million to participants in 2024 and has delivered over 100 MW to the grid during peak events.

## Market & Competition
1. **Ford and GM entered the PG&E pilot earlier but with costlier hardware:** The F-150 Lightning was approved in PG&E's V2X pilot in 2023, and GM's Ultium-based vehicles (Silverado EV, Equinox EV, Blazer EV, Cadillac Lyriq, GMC Sierra EV) joined in early 2025. All use DC bidirectional chargers requiring specialized, expensive infrastructure. Tesla's AC approach, if extended across its full vehicle lineup, could represent a substantially larger virtual power plant opportunity given California's EV market scale.

## Editorial Stance
The author argues that the AC vs. DC distinction is the key competitive differentiator — DC chargers at $6,000–$10,000 make V2G economics questionable for most homeowners, and Tesla's lower-cost approach could meaningfully accelerate adoption. The author cautions that Cybertruck is Tesla's lowest-volume vehicle, so this remains a proof of concept, with the real opportunity contingent on Powershare expanding to other Tesla models.
