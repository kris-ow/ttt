Electrek reports a reader-submitted photo of a pre-production Tesla Cybercab prototype spotted in Berkeley, showing a Cybertruck steering wheel bolted onto an exposed steering column via a temporary fabricated mount, offering the clearest public look yet at how Tesla equips its steering-wheel-less robotaxi design for public-road testing.

## Robotaxi
1. **Test mule hardware:** The spotted Cybercab has interior panels removed, revealing a Cybertruck steering wheel connected via a steering column to a fabricated mount with an adjustable rail — described as the kind of temporary rig used for a car with no factory driver provision. The car carried a Texas manufacturer plate and a "CYBERCAB" vanity plate, finished in a gold-ish wrap Tesla has used on other test units.

2. **Design vs. testing contradiction:** Tesla unveiled the Cybercab in October 2024 as a purpose-built two-seat robotaxi with no steering wheel, pedals, or mirrors, targeting a cost under $30,000, and began production at Giga Texas earlier this year. However, prototypes actually driven on public roads require a human driver and thus need real controls (the grafted Cybertruck wheel), while production units built without controls rely on a touchscreen virtual joystick for depot movement and cannot be deployed at scale or sold. The article notes regulations for testing autonomous prototypes on public roads generally require a human able to take over.

3. **Reader observation on size:** The reader who spotted the car in person, Adam Machacek, said he was surprised by its compact, city-friendly proportions, remarking that "one can not stop thinking this was originally meant to be the 'cheap' Tesla" — noting the Cybercab rides on the same next-generation platform once associated with Tesla's planned $25,000 vehicle before that project became the robotaxi.

## Editorial Stance
Electrek's Fred Lambert argues the photos capture a core contradiction: Tesla is mass-producing a car whose entire premise is autonomous operation, yet the units actually testing on public roads still need a human driver with a bolted-on steering wheel, while the "finished" production units can't yet be sold or deployed at scale. He states this is normal for engineering prototypes generally, but argues Tesla is unusual in having sold the Cybercab concept as finished two years ago while mass-producing it before the software that justifies it exists.
