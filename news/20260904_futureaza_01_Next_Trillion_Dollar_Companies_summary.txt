This is a discussion-format video (futureaza host Brian with guest Nick Gibbs of "Investing Against the Grain") debating whether Tesla and SpaceX should merge, and if so, when. Nick argues Tesla hasn't yet proven out the businesses (robotaxi, Optimus) that are supposed to justify its valuation, while SpaceX's Starship progress and data-center/compute rental revenue make it comparatively more "proven"; Brian counters that SpaceX isn't yet profitable and that its compute-rental revenue durability is uncertain given some AI compute customers reportedly transacting at a loss, while Tesla is already profitable and has real (if slow) FSD, robotaxi and Optimus progress underway.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation / Financials
1. **Merger debate — no consensus reached:** Nick argues Tesla shareholders don't yet "deserve" a merger premium because Tesla hasn't proven out robotaxi/Optimus revenue at scale, while SpaceX is closer to proving its business (Starship progress, data-center rental profits). Brian argues the reverse — Tesla is already profitable while SpaceX is not, and the durability of SpaceX's AI/compute rental revenue is unproven since some major compute buyers may be transacting at a loss; a market correction among AI compute buyers could hit that revenue. Brian says he doesn't see the urgency to merge now versus later, given cross-pollination (e.g. Panasonic) doesn't require a merger.
2. **Valuations cited in the debate:** Nick states Tesla is "worth about a trillion" and SpaceX "as of today is right at two trillion," and says a merger at that gap could deliver Tesla shareholders a premium if structured as a merger of equals (attributed to a "Larry Goldburg" framing raised by Nick, unconfirmed by the host).
3. **SpaceX profitability timeline claim:** Nick says he expects SpaceX to "start to show profits come September" and speculates that by year-end SpaceX could be more profitable per quarter than Tesla — his own forward projection, not a confirmed figure.
4. **Rationale for merger timing — control, not synergy:** Nick argues the real reason a merger might happen now is that Elon Musk wants consolidated control, suggesting recent Delaware legal developments around Tesla's board/voting structure alarmed Musk about potential loss of control, and that waiting longer (as companies grow larger and more independently profitable, per the Ma Bell/AWS-Amazon analogy) would make a merger politically and structurally harder to execute.
5. **SpaceX investor call praised; contrasted with Tesla's:** Nick and Brian both praise a recent SpaceX investor call as unusually clean and well-run (crediting Gwynne Shotwell), contrasting it favorably with Tesla's earnings calls; Brian notes Elon personally intervened during the call to ensure a reporter got a specific number before moving to the next question, calling it uncharacteristic engagement from Musk on the details of that call.
6. **Retail ownership shift cited:** Brian states Tesla's shareholder base used to be 60–70% retail and is now about 30%, asserting that a meaningful share of large Tesla investors he talks with weekly have sold off at least a third of their positions due to prolonged stock stagnation and better-performing alternatives (Nebius, CoreWeave, Micron, Sandisk, Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, Google).

## Market & Competition
1. **Tesla execution vs. stock performance:** Brian argues that while TSLA stock has been flat for shareholders over several years, execution has progressed — citing Optimus expected to ship from Fremont manufacturing sometime in the next year, robotaxi expected to scale soon, and FSD revenue continuing to climb, with "well over a thousand" FSD-equipped units deployed nationally awaiting activation (his figures, not independently verified in the transcript).
2. **Nvidia as a Berkshire-style holding company:** Nick argues Nvidia is emerging as a "future Berkshire Hathaway" by taking equity stakes in AI companies (citing stakes in Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, CoreWeave, and Nimbix as examples) rather than just selling chips, and separately notes Nvidia sometimes offers chip discounts in exchange for stock, which Nick describes as also inflating chip pricing/demand perception for other customers.
