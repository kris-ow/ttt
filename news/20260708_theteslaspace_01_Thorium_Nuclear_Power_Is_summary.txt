This video is an educational deep-dive into thorium-based molten salt nuclear reactors, featuring a visit to Copenhagen Atomics in Denmark. It explains the physics of nuclear fission, contrasts traditional uranium reactors with thorium "breeding" technology, and covers Copenhagen Atomics' reactor design, safety features, waste profile, and commercialization timeline. Tesla is mentioned only once, as a manufacturing analogy.

## Energy
1. **Thorium as a nuclear fuel alternative:** Thorium is a naturally radioactive heavy metal that is three to four times more abundant than uranium in the Earth's crust, but it is not directly fissionable — it must first be "bred" into uranium 233 by absorbing a neutron and undergoing radioactive decay (thorium 232 → protactinium 233 → uranium 233), a process taking roughly an hour and then a couple of months.
2. **Copenhagen Atomics' molten salt reactor design:** Founded by Thomas Peterson, the company dissolves nuclear fuel into molten salt heated to between 500–1,000°C, which flows like water and can be continuously filtered to remove fission waste products, improving fuel efficiency versus solid fuel rods. The reactor uses an "onion core" design with a central water-cooled layer, a uranium fuel salt layer, and an outer thorium "blanket" layer, and requires no control rods — safety is achieved via gravity-drained dump valves.
3. **Waste and safety profile:** Copenhagen Atomics projects about 1,000 times less nuclear waste than a traditional reactor; while the waste is more radioactive short-term, it decays to safe levels in roughly 200 years versus roughly 20,000 years for traditional nuclear waste. The company also believes it can eventually source uranium from spent traditional fuel rods, converting long-cycle waste into short-cycle waste.
4. **Manufacturing and deployment plans:** Each reactor core is designed to fit inside a standard 40-foot shipping container, intended to be mass-manufactured on a production line explicitly modeled on the Tesla Gigafactory approach to automation, with an initial goal of producing one reactor per day. Reactors would be certified for 5 years of continuous operation before replacement, versus 2-3 years for traditional reactor refueling shutdowns.
5. **Development timeline:** Copenhagen Atomics has built two full-scale non-nuclear prototypes; a third prototype will test liquid thorium and uranium salt flow at 600°C without a nuclear reaction. If successful, the company hopes to run its first energy-generating fission test the following year, with a goal of having the first 10 commercial reactors on the market in 2028. China is also cited as investing heavily in molten salt thorium reactor development.
