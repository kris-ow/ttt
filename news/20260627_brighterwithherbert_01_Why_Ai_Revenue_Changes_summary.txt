This video features futurist and science blogger Brian Wang (Next Big Future) arguing that the most important near-term opportunity for Tesla and SpaceX shareholders is not robotaxi deployment but rather AI compute and frontier model revenue. Wang contends that a Tesla–SpaceX merger would unlock compounding AI revenue — via data centers, inference rental, and frontier model monetization — that dwarfs what Tesla can realistically achieve from robotaxi and Optimus hardware buildout on its own timeline. The episode frames the merger question not as a matter of stock price fairness but as a strategic bet on who wins the AI race.

## Financials

1. **Tesla and SpaceX current financial positions:** Wang cites Tesla at approximately $1.4 trillion market cap with roughly $44 billion in cash and low debt. SpaceX is cited at approximately $2 trillion with over $100 billion in cash. Tesla's 2025 total revenue is referenced at $96 billion (approximately $70 billion from cars, just under $14 billion from energy, and $12.5 billion from services), with net profit of approximately $3.6 billion. For 2026, Wang projects Tesla revenue heading toward approximately $126 billion, describing this as an optimistic scenario assuming strong Q3 and Q4 performance.

2. **SpaceX AI revenue trajectory:** Wang asserts SpaceX is already at approximately $28–$46 billion in annual revenue for 2026 (up from $18.7 billion the prior year), driven heavily by AI compute rental. He projects SpaceX reaching approximately $180 billion in annual revenue by end of 2027 in a conservative scenario — without Cursor significantly contributing — and notes that figure would be roughly equivalent to Tesla's entire 2026 run rate. In a bull scenario where Cursor and Grok scale to Anthropic-level revenues, Wang suggests a combined SpaceX/XAI revenue trajectory approaching $1–$2 trillion by end of 2027, though he acknowledges this is a high-end scenario.

3. **Combined capex potential:** Wang estimates that separately, SpaceX/XAI could deploy $30–$60 billion in capex over the next 12 months, while Tesla could deploy $10–$25 billion (citing Tesla's stated goal of reaching $25 billion in capex). Combined, he argues they could easily achieve 25% more capital deployment, reaching $50–$100 billion, and use that scale to build additional gigawatt-scale AI data center projects — each of which he values at $100 billion or more per year in rental revenue.

## Market & Competition

1. **AI revenue vs. robotaxi revenue — the core reframe:** Wang's central thesis is that one million fully deployed robotaxis at maximum revenue of $50,000 each yields approximately $50 billion — a figure he says does not overwhelm the AI compute and frontier model opportunity. He emphasizes that SpaceX's terrestrial data center rental revenue alone could approach Tesla's entire company run rate by end of 2027, making robotaxi economics look modest by comparison. He notes that Anthropic is already at a roughly $50 billion run rate, and argues that if XAI's Grok and Macrohard (a joint Tesla/SpaceX AI product) can match that, the revenue arrives faster than robotaxi hardware can physically scale.

2. **Colossus rental deals as evidence of AI monetization speed:** Wang points to SpaceX/XAI already renting out Colossus compute to Google and Anthropic, with a deal cited for up to $50 billion per year for Colossus 2. He also references a deal with Reflection AI for up to $6.3 billion. He contrasts the chip-sale economics ($30 billion to build a $50 billion data center) with frontier model economics, estimating that owning the frontier model layer generates roughly 10x the chip/rental revenue — framing the chip money as roughly $30 billion versus intelligence monetization at roughly $300 billion.

3. **XAI–SpaceX merger as the structural template:** Wang uses the XAI acquisition by SpaceX (valued at approximately $250 billion, following a fundraise at approximately $230 billion) as an analogy for why a Tesla–SpaceX merger makes strategic sense. He argues the Cursor acquisition — which he says brought in Fortune 500 distribution, coding agent capabilities, and access to orbital data center commitments — would not have happened if Cursor had only received private XAI shares rather than public SpaceX shares. Cursor is described as already generating approximately $4 billion in annual run rate and doubling every four to six months.

4. **Antitrust timing risk:** Wang flags that delaying a merger raises DOJ antitrust exposure. Once Tesla is clearly dominant in autonomous transport and physical AI, and once XAI/Grok is proven competitive with Anthropic and OpenAI, regulators would be far more likely to block a combination of two AI leaders. He argues the window to merge without antitrust obstruction may be limited to the next few months.

## Robotaxi

1. **Current fleet scale and ramp skepticism:** Wang acknowledges the active robotaxi fleet is currently in the "less than a few hundred" range (consistent with RobotaxiTracker.com's figure of 748 active vehicles as of 2026-06-25). He expresses skepticism about a rapid ramp to one million units, saying he cannot see that happening imminently — his bull case is perhaps 100,000 robotaxis if things "really start popping off," which he says has not occurred yet. He frames the key uncertainty as: how many more months or years will Tesla be stuck in the early-ramp scenario before achieving meaningful robotaxi takeoff?

2. **Robotaxi revenue ceiling vs. AI:** Even at one million robotaxis generating maximum revenue of $50,000 each — yielding $50 billion — Wang argues this does not change the calculus in favor of staying separate from SpaceX, because AI compute and frontier model revenue can reach that scale sooner and with less capital-intensive hardware buildout.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus and Digital Optimus as slow-burn opportunities:** Wang acknowledges Optimus and "Digital Optimus" (a software/agent product described as a joint Tesla–SpaceX initiative) as potentially important, but characterizes them as significant slow-burn opportunities rather than near-term needle-movers. He argues that physically building humanoid robots at scale is slower than the proven build rate for AI data centers. He suggests Optimus deployment could help accelerate factory operations and megapod physical installation, but cautions that meaningful revenue impact is likely beyond the six-to-24-month window under discussion.

2. **Macrohard as the wildcard:** Wang repeatedly references "Macrohard" — described as a joint Tesla/SpaceX AI product (with Tesla providing hardware and SpaceX/XAI providing software/models) — as a potential near-term catalyst. He notes that a demo is reportedly expected within three months (i.e., by approximately September 2026). He had discussed this with Jo Bhakdi and characterizes it as "the thing no one's talking about." If Macrohard proves dramatically superior to Anthropic and OpenAI products, he believes it could generate Anthropic-level revenues quickly, especially given Cursor's existing Fortune 500 distribution.

## Energy

1. **Tesla–Sunrun virtual power plant deal:** Wang references a deal announced (at or near the time of recording) between Tesla and Sunrun, described as a US-wide virtual power plant aggregating approximately 16 gigawatts of distributed resources across approximately four million homes at roughly $400 per participant. He estimates this generates a few hundred million dollars in near-term revenue and characterizes it as not materially moving Tesla's needle on its own.

2. **Virtual power plant as a leverage tool for AI data centers:** Wang's more significant point is that this deal gives Tesla/SpaceX a negotiating asset with utilities — specifically, the ability to offer grid stability (blackout prevention) in exchange for prioritized permitting or power allocation for new AI data centers. He argues this indirect benefit — enabling faster data center buildout — is worth billions per month in potential AI revenue, vastly exceeding the direct virtual power plant revenue itself.

3. **Megapods at supercharger sites:** Wang highlights Elon Musk's announcement that Tesla plans to deploy megapods (distributed AI compute modules) at supercharger sites, citing over one million potential locations. Each megawatt of deployed megapod capacity is estimated at approximately $100 million per year in rental revenue at Rubin-chip-level pricing. Wang frames this as a Tesla capability that becomes far more valuable — and deployable faster — under a combined balance sheet with SpaceX's capital and AI model monetization infrastructure.
