This video from brighterwithherbert argues that Tesla is following Amazon's historical playbook: deliberately absorbing near-term losses via heavy reinvestment while hidden, high-margin businesses (services, energy, robotaxi) compound underneath a market that still prices it as a low-margin car company. Drawing on Amazon's AWS disclosure in 2015 as the template for a future re-rating event, the host lays out four "pass/fail" tests and three possible re-rating triggers, while also engaging with Michael Burry's bearish AI-bubble short thesis and Tesla's high valuation multiple as counterarguments.

## Financials
1. **Q2 earnings beat on revenue, miss on EPS:** Tesla's Q2 earnings, released July 22nd, showed revenue of $28.2 billion (up 26% year-over-year), beating estimates, while EPS came in at 33 cents versus 49 cents expected — a miss the host attributes to heavy reinvestment rather than weakness, citing capital spending of $5.79 billion for the quarter (up 142%) plus additional multi-billion-dollar spending on Optimus production this year (exact full-year Optimus capex figure not disclosed).
2. **Services and energy growing at software-like rates:** Services revenue was $4.58 billion in the quarter, up 50% at record gross margins; energy deployment reached 13.5 gigawatt-hours, up more than 40% year-over-year — segments the host argues the market still files under "car company" rather than valuing as distinct high-margin businesses, akin to AWS before its 2015 disclosure.
3. **Valuation counterpoint — high forward multiple:** Tesla trades around 156 times forward earnings; the host acknowledges this is a legitimate bear argument that part of a future re-rating may already be priced in, unlike Amazon's cheaper valuation in the mid-2010s before AWS was broken out.
4. **TSLA stock has been flat for five years:** The host notes TSLA trading near $389 (as cited in the video) is roughly where it first traded five years ago, framing this stagnation as analogous to Amazon's post-dot-com decade of sideways/underwater performance before its AWS-driven re-rate.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **FSD subscriptions scaling as recurring revenue:** FSD subscriptions hit about 1.5 million, up 56% year-over-year — cited as evidence of a recurring, software-margin revenue stream growing independently of car unit sales.

## Robotaxi
1. **Dual-fleet plan compared to Amazon's FBA marketplace:** The host highlights Tesla's stated plan for a dual robotaxi fleet — company-owned Robotaxis (including Cybercab) plus private owners adding their FSD-equipped cars to the network — as structurally similar to Amazon's Fulfillment by Amazon model, where third-party capital and inventory scaled Amazon without added CapEx. The host stresses this owner-fleet participation is still on the roadmap, not yet live; today's fleet is company-owned only.
2. **Robotaxi rides expanding across markets with no dedicated revenue line item:** Robotaxi rides are running in Austin, Dallas, Houston, and three Florida cities, but there is currently no separate financial disclosure for robotaxi revenue — the host treats the appearance of such a line item in a future quarterly report as the key "disclosure" signal that would mirror Amazon's 2015 AWS breakout.
3. **Cost-per-mile crown framed as a forecast, not yet proven:** The host explicitly flags that Tesla's claimed lowest-cost-per-mile advantage (via purpose-built Cybertruck/Cybercab design, wireless charging, and vertical chip integration) is currently a forecast pending Cybercab volume production ramp, not a demonstrated fact.

## Market & Competition
1. **Michael Burry's bear case cited and partially countered:** The host reports Michael Burry is currently short Nvidia, Tesla, and the semiconductor index, betting on a 30–40% crash by March 2027, arguing AI infrastructure spenders are barely up while chip sellers have risen sharply — a claim the host says a third-party breakdown ("Bull Theory") covered. The host partially counters that Tesla sits on the "spender" side of Burry's own framework, and that its flat five-year stock price means an AI valuation premium has largely not been priced in, unlike some AI infrastructure peers.
