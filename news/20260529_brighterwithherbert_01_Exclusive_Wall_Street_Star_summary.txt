This video features an extended interview with Alex Potter, Managing Director and Senior Research Analyst at Piper Sandler, discussing his firm's newly released report titled "The Definitive Guide to Investing in Tesla" — a 77-page second edition that models 17 separate Tesla revenue streams out to 2045 using a detailed DCF framework. The core thesis is that Tesla's modeled businesses (excluding Optimus and distributed inference) already justify approximately $400 per share, meaning the market is effectively giving Optimus away for free at current prices around the $450s. Joining the conversation are hosts Herbert, Alexandra Merz, and Jeff Lutz, who probe Potter on robotaxi economics, FSD licensing, the competitive landscape, a potential SpaceX-Tesla merger, and China's role in the EV and robotics ecosystem.

## Robotaxi

1. **Bottom-up city-level ridership model:** Potter describes a rigorous methodology starting with all global cities of 500,000 people or more — approximately 900 cities. For each city, the model estimates population, per-capita one-way trips, the share made by car, the share eventually captured by robotaxi, and Tesla's share of that robotaxi market. Many cities (e.g., Pyongyang) were zeroed out entirely. This demand-side approach was used to back-calculate the number of vehicles Tesla would need to produce rather than starting from a vehicle production capacity figure.

2. **Tesla assumed at 50% robotaxi market share in a duopoly:** Potter explicitly assumes a duopolistic outcome in the global robotaxi market, with Tesla capturing approximately 50% market share. He does not name the second dominant player but implies a large Chinese platform as the likely counterpart, given China's ability to replicate successful business models with a domestic cost structure.

3. **Cybercab production peaks around 2 million per year in the mid-2030s:** Potter's model backs into a peak annual Cybercab production of approximately 2 million units per year, which he notes is close to the capacity figures Elon Musk has discussed. This peak production level is not reached until the mid-2030s in his forecast.

4. **Global active robotaxi fleet reaches 14–15 million by 2045:** By the end of the 20-year forecast period, Potter's model projects approximately 14 to 15 million active Tesla robotaxis globally. He notes this reflects a ride-hailing framing rather than full transportation-as-a-service, with the latter partially captured through personal vehicle FSD subscriptions.

5. **Vehicle useful life assumed at six years with straight-line depreciation:** Potter states that in his model, Cybercabs are assumed to stay in service for six years, with depreciation spread over that period. He identifies this depreciation assumption as a significant driver of cost per mile calculations.

6. **Cost per mile modeled at 25 cents:** Working from first principles — charging station output and build cost, connectors per station, sessions per day, wholesale electricity cost, cleaning costs, depreciation per mile, and other inputs — Potter's model arrived at approximately 25 cents per mile as the cost to deliver a robotaxi ride. He notes this aligns with Tesla's own stated targets and validates the directional accuracy of those one-liner figures.

7. **Robotaxi becomes ~45% of revenue and nearly half of gross profit by 2045:** The report projects that by 2045, robotaxi rides will represent approximately 45% of Tesla's total revenue and close to half of all gross profit. By contrast, new car sales become "basically a rounding error" by that point.

8. **Model previously assumed asset-light software subscription approach:** Potter acknowledges that in an earlier version of his model, he assumed Tesla would not own or manage a robotaxi fleet but would instead sell Cybercabs to third parties and monetize through self-driving software subscriptions. He has since revised this view, now modeling Tesla as directly owning and operating the fleet and selling rides.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD subscription priced at $99/month — Potter calls it "quasi-free":** Potter uses FSD himself daily and argues that $99 per month — approximately $1,200 per year — dramatically underprices the productivity value being delivered. He draws a parallel to Steve Ballmer's dismissal of the iPhone, suggesting most people today similarly underestimate FSD's transformative value. He expects the monthly price to rise substantially as capability becomes undeniable.

2. **FSD licensing to other OEMs modeled to grow to ~$400 billion by 2045:** The report models FSD licensing to third-party automakers as a revenue stream that eventually scales to approximately $400 billion — described as roughly "four Teslas" in revenue terms. However, the inflection point for this licensing business is pushed out to roughly the mid-2030s due to the multi-year process OEMs would face: first acknowledging the need (perhaps around 2029), then redesigning vehicles around Tesla's electrical architecture, cameras, and compute.

3. **One in three vehicles on Earth modeled on Tesla's platform by 2045:** By the end of the forecast horizon, Potter's model assumes approximately one out of every three vehicles globally is built around Tesla's electrical architecture, sensors, and compute. He notes this is an assumption subject to geopolitical uncertainty and acknowledges the number could be 20% or 40% rather than ~33%.

4. **Western OEMs expected to eventually capitulate and license FSD:** Potter argues that Western OEMs are using a "Franken-monster" approach to self-driving — relying on consortia and tier-one suppliers — and will ultimately fail to match Tesla's cost-effectiveness and integration. He draws an analogy to the Supercharger network adoption, where Ford and others eventually switched to NACS after resisting, and predicts the same domino effect will occur with FSD licensing. He suggests the first OEM to announce FSD licensing could see a significant stock re-rating.

5. **S-curve timing shifts have outsized DCF impact:** Potter explicitly notes that shifting his robotaxi and FSD licensing S-curves forward or backward by one to three years has a significant effect on the DCF valuation due to time value of money, even if the long-run endpoints remain the same.

6. **Current FSD version meaningfully reduces driver nag:** Co-host Jeff Lutz mentions he is on FSD version 14.3.3 and describes a meaningful reduction in the nag prompt in the most recent release, saying he is now able to read emails and messages during drives — corroborating Potter's description of FSD nearing a genuine productivity unlock.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Optimus excluded from the $400/share valuation — Potter calls it too hard to model:** Potter explicitly excludes Optimus from his DCF, not out of skepticism but because he cannot conceptualize a credible bottom-up model. He says he genuinely believes Elon Musk's characterization of Optimus as an "infinite money glitch" that will make GDP metrics and labor market concepts unrecognizable, but that the scope of disruption makes it nearly impossible to put into a spreadsheet. His threshold was: if the 17 modeled businesses already justified a valuation above current stock price, he did not need to force an Optimus number.

2. **Optimus deployment in China raises US national security questions:** The panel raises the question of whether the US government will permit Tesla to manufacture Optimus robots in China given their strategic importance as humanoid AI systems. Potter and the co-hosts agree that China will likely want Tesla to build Optimus there, but flag US regulatory restrictions as the key open question — a dynamic distinct from EVs and software.

3. **Very few companies will survive the humanoid filter:** Jeff Lutz argues that while hundreds of companies may attempt to build humanoid robots, the number that can credibly combine a capable AI brain, manufacturable hardware, and cost-effective production will fit on "one or two hands." Potter agrees.

## Market & Competition

1. **China's EV success traced directly to Tesla's factory entry:** Potter gives a detailed account from his time covering China pre- and post-Tesla. Before Tesla's 100%-owned Giga Shanghai opened (he references the 2017–2018 period), Chinese EV companies were copying 20th-century Western OEM supply chains, relying on tier-one suppliers, and producing vehicles that nobody voluntarily bought. Government subsidies were being gamed. Tesla's arrival gave Chinese companies a modern, vertically integrated template to copy — and within a few years, companies like BYD, NIO, Li Auto, Leap Motor, and Xpeng had built compelling EVs with domestic cost structures.

2. **Chinese upstream battery and mineral supply chain was already being built pre-Tesla:** Potter acknowledges a significant blind spot in his earlier China analysis: while the EV vehicles were poor quality and subsidy-driven, Chinese investment in actual lithium and graphite refineries upstream was real and long-term. Today, critical battery materials can essentially only be refined in China, giving it a structural supply chain advantage that predates Tesla's involvement.

3. **Rivian identified as the closest Western analog to Tesla's vertical integration approach:** Potter credits Rivian with taking a page from Tesla's playbook — vertically integrated, direct consumer relationship, in-house electronics and electrical architecture. He is skeptical of their continued reliance on lidar (calling it an unnecessary "crutch") but distinguishes them favorably from OEMs that outsource their core technology stack. He notes Rivian lacks Tesla's massive, purpose-built data-harvesting fleet.

4. **Volkswagen's investment in Rivian cited as "anything but Tesla" strategy:** Alexandra Merz references Volkswagen's investment in Rivian as emblematic of OEMs doing "anything but Tesla" despite the mathematical reality that Tesla's approach is superior. Potter does not dispute this characterization.

5. **Chinese cars increasingly visible in European markets:** Alexandra Merz reports observing numerous BYD SUVs and Omoda vehicles in Italy during a recent visit, parked alongside BMWs and Mercedes at roughly half the price — evidence of China's export juggernaut reaching even brand-loyal European markets.

6. **Tesla's supply chain ranked #14 globally by Gartner in 2023, #1 among automakers:** Jeff Lutz cites a 2023 Gartner supply chain ranking placing Tesla at number 14 globally and first among all automakers — ahead of Toyota — as evidence of Tesla's underappreciated hardware and manufacturing capability. He argues Tesla's internal AI and robotics adoption (referencing Terafab and acquired robotics/hardware companies) will further widen this lead.

## Financials

1. **DCF covers 17 revenue streams over a 20-year horizon to 2045:** The Piper Sandler model is built bottom-up with 17 distinct revenue and gross profit streams, each modeled with specific assumptions about ramp timing, pricing, cost structure, and market penetration. Potter describes it as extremely detailed — the model takes roughly 30 seconds just to open due to file size.

2. **Sum-of-parts value approximately $400/share excluding Optimus and distributed inference:** After modeling all 17 businesses, Potter's DCF yields approximately $400 per share. Since Tesla was trading around the $450s at the time of the report, the delta implies the market is assigning a positive value to Optimus and other excluded items (like distributed inference) — or equivalently, getting them "for free."

3. **Conservative assumptions driven by regulatory uncertainty, not technical skepticism:** Potter stresses that his conservatism in modeling robotaxi and FSD licensing timelines reflects uncertainty about regulatory and societal responses — particularly outside the US — rather than any doubt about Tesla's technical capability. He explicitly says the technical lead is real and the cost structure is superior.

## Market & Competition (SpaceX-Tesla Merger)

1. **Potter personally expects a merger to ultimately happen:** Potter says that if asked years ago whether all of Elon Musk's companies would eventually be one entity, his assumption was always yes. He views the companies as pursuing the same existential mission, with extensive engineering cross-pollination already in place. He does not see Elon mentally delineating between them.

2. **Alexandra Merz projects merger announcement in three months, completion in Q2–Q3 2027:** Merz shares her specific scenario: an announcement approximately three months from the video's publish date (i.e., around August–September 2026), with the actual merger completing in Q2 or Q3 of 2027 — a process she estimates would take approximately 12–14 months.

3. **Merger governance risks flagged — SpaceX structure described as a "bunker":** Alexandra Merz raises concerns that the SpaceX corporate structure, unlike Tesla's 2010 IPO structure, is designed to protect Elon Musk's control on "all sides" with over 50% voting rights. She describes this as unprecedented and potentially problematic for institutional investors, regulators, and geopolitical counterparties (especially China). Potter acknowledges monopolistic power concentration as the biggest long-term risk.

4. **Merger seen as potentially complicating FSD licensing and China operations:** Alexandra Merz raises the possibility that a merged SpaceX-Tesla entity could make OEM licensing deals more politically fraught due to national security associations with SpaceX. Potter acknowledges the logic and says he left room in his model for some vehicles to use alternative (non-Tesla) self-driving solutions for geopolitical reasons.

5. **Post-merger SpaceX IPO could cause short-term Tesla capital reallocation:** Potter flags that a SpaceX IPO accompanying a merger could cause money currently in Tesla to rotate into SpaceX, potentially creating near-term pressure on Tesla's valuation — a risk more relevant to benchmark-constrained institutional investors than to long-term holders.
