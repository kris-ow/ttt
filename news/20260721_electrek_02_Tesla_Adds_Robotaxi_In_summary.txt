Electrek reports Tesla launched Robotaxi service in Tampa and Orlando the day before Q2 earnings, but argues the expansion is cosmetic since the underlying unsupervised fleet remains tiny and hasn't grown.

## Robotaxi
1. **New Florida markets launched without fleet details:** Tesla's @robotaxi account announced service in Tampa and Orlando on Monday with a four-word post, giving no vehicle count; the article notes Tesla rarely discloses fleet size for markets beyond Austin and that new markets have typically launched small, often with a Tesla employee monitoring from inside the car.
2. **Austin's unsupervised fleet stuck at ~17, down from ~25:** Per community tracking of the fleet, Tesla's flagship Austin market — launched June 2025 with a geofence expanded to the full metro in June 2026 — still runs about 17 active unsupervised cars, down from a peak of roughly 25 in late April. Adding Dallas's four cars, Tesla's total unsupervised operation is around 21 vehicles; the article's earlier May reporting found the fleet shrinking rather than scaling.
3. **Musk cited safety validation as the deliberate constraint:** On the Q1 2026 earnings call in April, Musk said the bottleneck is "rigorous validation, making sure things are completely safe" and that Tesla doesn't want "a single accidental injury" from the rollout — though the article notes there have already been a couple of injuries per Tesla's NHTSA reporting. The article says community tracking has put Austin's incident rate at roughly four times that of human drivers.
4. **Comparison to Waymo:** Waymo runs roughly 3,000 driverless vehicles, completes more than 500,000 paid trips per week across US cities, and recently expanded coverage by more than 20% — contrasted with Tesla's roughly 21 unsupervised cars after a year of operation.

## Editorial Stance
Fred Lambert argues Tesla's city expansions are a cheap messaging move timed to earnings, not evidence of scaling, since expanding the map costs little while growing the actual fleet is the hard, unmet task; he calls Musk's safety-validation explanation "the honest answer" and "the right call," but says it's inconsistent with a company promoting new cities as if it were scaling.
