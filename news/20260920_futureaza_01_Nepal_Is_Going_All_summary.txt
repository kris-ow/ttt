This video is an interview conducted by the channel host with Ashish Gajger, chairperson of Nepal's infrastructure development committee, about Nepal's push toward nationwide EV adoption. The discussion covers Nepal's import statistics, road and charging infrastructure limits, electricity supply, taxation policy, and public misconceptions about EV safety — none of it referencing Tesla, SpaceX, or their products or theses.

## Market & Competition
1. **Nepal's EV import share:** The official states that more than 70% of vehicles currently imported into Nepal are electric, which he calls an encouraging sign of consumer demand for EVs.
2. **Infrastructure challenges:** Of Nepal's roughly 107,000 km road network, only about 30,000 km is paved (black-topped); the rest is gravel or earth, which is difficult for low-clearance EVs to drive on. There are also no standards governing the spacing of charging stations — the electricity authority and private sector site chargers based on demand in areas with more EVs.
3. **Motorbike adoption lagging:** Despite four-wheeler EV enthusiasm, consumers remain reluctant to buy electric motorbikes, even though motorbikes make up around 80% of Nepal's total vehicle fleet — a gap the official says must close for Nepal to reach 100% EV adoption.
4. **Electric buses hindered by upfront cost:** Electric buses cost more than double the price of diesel buses upfront, discouraging operators despite electric buses' much lower per-kilometer running costs (the official cited roughly 20–25 rupees/km for petrol cars versus roughly 1 rupee/km for electric cars).
5. **Electricity supply not a bottleneck:** Nepal's electricity authority says it can meet EV charging demand; electricity demand peaks only in morning and evening, leaving surplus capacity for daytime or overnight charging. Nepal currently produces around 4,000 megawatts, with more hydropower projects in the pipeline, and the country aims to eventually export electricity to India and Bangladesh.
6. **Tax policy shift:** This fiscal year, Nepal's government reclassified vehicles into luxury and non-luxury categories, raising taxes on luxury vehicles while keeping taxes on low/medium-range vehicles roughly flat versus the prior year; EVs remain more expensive upfront than gasoline vehicles even under the new structure.
7. **Public misconceptions as the main barrier:** The official attributes remaining resistance to EVs mainly to unfounded rumors about EVs being more crash-prone (which he attributes to drivers' unfamiliarity with gearless EV controls) and fire risk, plus unresolved questions about recycling or disposing of used EV batteries, since Nepal lacks a recycling plant.
