This roundtable (Herbert with panelists Alexandra Merz, Jeff Lutz, and Cern Basher) covers SpaceX's post-IPO share lockup, its recent earnings call, and how the reported numbers are reshaping expectations for a Tesla-SpaceX merger, including possible exchange ratios and price floors.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation
1. **Share lockup expiration was a non-event:** SpaceX's first lockup expiration (for early investors and employees, not SPV holders who remain locked until next year) had been hyped by media as a mass-selling event citing a 900 million share figure, but the stock only saw two selling waves (at the open and above $115) and closed up about 5%. Panelists attributed the muted reaction to heavy pre-positioning via options trading over the prior two-plus months, a ban on hedging during lockup, and anecdotal reports of employees choosing not to sell given confidence following the earnings call.
2. **Institutional price targets skew bullish vs. retail sentiment:** A table of institutional analyst price targets for SpaceX showed most above $200, with Wolf Research low at $175 and Raymond James high at $800; panelists noted this contrasts with more bearish retail chatter, and cited a reported ~34% short interest as a percentage of float.
3. **Merger sentiment has shifted sharply toward "sooner the better":** A poll by Alexandra Merz showed over 50% of respondents (813 votes at time of taping) favoring an immediate merger, with under 8% opposed — a reversal from weeks earlier when merger talk drew heavy pushback. Merz attributes the shift to the earnings call showing SpaceX may not need Tesla's robotaxi cash and can generate comparable or faster cash flow via AI compute.
4. **Merger financial comparison — cash, capex, margins:** Per Alexandra Merz's analysis: Tesla cash reserves are roughly $43.5 billion vs. SpaceX's roughly $100 billion (post-IPO, including an $86B raise and $25B bond issuance minus $15B spent last quarter); Tesla's H1 capex was $8.3 billion (full-year guide $25 billion) vs. SpaceX's $18 billion in a single quarter; Tesla's overall margin is around 17% (auto ~17%, energy ~20%) vs. SpaceX's ~40% margin in Starlink/connectivity but negative margins in AI and space launch. Current market caps cited were roughly $1.34 trillion (Tesla) and $1.77 trillion (SpaceX) at time of taping.
5. **Merger exchange ratio and floor price debated:** At current market prices, a straight-ratio merger would value Tesla shares at roughly 3-to-1 against SpaceX shares. Alexandra Merz proposed a ratio around 4.2-to-1 with a price floor for Tesla shares (she suggested at least $600, while acknowledging retail may want higher, and Jeff Lutz suggested a floor above $600), arguing a floor would stabilize retail sentiment ahead of a shareholder vote (potentially targeted for around November) and that any shortfall would be resolved via SpaceX issuing additional shares (dilution), not cash. Merz noted Musk holds roughly 48% of SpaceX and retail's share of Tesla's shareholder base has fallen to about 22%, limiting retail's voting influence.
6. **Employee retention seen as a merger driver:** Panelists argued that Tesla AI employees, having watched peers at other AI labs and chipmakers see outsized compensation gains while TSLA has been comparatively flat, are a motivating factor for Musk to complete a merger that gives them exposure to SpaceX's growth.
7. **Historical merger-timing pattern cited:** Alexandra Merz's research found that large U.S. mergers over the past 15 years were typically announced Monday premarket, with the very largest announced on Sundays to give time for the news to be digested before markets open; she stated a belief a Tesla-SpaceX announcement could plausibly come within the next one to two weeks, though this is her own speculation, not confirmed.

## Financials
1. **SpaceX capex has an unusually short payback period:** Cern Basher highlighted that SpaceX's capital spending on AI compute infrastructure reportedly has a payback period of less than one year, unlike traditional capex (5–25 years), which he says effectively turns capex into something closer to recyclable working capital, allowing SpaceX to self-fund continued expansion without necessarily raising additional debt or equity — he likened this to Dell's historical negative cash-conversion cycle, though noted SpaceX's case isn't quite as favorable since it still pays costs upfront before being reimbursed.
2. **SpaceX targeting $100B ARR by December, revenue timeline pulled forward:** In quoted remarks, Elon Musk (on the earnings call) said SpaceX expects to reach over $100 billion annual run rate by December of this year, and that the company's internal projection for reaching $1 trillion in actual revenue has moved up from 2031 to 2030, with a "non-zero chance" of 2029. Musk reportedly clarified in a follow-up that the $100B figure is a floor ("what we'd achieve if we basically did nothing"), suggesting it could be higher. Panelists rated confidence in the $100B figure as high, but were more cautious on the 2029–2030 revenue targets given the multi-year horizon.
3. **Compute buildout target raised to as much as 20 gigawatts:** Musk stated SpaceX expects to end this year with over 2 gigawatts of compute online, and that cumulative compute by end of next year could be "several times higher" — closer to 10 gigawatts than 5. He further stated a tentative target of 20 gigawatts of power/cooling capacity online by end of next year, with Cern Basher noting Musk hedged that a portion could slip, suggesting 15GW as a more realistic figure. Panelists (Jeff Lutz) suggested "guard-banding" expectations toward the 6–8GW range given uncertainty on both compute volume and per-gigawatt pricing (cited estimates ranging roughly $40–70+ per unit of compute pricing, unconfirmed).
4. **Only ~28% of $100B ARR target currently attributable to named deals:** Cern Basher's chart analysis found that named customer commitments (Anthropic, Alphabet, Reflection AI) account for roughly a quarter (or slightly more) of the targeted $100 billion ARR, meaning the majority of that revenue relies on deals not yet publicly announced; management reportedly indicated some additional deals were signed in early Q3 and may be disclosed before the next earnings report (around October).
5. **Terafab investment reported at $119 billion:** A leaked figure early in the week put the joint Tesla-SpaceX Terafab chip manufacturing project at a $119 billion investment; panelists said the scale shown in Tesla/SpaceX renders makes that figure seem almost implausibly small given the facility's apparent size (described as equivalent to roughly 10 Giga Texas-sized factories, with 1 terawatt of compute production per year as the stated goal).

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Colossus and related compute sites detailed:** Cern Basher's chart showed current/named compute sites including Colossus (roughly 340 megawatts), Colossus 2 (nearly a gigawatt, ~496 megawatts), and Macrohard/"minihard" facilities (~350 megawatts each) — together representing less than a quarter of the roughly 8–10 gigawatt target for end of 2027, implying additional sites not yet announced will be needed; the existing Memphis site was described as likely near its land capacity.
2. **Revenue implications of compute scale-up:** If SpaceX reaches roughly 8 gigawatts of compute by next year at current rental pricing, Cern Basher estimated the associated revenue opportunity could be in the $500–600 billion range (his estimate, not company guidance) — roughly five times Tesla's current scale, contingent on pricing holding and demand continuing, and aided by SpaceX's use of next-generation chips.
3. **Terafab positioned as a vertically integrated chip/AI hub:** Panelists (referencing a separate conversation with an analyst referred to as Phil Beisel) described Terafab as combining chip fabrication (akin to TSMC), memory production (akin to Micron), GPU design/assembly (akin to Nvidia), and packaging (akin to Amkor) on a single campus at a much larger scale than any existing peer, though Intel's specific role (financial vs. technical) remains unclarified per the panel.
4. **Musk highlighted contract cancellation clauses as a hedge, not a risk:** Musk reportedly noted that cancellation clauses in Anthropic/Alphabet compute contracts were requested by SpaceX itself, giving it optionality to redirect compute to internal higher-value uses (e.g., inference for Grok, Optimus, or other Musk-ecosystem products) if external rental pricing softens or internal demand rises; panelists noted only about 10% of compute is currently earmarked for training, with inference expected to be the larger long-term driver.

## Market & Competition
1. **Risk profile comparison between Tesla and SpaceX:** Panelists debated relative execution risk, agreeing SpaceX's single-event risk (e.g., a rocket failure) is currently higher than Tesla's, but noted a Tesla robotaxi safety incident could carry greater reputational/business risk given its B2C nature versus SpaceX's more insulated B2B/government customer base (referencing Blue Origin's setbacks as a cautionary example for launch-related delays).
