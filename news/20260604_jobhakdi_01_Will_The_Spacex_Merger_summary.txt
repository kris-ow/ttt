This video features a live debate between Jo Bhakdi and Alexandra Merz, hosted by Herbert, on the topic of a potential SpaceX acquisition of Tesla following SpaceX's anticipated IPO on June 12, 2026. The central discussion revolves around whether such a merger is likely, when it might be announced, at what valuation ratio it would be structured, and whether Tesla shareholders would benefit or be harmed. Both guests broadly agree a merger is being planned but differ on the mechanics, timing, and structure of the offer.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX IPO and valuation trajectory:** Alexandra Merz and Jo Bhakdi both accept that SpaceX is targeting an IPO around June 12, 2026, with current whisper valuations in the $2–4 trillion range. Merz believes SpaceX's price will be volatile but structurally supported upward over six months due to three major index inclusions (MSCI, FTSE, and NASDAQ) in the first 15 trading days, followed by a potentially massive S&P 500 inclusion around mid-December 2026, which she compares to Tesla's transformative S&P inclusion in 2020. She argues these index-driven inflows were deliberately engineered as part of a broader merger plan, noting that major index makers softened their rules specifically to accommodate the SpaceX IPO.

2. **The merger thesis and breadcrumbs:** Both guests argue there are strong indications that a SpaceX-Tesla merger has been in preparation for at least a year. Key "breadcrumbs" cited include: (a) language in the SpaceX S-1 amendment explicitly referencing share issuance for acquisitions; (b) SpaceX authorizing 36 billion shares while currently having only approximately 12.86 billion outstanding — roughly three times the total company — which Bhakdi argues is far more than needed for routine employee incentives or stock splits; (c) the structured setup of SpaceX's lock-up period and index inclusion timeline; and (d) Tesla's unresolved share count questions that Alexandra Merz says went unanswered when she formally inquired.

3. **Merger ratio debate — one-to-one vs. one-to-two:** Jo Bhakdi originally explored whether SpaceX might acquire Tesla at two times SpaceX's own valuation (i.e., a 1:2 ratio), arguing this would be only a marginal reduction in Elon Musk's voting control — from approximately 35% to 29% ownership — and therefore Musk has little incentive to push for any particular ratio. However, Alexandra Merz countered that there are not enough authorized SpaceX shares to support a 2:1 acquisition (13 billion SpaceX shares outstanding × 2 = 26 billion needed, plus existing issuances for Echostar and other entities, exceeds the 36 billion authorized). Both converge on the view that the offer will likely be dynamic or structured with a collar/ratchet rather than a fixed ratio.

4. **Proposed merger structure — ratchet mechanism:** After extensive back-and-forth, both guests converge on a preferred structure: a one-to-one market cap offer with a guaranteed floor (ratchet), meaning Tesla shareholders would receive at minimum a set absolute dollar amount per share regardless of SpaceX price movements between announcement and vote. Bhakdi argues this is essential for deal certainty — a pure floating one-to-one offer could fail if SpaceX declines or Tesla appreciates organically via robotaxi scaling. The ratchet would remove downside risk for Tesla shareholders, drive Tesla's price to the floor immediately upon announcement, and create a near-certain yes vote. Merz agrees a ratchet would be ideal but notes it may not be necessary if SpaceX's price is well-supported by index fund inflows.

5. **Timing of announcement — Alexandra Merz's Goldilocks scenario:** Merz argues the merger announcement could come as early as late June 2026 (just before the NASDAQ index inclusion, approximately three weeks after the June 12 IPO) and no later than August 15, 2026. Her reasoning: (a) Elon Musk must exercise his 2018 compensation package stock options by August 15, converting approximately 300 million options into restricted stock units at a strike price of $23 per share, at a cost of approximately $7 billion — a higher Tesla stock price reduces this burden; (b) politically, acting while the Trump administration is in power and regulators are favorable reduces risk; (c) an August announcement would allow Tesla's shareholder vote to be held around the November 2026 shareholder meeting, with a six-week minimum proxy publication period required for a company of Tesla's size. She estimates the full regulatory merger completion at approximately Q2 2027.

6. **Tesla share count mechanics:** Merz emphasizes that Tesla's outstanding share count is a critical variable for any market cap-based merger offer. Currently Tesla has approximately 3.7 billion shares outstanding. After Musk exercises his 2018 stock options (adding approximately 300 million shares) by mid-August 2026, Tesla's outstanding share count will reach approximately 4 billion. Separately, the 2025 compensation package already added approximately 400 million shares (inserted in November 2025). Tesla's current authorized share count is 6 billion, well above the 4 billion that will be outstanding. Merz draws a comparison to Tesla's 2010 IPO, when it authorized 2 billion shares but issued fewer than 100 million — a 20x gap that lasted 12 years — to argue that SpaceX's large authorized share count does not prove acquisition intent on its own.

7. **Governance and voting control:** Alexandra Merz focuses extensively on governance dimensions that she says few analysts are covering. SpaceX's S-1 grants Elon Musk Class B shares with 10-to-1 voting rights, giving him 85% voting control despite approximately 50% economic ownership. Tesla shareholders vote by simple majority (50% plus one of all outstanding shares). Merz identifies key institutional voters as Vanguard (already invested in both companies), BlackRock (reportedly seeking $10 billion of the SpaceX IPO), ARK Invest, and Baron Capital, and argues these plus Musk's approximately 20% Tesla stake and roughly one-third of retail could clear the 50% threshold. One governance concern she raises: unlike Meta and Google, where super-voting shares revert to ordinary shares upon the founder's death or incapacitation, SpaceX's S-1 allows Musk's Class B shares to pass to his family members — a clause she believes Tesla's board should push back on in merger negotiations.

8. **Political rationale for speed:** Merz argues the merger should happen as quickly as possible because Musk's current political leverage with the Trump administration provides a favorable regulatory environment. She warns that if Democrats win both the House and Senate in the 2026 midterms, Elon Musk becomes a political target again, committees could be weaponized, and the regulatory path becomes far harder. She also raises unresolved questions about Tesla China operations and whether they can be firewalled in a combined entity.

9. **Lock-step trading mechanics and merger arbitrage:** The guests discuss how Tesla and SpaceX stocks would trade between announcement and vote. Both agree the stocks would move in approximate lock-step driven by merger arbitrage hedge funds, but Bhakdi argues this lock-step would be asymmetric — SpaceX going up pulls Tesla up (deal remains attractive), but if Tesla organically surpasses SpaceX's implied offer price, the stocks decouple and the vote risks failure. Merz counters that a ratchet floor solves this by giving Tesla shareholders certainty on their minimum, making the Tesla price jump to the floor immediately and removing the decoupling risk. She also notes the S&P inclusion in December 2026, potentially over $100 billion in passive purchases, would provide sustained upward pressure.

## Financials

1. **Elon Musk's 2018 stock option exercise economics:** The 2018 Tesla compensation package requires Musk to exercise his options (at a $23 strike price) before August 15, 2026, in a two-step process — first exercising into shares, then converting those into restricted stock units that he must hold until 2033. The exercise adds approximately 300 million shares to Tesla's outstanding count. The cost to exercise is approximately $7 billion. The tax event from this package is not due until January 2028. Musk's options to finance the $7 billion exercise cost include selling Tesla shares (higher price = fewer shares sold), pledging shares as collateral (higher price = less pledged), or a net exercise in which Tesla effectively lends him the $7 billion in exchange for shares, resulting in slightly fewer net shares received. In all scenarios, a higher Tesla stock price at the time of exercise is financially advantageous to Musk.

2. **Elon Musk's combined voting and ownership under various merger scenarios:** Bhakdi's modeling shows that under a 1:1 market cap merger, Musk's voting rights over the combined entity remain dominant due to his 10-to-1 Class B SpaceX shares. Under a 2:1 scenario (SpaceX buys Tesla at twice SpaceX's valuation), Musk's ownership stake in the combined entity would decline from approximately 35% to approximately 29% — a reduction Bhakdi characterizes as marginal and insufficient to motivate Musk to manipulate the merger ratio in either direction. His main conclusion: Musk has no strong personal financial incentive to favor any particular merger ratio.

3. **Jo Bhakdi's directional 12-month scenario:** Bhakdi states his base case (not a firm prediction) is that over a 9–12 month horizon from mid-2026, SpaceX will face difficulty sustaining its IPO valuation due to a lag in catalysts to justify the multiple, while Tesla will appreciate significantly on robotaxi and Optimus news. His directional scenario: SpaceX at approximately $1.5 trillion and Tesla at approximately $3 trillion, making Tesla the larger company by market cap at that point. He argues this scenario does not prevent a merger but does make a simple one-to-one market cap offer more complex and potentially less favorable to SpaceX.
