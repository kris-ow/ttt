This video from "brighterwithherbert" covers the accelerating humanoid robot manufacturing race, focusing on major milestones from Figure (Figure 3) and 1X (Neo), with a comparative discussion of where Tesla's Optimus stands relative to these competitors. Host Herbert is joined by robotics expert and two-time robotics company founder Scott Walter, who provides technical commentary on factory capabilities, actuator design, autonomy claims, and realistic production forecasts. The core argument is that humanoid robotics has decisively shifted from demo videos to a manufacturing war, and Tesla investors need to understand Optimus's position in that race.

## Humanoid Bots

1. **Figure's 24x Manufacturing Scale-Up:** Figure has scaled its humanoid robot manufacturing 24x over the last 120 days, going from producing one robot per day to one robot per hour. The current production rate is approximately 55 robots per week. Scott Walter explains that by working roughly six days a week on nine-hour shifts (~54 hours/week), Figure averaged about 120 minutes per robot in March, bringing that down to approximately 90 minutes by end of March, and further to approximately 60 minutes in April. He projects Figure will likely hit a run rate close to 400 robots per month by end of 2026, putting annual output somewhere between 3,000 and 4,000 units, with a possible target of around 5,000. Brett Adcock has publicly stated Figure hopes to produce "thousands" of bots in 2026, which Scott interprets as somewhere between 1,000 and roughly 9,000–10,000 — likely around 5,000 — with parts already on hand to support that target.

2. **Figure's Four-Building Campus and "The Grid" Training Facility:** Figure's campus consists of four buildings: HQ (manufacturing), Bot Q (quality assurance and burn-in), The Grid (a large new training center), and a fourth building under lease but not yet occupied. Newly assembled robots are calibrated and verified at Bot Q, then physically walk across to The Grid. Scott Walter, who visited the campus in late January 2026, confirmed The Grid is real and not CGI. The Grid features a 14x14 array of robots (approximately 196 bots visible in one drone shot), and Brett Adcock has described plans for a gymnasium-scale training area with a raised James Bond-style viewing port overlooking the bots. Figure has reportedly run autonomous operations non-stop for over 72 hours with bots swapping in and out without software errors.

3. **Figure's Vulcan AI Controller and Fault Tolerance:** Figure demonstrated a new AI robot controller called Vulcan, which allows the robot to lose up to three actuators in its lower body and still maintain balance and hobble toward a repair station rather than falling. In a live demo, the right knee actuator was disabled, and the robot successfully limped off autonomously. Scott explains that previously any joint loss in the lower body would cause a fall, and that the Vulcan system locks out the affected limbs and redistributes balance across remaining joints. He also notes that a fleet-level coordination system allows nearby healthy robots to automatically swap in when a robot signals a fault, charging issue, or failure.

4. **Figure's Deployment and Customer Use Cases:** According to the Figure white paper (published alongside the production video), robots are being allocated across four use cases: internal R&D, general training, an external home-environment partner (Brookfield, announced October 2024, deploying bots in empty apartments across different configurations), and undisclosed customer sites. BMW has been publicly confirmed as one customer site. Brett Adcock confirmed in an interview that all Figure robot demos are fully autonomous — not teleoperated — and are powered by their Helix 2 AI model. He described the current state as "chapter one" of humanoids entering society, with "chapter two" being scaling to hundreds, thousands, and tens of thousands of deployed robots.

5. **1X's NeoFactory in Hayward, California:** 1X (backed by OpenAI from an early investment) opened a 58,000 square foot NeoFactory in Hayward, California — described as America's most vertically integrated humanoid robot factory — built in just three months with 200 employees. The facility is fully operational and has a stated target production capacity of 10,000 Neo home robots per year, with a longer-term goal of 100,000 robots by end of 2027. The factory received over 10,000 pre-orders in the first five days after announcement. Herbert and Scott Walter both hold pre-orders for a Neo unit.

6. **1X's Unique Design Philosophy — Safety-First Home Robot:** Neo is designed specifically for home deployment, weighing approximately 30 kilograms — roughly half the mass of competitor humanoids (which range from 50–70 kg). It uses a tendon-driven actuation system with an aluminum endoskeleton covered by a soft lattice structure and outer garment, eliminating the need for harmonic drives and making it safer for contact with vulnerable humans. The factory manufactures motors, actuators, tendons, tactile fingers, hands, suits, and battery packs in-house. 1X has already built 17,000 Rev 2 motors at the Hayward facility. The bot has 22 degrees of freedom in its tendon-driven hands. Scott notes that while 1X claims true vertical integration, it still sources some components externally (e.g., Nvidia chips are visible in facility footage), and that some components like the soft lattice skin likely require 3D printing rather than injection molding at scale.

7. **1X's World Model and Teleoperation Strategy:** 1X released what they call a "1X World Model" in which Neo can turn any prompt into autonomous action and generate unlimited synthetic data of itself performing real-world tasks, creating a self-teaching flywheel. However, Scott explains the 10,000 home units being shipped will not be fully autonomous — rather, 1X plans to use permissioned teleoperation for training, where tele-operators (with homeowner approval) can control the robot remotely to complete tasks and generate additional training data. Geofencing features are built in to restrict robot access to certain areas of the home.

8. **Tesla Optimus — Version 3 Delays and North Campus Manufacturing:** Elon Musk has stated Tesla is not publicly revealing Optimus Gen 3 to prevent competitors from copying the design. Scott notes that Xiaomi recently revealed a robot called Cyber One with kinematics nearly identical to Optimus and aesthetic padding reminiscent of Figure 3, illustrating the concern. The original Optimus Gen 3 reveal was expected around end of 2024, then pushed to end of 2025, then Q1 2026, and now appears likely to be fall 2026 at the earliest — Scott believes it could be even later. Tesla is converting the Model S and Model X production lines at Fremont into an Optimus manufacturing line described as having 1 million unit per year capacity, and has filed a new building permit for a massive "North Campus Manufacturing" facility described as a 10 million unit per year scale factory. Scott interprets the Fremont line as realistically a 10,000-unit learning line, with the North Campus being the future scale facility once Tesla understands how to manufacture at that level.

9. **Realistic Optimus Production Expectations for 2026:** Scott Walter is firm that Tesla will not produce more than "a couple thousand" Optimus units by end of 2026. He points out that Elon Musk himself acknowledged on the most recent earnings call that it is "literally impossible to predict" the ramp due to too many moving parts, walking back from prior optimistic statements. Scott argues that supply chain constraints — particularly high-precision components like planetary roller screws — are the real bottleneck, regardless of factory readiness. He speculates Tesla may need to vertically integrate actuator and precision component manufacturing (similar to how Tesla built Terafabs for chips), which would require long-lead-time specialized CNC equipment that nobody currently produces at the volumes needed. He suggests the 25,000 Optimus units Elon has referenced for internal training purposes are the critical near-term target, and delays in hitting that number would cascade across the entire program.

10. **Competitive Landscape — Who's in Third Place:** Scott frames the current US humanoid robot race as: Figure and 1X having established manufacturing operations, while Tesla, Boston Dynamics (next-gen Atlas), and Apptronik are in a "fight for third place" in demonstrating their next generation of bots and scaling. He expects both Boston Dynamics and Apptronik to reveal next-generation robots later in 2026. He notes that Chinese companies (multiple, unnamed) are already producing humanoid robots at scale, and cites Unitree as having made approximately 5,000 robots in 2025, purchased by companies primarily for R&D and deployment readiness rather than active ROI-positive use.

## Market & Competition

1. **China vs. US Humanoid Manufacturing Gap:** Scott repeatedly notes that China has multiple humanoid robot companies already producing at scale — not just one — while the US is only just beginning to see Figure and 1X reach production volumes of hundreds of units per month. He cites Unitree's ~5,000 units produced in 2025 as an example of Chinese scale. The Xiaomi Cyber One robot is cited as an example of rapid Chinese design iteration copying from US designs.

2. **Robots-as-a-Service Business Model Emerging:** Scott explains the near-term commercial model for humanoid robots is robots-as-a-service (RaaS), where customers contract for throughput and efficiency rather than purchasing robots outright. Customers care about ROI, not how many robots are required to achieve it — whether it's 1:1 or 2:1 robots-to-task coverage. He says many companies are currently deploying robots under R&D budgets even below ROI thresholds, treating it as competitive intelligence for when robots become economically compelling in two to three years.
