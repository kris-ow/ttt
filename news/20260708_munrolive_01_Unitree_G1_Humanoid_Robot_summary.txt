This Munro Live video features Schaeffler engineers and a Munro Associates actuator specialist dissecting a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, examining its actuators, gearing, cooling systems, and structural design choices. The discussion is primarily an engineering deep-dive into a competitor humanoid platform, with Optimus referenced only briefly as a comparison point for wrist joint actuation design.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Optimus wrist uses SPU actuation, analogous to G1's ankle RSU design:** Presenters explained that the Unitree G1's ankle uses a "rotary spherical universal" (RSU) actuation strategy — two rotary motors driving tie rods through spherical joints to move the ankle remotely and share torque. They noted Tesla's Optimus uses a similar concept in its wrist, called "SPU" (spherical prismatic universal), which substitutes a linear actuator for the rotary one but relies on the same spherical-joint kinematic principle.
2. **Schaeffler's vertical integration strategy for humanoid actuators:** A Schaeffler executive (head of humanoids, North America) described the company's push into humanoid robotics, leveraging automotive-derived expertise in controllers, motors, gearboxes, bearings, sensors, and batteries. Schaeffler displayed rotary actuators using strain-wave gearing, quasi-direct-drive planetary designs, and linear actuators using ball screws and planetary roller screws, claiming coverage of "over 50%" of the componentry found in a humanoid robot.
3. **Cost trajectory expectation for actuators at scale:** A Munro analyst suggested that applying automotive manufacturing approaches (as was done historically with ball screws for electric power steering) could bring humanoid actuator costs down from roughly $1,000 per unit to roughly $100 per unit at scale, citing a robot using around 24 actuators as the reference point.
4. **G1 design shows heavy CNC-machined (billet) construction over castings:** Analysts observed that most of the G1's aluminum housings are CNC-machined from solid blocks rather than cast, attributing this to a "time to market" priority and the need for design flexibility during early-stage iteration, despite castings being more cost-effective at higher volumes.
5. **Multiple cooling strategies observed, including heat-pipe technology from CPU cooling:** The teardown revealed the G1 uses anodized housings for passive heat dissipation, centrifugal fan-driven air cooling for hip actuators, and — in high-duty-cycle joints like the knees — a copper heat pipe using phase-change refrigerant, a technique borrowed from CPU/laptop cooling and described as a novel application to motor stators.
6. **DC bus voltage instability required added capacitor filtering:** Because each of the G1's motors can act as both motor and generator, voltage on the shared DC bus fluctuates; Unitree added filtering capacitors near the actuators to smooth these ripples and improve motor controllability, though analysts noted this was implemented reactively rather than optimally packaged, suggesting future design iteration.
7. **Trade-offs between strain-wave and planetary-gear actuators:** Panelists contrasted the G1's planetary-gear actuators (back-drivable, ~15:1 ratio per stage, used for agile control but with backlash) against strain-wave designs supplied by Schaeffler (near-zero backlash, high gear ratios around 100:1, lightweight and compact, but not back-drivable and prone to flex-spline damage under high impact).

Notable quotes and data:
- "There's a lot of value in humanoids that we can capture" — Schaeffler executive on the company's actuator/component portfolio covering over 50% of humanoid content.
- Strain-wave gear actuators can achieve roughly 100:1 gear ratios; the G1's planetary gear sample shown was about 15:1.
- Estimated potential actuator cost reduction from roughly $1,000 to roughly $100 per unit if automotive-style manufacturing scale is applied, referencing a robot with ~24 actuators.
- Roller screw thread grinding time estimated at a few minutes up to 15–20 minutes per part depending on depth and precision required.
- Optimus wrist actuation described as using an "SPU" (spherical prismatic universal) mechanism, paralleling the G1 ankle's "RSU" (rotary spherical universal) design.
- Analysts noted the exposed cardan joint and grease in the G1 ankle mechanism would likely degrade quickly in real-world/production use (dust, sand ingress) and is not "ready for prime time."
