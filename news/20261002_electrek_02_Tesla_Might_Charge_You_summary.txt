Tesla launched a new "Emergency Drive Away" feature letting drivers shift into Drive and pull away while still plugged into a Supercharger, introduced about two months after a shooting at a Supercharger station in Twin Falls, Idaho. Tesla disclosed the feature can cause up to $25,000 in damage to the Supercharger and requires a mandatory vehicle service visit, but has not clarified who is financially responsible.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Feature mechanics and costs:** Tesla says Emergency Drive Away works on V2, V3, and V4 Supercharger stalls (not on home chargers) and will damage both the vehicle and the charger. In response to an owner question, Tesla's charging team said the Supercharger damage could be up to $25,000, depending on the site, describing it as "electrical and civil" damage. The car requires a mandatory service visit for the charge port, with no price given; Tesla also said glycol coolant seen leaking in its demo video would be cleaned up as part of a mandatory Supercharger service visit.

2. **Unanswered liability question:** Multiple owners asked Tesla directly whether a driver who uses the feature in a real emergency would be responsible for paying for Supercharger repairs; Tesla did not answer this question while responding to other questions in the same thread. Tesla did confirm that penalties exist for misuse, stating in its launch post that deliberate misuse "will invoke additional penalties," without specifying what those penalties are or how misuse would be determined.

3. **Cost context (author's analysis):** The article notes that $25,000 represents 40% of the $500,000 (roughly $62,500 per post) hardware cost of an eight-post V4 Supercharger site listed in Tesla's Supercharger for Business configurator. It also compares the figure to the $299 EVject breakaway adapter (over which Tesla sued EVject in 2024 over overheating claims) and to state minimum property-damage insurance requirements, citing $15,000 in Idaho, $10,000 in New York, and $5,000 in Pennsylvania — all below the stated potential $25,000 damage cost.

## Editorial Stance
The author (Fred Lambert) frames Tesla's silence on who pays for damage as a notable gap, implying drivers using the feature—potentially in genuine emergencies—could be exposed to costs exceeding their state's minimum insurance coverage, since Tesla can bill the payment method on file for the Supercharger account.
