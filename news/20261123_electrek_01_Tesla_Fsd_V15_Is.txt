Author:      Fred Lambert

Published:   2026-11-23 16:00 UTC
Fetched:     2026-11-23 16:03 UTC

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Tesla’s FSD v15 is its last real shot at delivering unsupervised self-driving on the AI4 computer in millions of customer cars. Elon Musk spent the weekend explaining on X how the new software uses the hardware, and there isn’t much room left.

He also confirmed that cars won’t get the next-gen AI5 chip, and that the mid-cycle AI4.5 upgrade just had its RAM cut from 32GB to 24GB.

## Musk explains where AI4’s compute goes

The most visible part of FSD v15 is Tesla Reality View, a live, video-game-like rendering of the car’s surroundings with chevrons floating in the sky for upcoming turns. Musk called it “real-time AI video generation (no gaming engine!) using just half of one AI4 TRIP accelerator.”

The fan accounts ran with that as a flex. But Musk’s follow-up posts tell a different story.

 
 
 

He explained that after Tesla moved a decoder into SRAM, “we only have half a TRIP out of three on SoC B, for this reality visualizer, so spent the compute as best we could.” In other words, half a TRIP is what was left over. He added that TRIP, Tesla’s neural accelerator, “basically only handles 8-bit int and has to run softmax in emulation mode in 28 steps.”

In another post, Musk described Reality View as a roughly 1-billion-parameter diffusion model running off the same vision encoder features “that are plugged into the 3B encoder model that produces the driving actions.” He admitted the visualization’s latency is “a bit high due to compute constraints.”

That 3B figure is worth noting. Tesla talked up a 10x jump in FSD model size for over a year, first for v14 and then for v15. Musk didn’t say whether 3B covers the entire driving stack, but it’s nowhere near 10x.

And Tesla’s own engineers aren’t describing a chip with lots of headroom. Dilreet Raju, who works on Tesla’s AI silicon, wrote that the hardest work of his life was co-designing the v15 vision encoder “to squeeze out the last few percents of MFU from AI4.” MFU is model FLOPs utilization, a measure of how much of the chip’s theoretical compute the model actually uses. Musk replied with a target emoji.

## The redundancy problem

When Tesla unveiled its FSD computer in 2019, the selling point was redundancy: two independent chips on one board, each with its own power and memory, so one could take over if the other failed. As we noted when Tesla started shipping the AI4.5 computer in Model Y in January, Tesla’s FSD computers have historically used two identical chips for exactly that reason.

That’s what an unsupervised, Level 4 system needs. With nobody in the driver’s seat, a hardware fault can’t simply hand control back to a human. The car needs enough healthy compute left to keep seeing the road and pull over safely.

But Musk’s own description shows SoC B running the decoder and the Reality View model, not sitting idle as a mirror of SoC A. Tesla hackers, including greentheonly, have reported for years that Tesla started splitting its FSD workload across both chips back in the HW3 days.

Tesla has never explained how much of AI4 it reserves for failover, or whether a single SoC can run enough of the stack to bring a car to a safe stop on its own. The more of the board v15 needs, the harder that question gets.

Waymo has answered it publicly. The company says both of its onboard computing systems run full workloads in parallel and that a secondary computer can bring the vehicle to a safe stop if the primary fails, backed by redundant braking, steering and power. Its new custom chip is rated at more than 1,000 TOPS. By our own comparison of AI4 and Nvidia’s Drive Thor, AI4 is estimated at roughly 100 to 150 TOPS across both chips.

## FSD v15 is still mostly in the hands of early testers

Tesla started rolling out FSD v15 on Saturday, claiming “25x better handling of safety critical cases” without any baseline. So far, the people posting videos are mostly the usual crowd of Tesla-focused accounts that get early builds. Just hours before the rollout started, Musk wrote that v15 “goes to wide release next month.”

The early clips are almost all about Reality View, the new parking spot selection, and the floating arrows. Those are UI features. They say very little about how many miles v15 can drive between critical interventions, which is the only number that matters for unsupervised driving.

## AI4.5: more RAM, not much more speed

That brings us to AI4.5. On Tesla’s Q1 2026 earnings call, Musk announced an “AI4 Plus” with RAM doubled from 16GB to 32GB per chip, which raised the question of whether HW4 would follow HW3.

This weekend, Musk said “AI4.5 is a relatively small performance improvement that is mostly due to needing to upgrade RAM to LPDDR5X.” Hours later, he added: “RAM is super expensive, so we reduced AI4.5 from 32GB to 24GB. That will be fine. It’s hard to use 32GB anyway, given memory bandwidth constraints.”

He also said that AI4 “costs twice as much now due to memory and storage price hikes.”

So the mid-cycle refresh exists mainly because Tesla needs a newer memory type it can actually buy, not to unlock bigger models. If memory bandwidth is the bottleneck, as Musk keeps repeating, adding capacity doesn’t change much.

## AI5 and AI6 go to Optimus

Asked by an owner on Sunday whether to wait for AI5 before upgrading from HW3, Musk answered: “It’s going to Optimus. Cars will remain at AI4. That’s enough to solve superhuman driving.”

An interesting comment, as it destroys one thing that HW3 owners were holding to: Tesla was delaying HW3 upgrades until it had AI5. Now, it’s clearly not the case and Tesla has no excuse not to upgrade HW3 cars.

He described AI5 as “a gigantic improvement. Roughly 12X more compute,” and later as “roughly an order of magnitude hardware performance improvement, all things considered. Memory bandwidth is a constraint.” Last year, he said that by some metrics AI5 would be 40 times better than AI4.

AI5 is also moving around. In October, Musk said Tesla cut AI5’s RAM in half to 72GB to get enough volume for Optimus, then nudged it back up to 96GB a day later. AI6 was cut by a third, to 144GB of LPDDR6. When asked about AI6 this weekend, Musk replied with a chef’s kiss meme.

As we reported in July, Samsung’s Texas fab won’t reach AI5 volume production until the second half of 2027, nearly two years behind Musk’s original timeline. And that volume is now headed to robots and data centers, not cars.

## Electrek’s Take

This is the situation for AI4 owners: v15 has to be the one. And we heard Elon Musk calling every FSD update “the one” for the last decade.

Tesla’s own engineers are talking about squeezing the last few percent of utilization out of AI4. Musk describes a half-accelerator scrap of compute left for the visualizer. The AI4.5 refresh is a RAM swap that just got downsized, and AI5 isn’t coming to cars. If v15 can’t get to unsupervised driving on AI4, there’s no hardware escape hatch for the fleet.

We’ve seen this movie before. Tesla said HW3 was enough for unsupervised self-driving until it wasn’t, and those owners are still waiting on an answer about upgrades. AI4 owners paid for the same promise.

And even if v15 drives well, I want to know how Tesla handles a chip failure with nobody behind the wheel. That’s not optional for Level 4. If the stack needs both SoCs to drive, what brings the car to a safe stop when one of them crashes?

As for v15 itself, I’m not drawing conclusions from influencer clips. Reality View looks great, but it’s also visibly laggy, and Musk admits it’s compute-limited. A great visual update is nice. Driving performance is what matters. I’ll wait until I get it in my own car to see how big the improvement actually is.
