This is a two-person discussion between the host and analyst Alexandra Merz reacting to a Wall Street Journal report (citing journalist Becky Peterson) that Tesla was weighing a sale of its China business to pave the way for a potential Tesla–SpaceX merger, which Elon Musk publicly called "fake news." The two debate whether Tesla China divestiture is realistically on the table, how a merger might be structured and timed, and what risks — dilution, shareholder vote dynamics, and China negotiating leverage — could mean for Tesla shareholders.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation / Financials
1. **WSJ report and Musk's denial:** A Wall Street Journal article reported Tesla was weighing options — including a possible sale — for its China business to facilitate a Tesla-SpaceX merger; Musk called it fake news within about two hours. Merz argues the headline's specific claim (an active weighing of a *sale*) is likely false, but the underlying reality — that Tesla China issues must be addressed and negotiated with Chinese regulators as part of any merger — is not, and Musk's denial specifically targeted the sale framing, not the broader China-related negotiation.
2. **Merz's central theory — an SM waiver clause:** Merz's working theory (her own speculation, not confirmed) is that the merger agreement will include a clause stating Chinese regulatory approval (State Market Regulatory / "SM" authorization) is not a condition to closing, removing China's ability to stall the deal via delay tactics, while substantive negotiations over what Tesla China is permitted to produce (cars only vs. unsupervised FSD/bots) continue in parallel.
3. **Divestiture probability and mechanics:** Merz assigns roughly a 10-15% probability to an actual full divestment of Tesla China, viewing an 80-90% likelihood that China-related terms get resolved either within the merger negotiation period or afterward with reduced financial significance. The host relays a private conversation with an M&A professional at a "big three" consulting/audit firm who said outright spin-offs of businesses like this rarely happen because value lies in Tesla's core (autonomy, Musk) rather than the China assets; a joint-venture-style ring-fencing arrangement, similar to what other automakers have with Chinese partners, is seen as more likely than a sale.
4. **Proposed deal structure and valuation floor:** Merz speculates Musk may announce a merger with a stock-price "floor" (she guesses around $600 for TSLA) to make the deal attractive despite SpaceX's currently low private share price (cited around $100), given a roughly nine-month gap between announcement and closing during which both stocks could move.
5. **Timing estimate:** Merz's best estimate (explicitly speculative) is an announcement in the window of roughly August 10-15, 2026 — timed to allow a shareholder vote by November 2026 and to occur within the current, merger-favorable U.S. administration; she estimates a shareholder vote roughly three to four months after announcement and final closing around nine months later.
6. **Vote math and retail power:** Merz calculates that of Tesla's ~3.95 billion shares outstanding, about 400 million cannot vote (tied to the 2025 compensation package) leaving ~3.5 billion; Musk controls roughly 700 million votes, meaning roughly 1.1 billion additional votes are needed for a majority — an amount she believes institutional investors alone (now over 50% of Tesla's shareholder base, versus retail down to the low 20% range) can supply even without retail support.
7. **SpaceX share lockups and index weighting:** Merz notes early SpaceX investor lockup windows are opening in September and December 2026, with roughly 13% of early investors under SPV-based lockups extending as far as August 2027; she also notes SpaceX is currently about 1% of the QQQ index and should rise to roughly 3% as additional lockups free up float for index purchases.
8. **Downside scenario if the vote fails:** Both agree a Tesla shareholder "no" vote would likely send TSLA considerably lower, largely due to market expectations that Musk could walk away from Tesla, as previously implied around his compensation-package negotiations; a SpaceX-side "no" vote is seen as near-zero risk given Musk's ~82% SpaceX voting control.
9. **Company X ticker speculation:** The host raises unconfirmed speculation that Musk may have acquired the dormant "X" ticker (previously used by Zillow, per Merz, as one of only two known transfers of a single-letter ticker between exchanges); Merz says she believes with near certainty Musk holds it, but doesn't think it implies a separate new holding-company structure given how much legal effort already went into SpaceX's IPO restructuring.

## Market & Competition
1. **Political backdrop and China leverage:** The speakers discuss China's history of favorable treatment toward Tesla (unique wholly-owned factory access, a large government loan) as evidence China has strategic reasons to keep Tesla operating there, while also noting Musk's stated 2025 concerns (raised in a prior interview, unspecified venue) about Taiwan and reducing dependence on China for rare-earth minerals and supply chains as a driver of the merger's timing.
2. **Bot economics as a long-term China lever:** Merz argues that once humanoid robot labor drives production costs toward roughly $1/hour in the U.S. and Europe (versus a currently estimated ~$60/hour), China's current ~$4/hour labor cost advantage becomes far less relevant, reducing Tesla's dependence on Chinese manufacturing over time (she frames a hypothetical 2030 timeframe for this shift).
