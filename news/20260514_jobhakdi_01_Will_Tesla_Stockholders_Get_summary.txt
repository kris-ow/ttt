## Market & Competition

1. **Rich Chu's bearish merger case:** Rich Chu published a critical assessment arguing the 1:1 merger is a significant downside risk for Tesla shareholders. His key points: SpaceX's IPO valuation will be artificially inflated by supply-demand imbalance and retail/index fund demand; once lockup periods expire and insider shares flood the market, SpaceX will act as an anchor dragging Tesla down rather than a magnet pulling it up. He also argued the two companies have diverging duration profiles — Tesla is entering a cash flow harvest season via robotaxi, while SpaceX is burning capital on early-stage orbital infrastructure that is years from maturity.

2. **Jo Bhakdi's partial agreement with Rich Chu:** Bhakdi agrees with virtually all of Rich's short-term analysis. He concurs that SpaceX's IPO will be overhyped, that a post-IPO deflation is likely (he estimates roughly 30% down from IPO price as "very likely, very possible"), and that for approximately the next 18 months, Tesla shareholders are better off without the merger. He explicitly states he would bet that the merger is worse for Tesla shareholders through the end of 2027.

3. **Merger-of-equals framing and conspiracy concerns:** Bhakdi notes that initial narratives involved SpaceX acquiring Tesla at only a 20–30% premium, which faced retail investor pushback, leading to a pivot toward a "merger of equals" framing. Rich Chu characterizes this as a coordinated manipulation against Tesla shareholders' interests, though Bhakdi distances himself from the conspiracy framing while acknowledging it as a legitimate concern worth noting.

4. **Elon Musk control as a practical constraint:** Bhakdi raises the pragmatic point that regardless of shareholder sentiment, Elon Musk is in control, and voting against the merger is unlikely to produce an alternative plan — it would simply derail Elon's plan without substituting a better one. He treats this as a strong factual constraint on shareholder agency, even if it is an uncomfortable moral argument.

5. **SpaceX orbital AI data center thesis:** Bhakdi's long-term bullish case for SpaceX rests on the concept of orbital data centers — launching AI compute infrastructure into space, powered by solar flux, creating what he describes as an infinitely scalable business model. He argues that the global energy bottleneck for AI compute makes space-based infrastructure inevitable, and that everything hinges on Starship becoming commercially ready and scalable. He believes Starship's commercial operations timeline is not subject to indefinite delay.

6. **Starship as the key risk factor:** Bhakdi identifies Starship's commercial readiness as the singular critical risk for SpaceX's valuation trajectory. He believes commercial Starship operations could begin as early as this year (2026), with scaling in 2027, and that if this timeline holds, SpaceX's growth rate will ultimately exceed Tesla's because the addressable market for space-based AI compute is theoretically infinite.

## Financials

1. **Tesla standalone market cap projections:** Bhakdi's discounted cash flow model (described as conservative, based on robotaxi and Optimus scaling) projects Tesla's market cap as follows: approximately $1.5 trillion at present (mid-2026); $2 trillion by end of 2026; $3 trillion by end of 2027; $5 trillion by end of 2028; $8 trillion by end of 2029. He frames these as his most reasonable base-case estimates, acknowledging scenarios could be higher or lower.

2. **SpaceX IPO and near-term valuation trajectory:** Bhakdi projects SpaceX will IPO at approximately $2.2 trillion, which he sees as realistic but acknowledges uncertainty. He then expects a roughly 30% post-IPO deflation, with SpaceX likely remaining below its IPO valuation through end of 2027 due to lagging cash flows. He projects SpaceX reaches approximately $2 trillion by end of 2027, after which it enters a hyper-growth trajectory he believes will ultimately exceed Tesla's long-term growth rate.

3. **Merger impact on Tesla shareholder returns — 18-month horizon:** Using a simplified merger-of-equals model (summing the two market caps), Bhakdi computes that over the next 18–20 months a merged entity underperforms Tesla standalone. Specifically: in 2027, the merged entity delivers approximately 38% growth versus Tesla standalone's projected 50%. On a $1 million investment post-merger, he estimates shareholders would earn approximately $380,000 versus $500,000 without the merger — a meaningful but not catastrophic shortfall.

4. **Merger impact on Tesla shareholder returns — long-term horizon:** By 2028, Bhakdi's model shows the merged entity outperforming Tesla standalone (approximately $800,000 return vs. $666,000 on a $1 million investment). By 2029, the outperformance widens further ($880,000 for the merged entity vs. $600,000 for Tesla alone). He concludes that by December 2029, SpaceX alone would be larger than Tesla, and the merger will have been net positive for Tesla shareholders on a cumulative basis.

5. **SpaceX long-term growth rate vs. Tesla:** Bhakdi argues SpaceX's long-term sustainable growth rate will exceed Tesla's because the addressable market for space-based AI compute is unlimited, whereas Tesla's robotaxi and Optimus markets, while enormous, are finite. He projects Tesla growing at roughly 40–50% annually in the near term, with SpaceX ultimately growing faster once orbital infrastructure reaches maturity.
