First, the reason I ve chosen Fervo, Eavor, and&nbsp;

There are other companies working on geothermal,&nbsp;

&nbsp;
Let s start by taking a look at how conventional&nbsp;
geothermal works, and then do a mini-deep-dive&nbsp;&nbsp;
into how companies like Fervo, Eavor,&nbsp;
and Quaise are approaching geothermal.
&nbsp;
Conventional geothermal power generation&nbsp;
involves tapping into shallow hydrothermal&nbsp;&nbsp;
reservoirs. That is, high temperature water&nbsp;
reservoirs that are near the surface of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
earth s crust. That high temperature water is&nbsp;
used to spin a turbine to generate electricity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which can be done in three ways.
First, for superhot reservoirs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
dry steam can be used directly from the reservoir.&nbsp;
This method is the simplest and most efficient,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but superhot reservoirs with&nbsp;
the right geology are rare.
&nbsp;
Second, for slightly cooler, hot reservoirs&nbsp;
with relatively clean water, high-temperature&nbsp;&nbsp;
high-pressure water can be brought to the&nbsp;
surface and flashed into steam. This is&nbsp;&nbsp;
the most common type of geothermal because it s&nbsp;
relatively simple and efficient and the suitable&nbsp;&nbsp;
reservoir geology is more common. That is, it s a&nbsp;
good balance of cost and resource availability.
&nbsp;
Third, for lower temperature hydrothermal&nbsp;
reservoirs or reservoirs with salty or corrosive&nbsp;&nbsp;
water, the water from the reservoir is brought&nbsp;
to the surface and run through a heat exchanger,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is called a binary cycle. The heat&nbsp;
exchanger contains an organic fluid with&nbsp;&nbsp;
a low boiling point, like isobutane, which is&nbsp;
vaporized to spin the turbine generator.
&nbsp;
The lower temperature requirement means&nbsp;
this method can be used in more reservoirs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but the higher capital costs and complexity&nbsp;
of the heat exchanger result in electricity&nbsp;&nbsp;
costs that aren t competitive, and&nbsp;
so it s seen limited deployment.
&nbsp;
Overall, as you can see on screen, regardless&nbsp;
of whether dry steam, flash steam, or binary&nbsp;&nbsp;
generation is used, geothermal resources are&nbsp;
relatively geographically limited. And where&nbsp;&nbsp;
there are opportunities, it doesn t always&nbsp;
coincide with population centers and therefore&nbsp;&nbsp;
infrastructure for low cost integration into&nbsp;
the grid. So, in order to reduce the cost of&nbsp;&nbsp;
geothermal and increase the areas where&nbsp;
it s viable, new methods are required&nbsp;&nbsp;
to reduce the cost of drilling and operation.
With all that in mind, let s take a look at Fervo,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Eavor, and Quaise, which are each&nbsp;
innovating in their own way with&nbsp;&nbsp;
drilling and well design so that low cost&nbsp;
geothermal can be built nearly anywhere.
&nbsp;
Fervo s geothermal works by drilling two wells&nbsp;
parallel to each other in dry rock, and then&nbsp;&nbsp;
drilling horizontally and fracturing, or fracking&nbsp;
the rock using high pressure water and sand.
&nbsp;
After that, plain water is injected into one&nbsp;
of the wells. That water permeates into the&nbsp;&nbsp;
fractured rock, which heats it, and then is&nbsp;
recovered by the parallel production well.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It s then pumped to the surface and a binary&nbsp;
cycle is used to spin a turbine generator.
&nbsp;
Horizontal drilling and fracking were of course&nbsp;
pioneered by the oil and gas industry over the&nbsp;&nbsp;
past couple of decades to increase the output&nbsp;
of each well drilled and now Fervo is leveraging&nbsp;&nbsp;
that technology for geothermal drilling.&nbsp;
Let s take a look at how that works.
&nbsp;
The deeper a geothermal well is drilled, the&nbsp;
hotter the rock, which is good for efficiency.&nbsp;&nbsp;
However, the rock also becomes denser with&nbsp;
depth. The denser and hotter rock is more&nbsp;&nbsp;
difficult to drill through, which is expensive.
By drilling deep enough to reach rock that s just&nbsp;&nbsp;
hot enough for geothermal, and then drilling&nbsp;
horizontally, it allows the well to be made&nbsp;&nbsp;
longer for the same cost, meaning more surface&nbsp;
area to heat the injection water more quickly,&nbsp;&nbsp;
meaning more energy that can be brought&nbsp;
to the surface to spin the turbine.
&nbsp;
And, of course, fracking pulverizes the&nbsp;
rock, which further increases its surface&nbsp;&nbsp;
area and allows the water to absorb&nbsp;
even more heat energy as it moves from&nbsp;&nbsp;
the injection to the production well.
Fracking does of course introduce risks,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it s not as bad as oil industry fracking. That&nbsp;
s for two reasons. First, Fervo s fracking is much&nbsp;&nbsp;
less extensive than oil industry fracking.&nbsp;
Second, it s occurring in dry rock rather&nbsp;&nbsp;
than petroleum-containing rocks that can seep and&nbsp;
release toxic hydrocarbons into water supplies.
&nbsp;
The last note on Fervo is that, as I said&nbsp;
a moment ago they re using a binary cycle,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or heat exchanger, which adds cost and&nbsp;
complexity. That cost and complexity is&nbsp;&nbsp;
offset by the lower cost of the drilling and&nbsp;
using a binary cycle will allow Fervo to drill&nbsp;&nbsp;
geothermal wells nearly anywhere and at shallower&nbsp;
depths by using cooler geothermal resources.
&nbsp;
Next, let s look at Eavor. Eavor also drills&nbsp;
in hot dry rock and uses horizontal drilling.&nbsp;&nbsp;
However, instead of two parallel wells with&nbsp;
no branching, they drill multi-branched wells&nbsp;&nbsp;
that connect at the tips to form closed&nbsp;
loops. After creating the loops they seal&nbsp;&nbsp;
the rock walls of the borehole, turning&nbsp;
it into what they call Rock Pipe.
&nbsp;
That is, it s a fully closed system&nbsp;
that doesn t allow the water that s&nbsp;&nbsp;
pumped through the geothermal system to&nbsp;
seep into the surrounding rock. Again,&nbsp;&nbsp;
after the water is injected and retrieved from&nbsp;
the geothermal loop, a binary cycle is used to&nbsp;&nbsp;
convert the thermal energy into electricity.
The benefit of Eavor s approach is that it has&nbsp;&nbsp;
almost no subsurface or seismic side effects&nbsp;
because it s fully self-contained, and it allows&nbsp;&nbsp;
for a thermosiphon effect. That is, the cold water&nbsp;
introduced at the surface will naturally sink,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and as it heats, it naturally rises on the&nbsp;
production side of the closed loop. That means&nbsp;&nbsp;
very little active pumping is required to run&nbsp;
the system because it s passively pumped through&nbsp;&nbsp;
the geothermal loop due to thermodynamics.
The drawback of Eavor s approach is that much&nbsp;&nbsp;
more drilling is required to achieve the&nbsp;
same surface area as Fervo s system, it&nbsp;&nbsp;
s technically more difficult to make the perfect&nbsp;
underground loops, and blockages are a potential&nbsp;&nbsp;
issue because the water has to rely on one return&nbsp;
pathway rather than hundreds of potential pathways&nbsp;&nbsp;
that are possible with Fervo s fracking method.
Lastly, let s look at Quaise. Quaise has by far&nbsp;&nbsp;
the greatest science fiction vibes of the three&nbsp;
companies, so it s attracted the most attention.
&nbsp;
For the first few kilometres of their&nbsp;
geothermal wells, they use conventional&nbsp;&nbsp;
drill bits to auger through softer rock. But,&nbsp;
once they hit ultra-hot and dense deep rock,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they switch to a gyrotron energy beam,&nbsp;
which instantly vaporizes the rock.
&nbsp;
The gyrotron they use is from nuclear fusion&nbsp;
research and it operates on the same principal&nbsp;&nbsp;
as a microwave. However, rather than using&nbsp;
around 1000 watts of power at a frequency of&nbsp;&nbsp;
around 2.5 Gigahertz, it uses 1 million watts&nbsp;
at a frequency of around 100 Gigahertz.
&nbsp;
By using a gyrotron to vaporize the rock, there&nbsp;
s no longer a drill-bit to melt or wear down,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so the well can be drilled far deeper than typical&nbsp;
geothermal at low cost. That means Quaise can tap&nbsp;&nbsp;
into deeper rock that provides superhot geothermal&nbsp;
temperatures, meaning a geothermal system with&nbsp;&nbsp;
much higher efficiency and lower cost.
The vaporization also has the handy side&nbsp;&nbsp;
effect of vitrifying the walls of the well.&nbsp;
What does that mean? With a typical well,&nbsp;&nbsp;
miles of drill pipe have to be intermittently&nbsp;
removed to not only replace the drill-bit,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but also to insert steel casing and concrete&nbsp;
to keep the borehole from collapsing.
&nbsp;
With Quaise, the drilling can occur continuously&nbsp;
because there s no drill-bit to replace and&nbsp;&nbsp;
the sides of the borehole are melted into&nbsp;
ultrahard obsidian, so no casing is required.
&nbsp;
The end result is that Quaise should be able&nbsp;
to drill through ultra-dense hot rock 10-50x&nbsp;&nbsp;
faster than conventional drilling. Roughly 3-5&nbsp;
meters per hour instead of .1 meters per hour,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which means a 10 km deep hole could be&nbsp;
drilled in 100 days instead of 10 years.
&nbsp;
What about drawbacks? Quaise isn t just adapting&nbsp;
existing drilling technologies to a new use case,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they re developing an entirely new way&nbsp;
of drilling, and along with that means&nbsp;&nbsp;
a raft of new engineering challenges.&nbsp;
Let s look at 3 quick examples:
&nbsp;
The first challenge is avoiding&nbsp;
plasma generation in the borehole&nbsp;&nbsp;
that could melt the boring equipment.
The second is removing the vaporized&nbsp;&nbsp;
rock and ash from the bottom of the&nbsp;
borehole, which is several miles deep.
&nbsp;
Third, injecting hot water so deep&nbsp;
and into such hot rock could create&nbsp;&nbsp;
issues for the stability of the borehole.
On that note, Quaise expects their geothermal&nbsp;&nbsp;
operations to create supercritical&nbsp;
steam. That is, steam that s at&nbsp;&nbsp;
extraordinary temperatures and pressures.
Much like Fervo, they intend to drill parallel&nbsp;&nbsp;
boreholes and fracture the rock between&nbsp;
them close to the bottom of the borehole.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But when the water is pumped into the injection&nbsp;
well, it won t remain as water, but will instead&nbsp;&nbsp;
turn into supercritical steam, which will&nbsp;
allow them to extract up to 10x more energy&nbsp;&nbsp;
when it s piped up from the production well.
Once that supercritical steam reaches the surface,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it will again go through a heat exchanger, but&nbsp;
instead of flashing a chemical like isobutane, the&nbsp;&nbsp;
supercritical steam will be hot enough to flash&nbsp;
water to steam to drive the turbine generator.
&nbsp;
Why not just use the supercritical steam&nbsp;
directly to drive the turbine? Because the&nbsp;&nbsp;
supercritical steam will contain corrosive&nbsp;
compounds that would damage the turbine.
&nbsp;
Overall, if Quaise can get this technology&nbsp;
working, it could ultimately be the cheapest,&nbsp;&nbsp;
cleanest, most power dense, and most&nbsp;
geographically flexible geothermal technology.
&nbsp;
The question is, how long will it take,&nbsp;
and how much will the electricity cost?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Let s compare each of the technologies.
Starting with cost, Fervo is targeting&nbsp;&nbsp;
$100/MWh in the next few years and $50/MWh in the&nbsp;
2030s. The estimated electricity cost from Eavor&nbsp;&nbsp;
s test facility is over $200/MWh, but they claim&nbsp;
to have line of site to $75/MWh. Meanwhile, Quaise&nbsp;&nbsp;
has given a range of estimates from $20 - 115 per&nbsp;
MWh. That is, they don t really know yet because&nbsp;&nbsp;
their technology is still under development.
For comparison, both solar and gas turbine&nbsp;&nbsp;
generation are currently around $100 per&nbsp;
megawatt hour. You ll often see lower costs&nbsp;&nbsp;
mentioned for each, but it often doesn t take&nbsp;
into account the cost of firming for solar&nbsp;&nbsp;
power and recent increases in the cost of gas&nbsp;
turbine generators due to data center demand.
&nbsp;
In terms of progress and scaling, Fervo&nbsp;
s Project Red test site in Nevada was&nbsp;&nbsp;
able to hit a maximum of 3.5 Megawatts&nbsp;
of electrical power in stress testing,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and in continuous operation delivers a steady&nbsp;
state average of 1.4 Megawatts which is used&nbsp;&nbsp;
to offset Google s local data center demand.
Fervo is now working on a full scale facility&nbsp;&nbsp;
in Utah that s targeting 100 megawatts&nbsp;
of steady state power by early 2027,&nbsp;&nbsp;
then 500 megawatts by 2028, and long&nbsp;
term up to 2 Gigawatts at that site.
&nbsp;
As for Eavor, their test site in Germany was&nbsp;
targeting 8.2 Megawatts of electrical power,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but fell severely short at between a half&nbsp;
to two megawatts of electrical power.&nbsp;&nbsp;
As a result, they re backing away from building&nbsp;
the geothermal powerplants themselves and looking&nbsp;&nbsp;
to license their drilling technology instead.
As for Quaise, they haven t yet started to scale&nbsp;&nbsp;
because they re still working through engineering&nbsp;
challenges. Their goal is to have a 50 Megawatt&nbsp;&nbsp;
geothermal well operating and producing power&nbsp;
by 2030, but I expect that timeline to slip.
&nbsp;
Overall, if all goes well, my expectation&nbsp;
for all three of these companies combined&nbsp;&nbsp;
is to hit maybe 1 Gigawatt of next&nbsp;
generation geothermal power by 2030.
&nbsp;
In summary, next generation geothermal power&nbsp;
includes an array of technologies that solve&nbsp;&nbsp;
the geographic limitations of conventional&nbsp;
geothermal by allowing geothermal energy to&nbsp;&nbsp;
be tapped into nearly anywhere and at low cost.
Some of those technologies, like Quaise Energy s&nbsp;&nbsp;
gyrotron drilling are still some years away.&nbsp;
But others, like Fervo s horizontal drilling&nbsp;&nbsp;
and fracking, are successfully past the prototype&nbsp;
stage and are ready for major power production.
&nbsp;
So if all goes well, we should see Fervo&nbsp;
s technology start to scale in earnest&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the next few years, and from there it could&nbsp;
scale rapidly. That s because the equipment and&nbsp;&nbsp;
knowledge are widely available from the oil and&nbsp;
gas industry and it only takes Fervo a few weeks&nbsp;&nbsp;
to drill a well. The time consuming part is things&nbsp;
like permitting and connecting to the grid.
&nbsp;
As for cost, it looks like $100 per MWh is in&nbsp;
the bag, which is competitive with solar and gas,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and in the longer term, with scale, they could&nbsp;
provide some of the cheapest and cleanest power&nbsp;&nbsp;
available at closer to $50 per MWh.
As a final note, geothermal power is a&nbsp;&nbsp;
great complement to solar power because it&nbsp;
can provide baseload rather than intermittent&nbsp;&nbsp;
power. The other major options for baseload&nbsp;
generation are gas and next generation nuclear,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but I suspect that geothermal will&nbsp;
eventually gain broader appeal.
&nbsp;
That s because gas generation faces&nbsp;
noise and air pollution concerns,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and next generation nuclear faces larger&nbsp;
challenges with engineering and financing.
&nbsp;
As always, that s what seems likely to me and it&nbsp;
s not an indication of personal preference. I m&nbsp;&nbsp;
