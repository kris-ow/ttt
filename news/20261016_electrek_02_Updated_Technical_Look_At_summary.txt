Electrek's Jameson Dow gives a technical breakdown of Tesla's newly re-unveiled next-gen Roadster, assessing the physics and practicality behind its demonstrated capabilities (flying, flipping, driving upside down, sub-1-second 0-60) rather than just reporting the reveal.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Roadster re-unveiled after ~9-year delay:** Originally shown in 2017 with a claimed 600-mile range, the next-gen Roadster has now been re-revealed in a different form; Tesla claims a 230+mph top speed but now quotes only "over 300 miles" of range, which the author attributes to a smaller battery — possibly due to reduced power targets, improved cell power density, or reliance on thrusters/fan system to supplement performance instead of a huge battery.
2. **Three trims announced:** Performance, Thrust, and Rocket. Only Rocket is "air ready" with cold gas thrusters (including underneath) for short-duration flight; it is a single-seat variant, with the second seat's space apparently used for larger gas tanks. Tesla says Rocket is invite-only and "not approved for public road use," though the article notes Tesla says nothing similar about the Thrust trim despite it also having rear thrusters.
3. **Upside-down driving via "multi-mode suction system":** A fan-car system (similar to the McMurtry Spierling) creates a low-pressure zone under the car using sealed skirts, letting ambient air pressure stick the car to a surface; a patent reportedly shows the skirts can be adjusted for use beyond smooth track surfaces. Tesla's displayed downforce figure of 4,700kg struck the author as implausibly high for a presumably sub-2,000kg car, especially compared to McMurtry's ~1,000kg car producing around 2,000kg of downforce — the author suspects "liberties were taken" with the number.
4. **Jump/kickflip uses cold gas thrusters, offers no performance benefit:** The ramp jump and kickflip stunts use the same cold-gas-thruster hardware as the flight system; the author argues upward thrust reduces tire load and hurts performance, and the added thruster hardware's weight/complexity further hurts acceleration (citing Force = Mass × Acceleration). Side-mounted thrusters, however, could plausibly aid cornering by adding lateral force independent of tire grip.
5. **0-60 claims rely on rear thrusters:** Tesla showed a recorded 0-60 time of 1 second, then a live demo result (after technical issues) of 0.81 seconds, and later tweeted the top-spec version would do 0-60 in under 0.59 seconds. Four rear ports reportedly produce 80 kilo-Newtons of thrust (a rocket-force unit not directly convertible to horsepower), pushing the car beyond normal tire-traction limits. The author notes thrusters appeared to activate well before the car started moving in slow-motion footage, raising questions about "wind-up" lag and whether official 0-60 rollout-timing conventions were followed.
6. **Top speed unlikely to benefit much from rockets:** At high speed, aerodynamic drag — not tire traction — limits acceleration, so the thrusters' tire-traction-relief function is largely moot; the author is skeptical the quoted 230mph top speed (Rocket trim only) comes mainly from the thrusters, speculating it may instead relate to reduced weight (single seat), higher-rated tires, software governor differences, or a different battery.
7. **Adjustable rear wing enables active aerodynamics:** A movable wing lets the car generate high downforce when cornering (at the cost of drag) while reducing drag at top speed by retracting — a standard "aerodynamic efficiency" technique also used in Formula One, which the author notes can have drag coefficients of 0.7–1.0+ versus ~0.2 for the most efficient EVs.
8. **Practicality concerns around thruster use:** The article questions how quickly cold-gas tanks could be refilled during driving, whether use would divert power from propulsion, and whether the technology could function safely outside a closed course given the large gas plume shown extending "tens of meters." It also questions whether a human driver could physically tolerate the rapid accelerations shown in the flight demo, comparing the forces to those requiring fighter-pilot-level training.

## Market & Competition
1. **Competitive landscape has caught up:** The author notes Tesla is no longer first to market with extreme EV performance — citing the Rimac Nevera R's 300km/h run beating a Bugatti Chiron Super Sport by 3.5 seconds, the Xiaomi SU7 Ultra's Nürburgring record, Porsche's speed claims, BYD's jumping suspension demo, and the McMurtry Spierling (a commercially available track car capable of driving upside down via a fan-car system). A Chinese robot vacuum maker, Dreame, had separately proposed a solid-rocket-booster hypercar producing 100kN of thrust and a 0.9-second 0-60, but canceled the project.

## Editorial Stance
The author is skeptical that the Roadster's rocket/flight gimmicks offer genuine performance or practical value, viewing the jump/kickflip as pure spectacle, and suggests that Tesla's SpaceX references during the presentation signal the event was partly intended to promote the idea of a SpaceX/Tesla merger that could benefit Musk personally.

Notable quotes and data:
- Original 2017 Roadster claim: 600-mile range; new version: "over 300 miles"
- Tesla-claimed downforce: 4,700kg (author questions plausibility)
- 0-60 claims: 1 second (recorded demo), 0.81 seconds (live demo), under 0.59 seconds (tweeted, top-spec)
- Rear thrusters: 80 kilo-Newtons of force across four ports
- Top speed claim: over 230mph (Rocket trim)
