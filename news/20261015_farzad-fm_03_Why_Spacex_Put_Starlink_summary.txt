This video is a technical explainer on why SpaceX placed Starlink satellites in low Earth orbit rather than traditional geostationary orbit, and how the system's phased-array antennas and inter-satellite laser links let it deliver low-latency internet globally, including over oceans and poles with no ground stations.

## SpaceX — Starlink
1. **Low orbit cuts latency dramatically:** Traditional satellite internet uses geostationary satellites parked about 36,000 km above Earth, which adds significant round-trip delay (described as over half a second). Starlink satellites instead orbit around 500 km up — roughly 70 times closer — cutting latency to a few tens of milliseconds, which the narrator says makes the connection feel like cable.
2. **Low orbit requires a massive fleet:** Because low-orbiting satellites move quickly and each one only crosses a given sky for minutes, Starlink needs thousands of satellites in its constellation to maintain continuous coverage, rather than the handful needed for geostationary coverage.
3. **Phased-array dishes track satellites electronically:** Starlink user dishes contain phased-array antennas with hundreds of small antenna elements that shift signal timing in tiny increments to electronically steer the beam toward whichever satellite is overhead, with no physical moving parts, allowing a seamless handoff as satellites pass overhead.
4. **Laser inter-satellite links extend coverage to ground-station-free areas:** Over oceans, poles, and other regions without ground stations, Starlink satellites relay data to each other via laser links, hopping data across space until it reaches a point near a ground station, enabling truly global coverage.
5. **Narrator's framing of SpaceX's business:** The narrator characterizes SpaceX as "a global internet company that happens to own its own rockets," underscoring Starlink's centrality to the company's business model beyond launch services.
