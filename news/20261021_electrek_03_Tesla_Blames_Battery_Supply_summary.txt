Electrek's Fred Lambert examines Tesla's Q3 2026 shareholder letter claim that battery supply, not demand, limited auto sales, arguing the company's own figures contradict that explanation, particularly falling average selling prices.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Deliveries and production down:** Tesla delivered 486,532 vehicles in Q3 2026, down 2% from 497,099 a year earlier. Model 3 and Model Y deliveries fell 1%; "other models" (Cybertruck, Semi, remaining Model S/X) fell 48% to 8,295 units. Tesla produced 464,391 vehicles, meaning it delivered 22,141 more than it built — the second straight quarter of drawing down inventory, following roughly 50,000 more cars built than sold in Q1. Global inventory fell to 11 days of supply, down from 27 days in Q1 and 15 in Q2.
2. **Factory utilization and capacity:** Model 3/Model Y production ran at roughly 1.83 million units annualized against more than 2.25 million units of listed installed capacity across Fremont, Shanghai, Berlin and Texas — about 81% utilization, per the article's calculation.
3. **Record markets amid falling prices:** Tesla reported record deliveries in Canada, Australia, Japan and Taiwan and record production at Gigafactory Berlin, and cited "strong demand" and plans to increase production across factories. However, Tesla also cited a lower year-over-year vehicle average selling price (ASP), even excluding currency effects, as a reason revenue didn't grow — which the article argues is inconsistent with a genuine supply constraint.
4. **Backlog claim without figures:** Tesla stated it ended Q3 with its largest backlog for passenger vehicles in four years but did not disclose a specific number.

## Energy
1. **Battery cells diverted to other products:** Tesla said energy storage had its highest-ever order rate and backlog, Megafactory Texas began production with 50 GWh of planned capacity, and Tesla is increasing 4680 cell output to support Cybercab, Tesla Semi, and higher Model Y volumes. Tesla's outlook also noted deliveries depend on "allocation decisions between sale to customers and use for our owned and operated fleet," suggesting part of the stated battery constraint reflects Tesla's own allocation choices rather than pure external supply shortage.

## Editorial Stance
Lambert argues battery supply is a real constraint (worsened by tariffs on Chinese cells) but that Tesla's "supply constrained" framing is an old talking point not supported by this quarter's data — falling deliveries, falling prices, and a prior quarter of unsold inventory. He suggests the more likely explanation is a mix of modestly recovering demand in some markets and a deliberate choice to route battery cells toward Megapack, Cybercab, and Semi rather than vehicle sales.
