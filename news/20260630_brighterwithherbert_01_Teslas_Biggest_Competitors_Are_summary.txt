This video features host Herbert and guest Jeff Lutz — a former C-suite supply chain executive at Google, Lenovo, and Motorola, now running his own consulting firm — discussing how Tesla's biggest predicted competitors in autonomous driving and EVs have largely failed to execute, while Tesla continues to expand. The core thesis is that a famous analyst chart from years ago ranked Tesla near the bottom of the autonomous driving race and legacy OEMs at the top, but real-world outcomes have inverted that prediction almost entirely. The conversation covers the collapse or retreat of competitors like GM Cruise, Volkswagen/Bosch, Polestar, and Waymo's strained Uber partnership, alongside Tesla's Giga Berlin expansion and the imminent US launch of the Model Y L.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **The "leaders" chart has aged poorly:** Herbert and Jeff reference a widely circulated analyst chart from several years ago that ranked GM Cruise, Waymo, Volkswagen, Daimler, Ford, and others as autonomous driving leaders, with Tesla near the bottom as a "challenger." Jeff notes the chart was never signed by its author and has since been weaponized by Tesla bears. In practice, nearly every company ranked above Tesla has either shut down its program, scaled it back dramatically, or failed to achieve commercial viability, while Tesla's FSD has continued advancing toward mass-market deployment.

2. **SAE level framing was the wrong metric:** Jeff argues that analysts and legacy OEMs obsessed over SAE autonomy levels — a certification-style taxonomy — rather than focusing on consumer-relevant outcomes: miles driven under automation, cost per mile, and scalability. He compares it to marketing a phone by its IP water-resistance rating (e.g., IP67 vs. IP68) instead of explaining what that means practically. Tesla, he says, was always focused on getting the most miles done under automation at the right price point, which is the only metric that ultimately matters commercially.

3. **Volkswagen ends Bosch autonomous driving partnership:** Volkswagen ended its self-driving partnership with Bosch after determining the technology was not competitive with other systems, specifically citing Tesla's FSD as the benchmark. This came alongside Volkswagen cutting up to 100,000 jobs and planning to close four factories in Germany. Herbert notes that just a year prior (approximately June 2025), Volkswagen had publicly stated its goal to overtake Tesla in the robotaxi race by launching an autonomous ID. Buzz in 2026. Elon Musk's response to that claim, per the discussion, was simply: "Guess not."

4. **Mercedes/Daimler's Level 3 quietly dropped:** Herbert points out that Mercedes (with Bosch) claimed to be the first to offer Level 3 autonomy, but with severe restrictions — only on certain highway segments, under 40 mph, only with a lead vehicle, only during certain hours. The feature was eventually discontinued because adoption was negligible and practical utility was near zero, undermining the credibility of their claimed leadership position.

5. **Tesla's lead measured in years, not months:** Jeff pushes back hard on the analyst view that autonomous driving is standard software that competitors can replicate within six months of Tesla solving it. He argues the lead is measurable in multiple years — possibly half a decade — because it requires simultaneously mastering AI capability, manufacturing scale, and unit economics under one roof, a combination no competitor currently has. He references Lars Moravy's articulation of Tesla's integrated manufacturing and AI capability as evidence of a qualitatively different league.

## Robotaxi

1. **Waymo–Uber relationship shows signs of strain:** Uber no longer offers Waymo rides in Phoenix, a development Herbert frames as a second signal of friction between the two companies, following reports of Uber's CTO publicly criticizing Waymo around a month prior. Jeff's interpretation is that this reflects a margin conflict: Waymo may have concluded that offering rides through its own app directly captures more economics than routing through Uber's platform. Jeff warns this is precisely the risk of Uber's platform model — it depends on third-party AV operators who have their own incentive to disintermediate it.

2. **Uber's platform thesis increasingly challenged:** Jeff corrects Herbert's characterization that Uber's stock has been performing well, noting it is down approximately 18% on a 12-month basis and down roughly 8–10% year-to-date. He observes that on days with strong Tesla news, Uber tends to dip, suggesting the market is beginning to price in robotaxi displacement risk. Jeff is skeptical of Uber's stated strategy of aggregating 20-plus AV vendors as a commodity platform, arguing the company's value proposition erodes if Tesla offers rides at a materially lower price point through a vertically integrated stack.

3. **Tesla's vertically integrated unit economics as structural advantage:** Jeff emphasizes that Tesla's strategic choice to own the entire unit economic stack — vehicle hardware, FSD software, fleet management, and ride delivery — is the correct one for long-term margin capture. Outsourcing any layer, as Uber's model requires, creates the exact friction now visible in the Waymo situation. He believes Tesla's robotaxi pricing will be substantially lower than incumbents due to this integration, which will drive adoption beyond the current ride-hailing addressable market into second-car and third-car replacement decisions.

4. **Robotaxi supply remains a binding constraint today:** Jeff recounts personal experience attempting to hail both a Waymo and a Tesla robotaxi in the Bay Area and being unable to get either due to insufficient fleet size. He describes the space as still "very nascent" and flags that the total addressable market analysis for robotaxi is being systematically undercounted by analysts who simply apply current ride-hailing rates rather than modeling the incremental demand created once price and availability reach mass-market thresholds.

5. **Cybercab production ramp and risk assessment:** Herbert references a community analyst (identified as "Xander") who used VIN numbers to estimate approximately 850 Cybercabs may already have been produced, up from earlier estimates of 100–200. Jeff argues Tesla's robotaxi risk is lower than it appears because: (a) Tesla has a profitable base auto business generating 1.7–2 million units per year; (b) the Cybercab platform uses Tesla's Unboxed manufacturing process and meets Tesla's safety standards; and (c) the fundamental question is no longer "will FSD work" but rather "when does it get calibrated to the Cybercab form factor," given it already works in Model Y today.

6. **Robotaxi as a re-rating, not a base business event:** Jeff frames robotaxi deployment as a "re-rating event" for Tesla — not essential to the core auto business case, but potentially transformative if it succeeds. He draws a parallel to the smartphone emergence, noting that at the time of the iPhone's launch no one anticipated scaling from hundreds of thousands to hundreds of millions of units annually. He suggests a similar non-linear outcome is possible if robotaxi displaces second-car ownership decisions at scale.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Giga Berlin double production expansion:** Tesla executive André Thierig announced that Giga Berlin is expanding production in two stages: first from 5,000 to 6,200 vehicles per week starting in July (with 1,000 new hires), and then a further increase to 7,500 vehicles per week beginning a ramp from October, adding another 1,000 employees — totaling approximately 3,500 additional hires for the year. This is occurring while Volkswagen is simultaneously closing four German factories and cutting up to 100,000 jobs, a stark contrast Herbert emphasizes.

2. **Herbert's theory: FSD Europe approval driving Berlin expansion:** Herbert offers his own hypothesis that the Berlin production expansion is driven by two catalysts: introduction of the refreshed Model Y at Giga Berlin, and early signals that FSD regulatory approval is expanding across Europe. He notes FSD has already received approval in approximately five to six European countries, and speculates Germany, Italy, and Spain — larger markets — may be next, which would justify front-loading hiring and capacity.

3. **Model Y L spotted in California, US launch described as imminent:** A camouflaged Tesla Model Y L (the extended, six-seat version) was spotted in Palo Alto, California on 19- or 20-inch wheels, with a community observer describing the US launch as "imminent." Jeff and Herbert both believe the Model Y L will outperform the standard Model Y in North America specifically because American consumers have a demonstrated preference for larger vehicles. Jeff suggests it could enter production as soon as the current quarter and become a major volume driver through the remainder of 2026. Herbert notes the Model Y L has already launched in other global markets, with North America being the last region to receive it.

4. **Polestar exits the US market:** Herbert and Jeff discuss Polestar's withdrawal from the United States, noting the brand had previously run advertising explicitly mocking Tesla. Jeff explains the exit was driven by a US trade ruling that ended the credit arrangement under which Polestar (owned by Geely) was importing China-built vehicles offset by Volvo US plant exports. Jeff's view is that the trade ruling was a final blow to a brand that was already failing on its own merits — no meaningful sales traction, products priced too high, and no self-driving capability.

## Market & Competition

1. **GM Cruise: collapsed and only partially revived:** Herbert recounts that GM Cruise, ranked as an autonomous driving leader on the famous analyst chart, was shut down following a serious accident and has since been revived only in a much smaller, limited form with no meaningful commercial-scale activity currently. Uber had announced a partnership with GM Cruise within approximately 90 days of Cruise's shutdown, which Jeff cites as evidence that Uber's partnership announcements lack due diligence rigor.

2. **Chinese EV manufacturers overhyped as European threat:** Jeff dismisses the narrative that Chinese EV manufacturers would rapidly take over Western European auto factories as "absurd." He argues that no rational Chinese OEM would want to acquire aging legacy factory infrastructure incompatible with their manufacturing methods. He notes that Chinese imports into Europe remain a slow-moving glut rather than a manufacturing takeover, and that Tesla's established brand, Supercharger network, service infrastructure, and FSD approvals in Europe give it a durable competitive position against Chinese entrants who still need to build all of that from scratch.

3. **Legacy OEM failure pattern: make vs. buy misjudgment:** Jeff identifies a structural cause for legacy OEM autonomous driving failures: C-suite executives made "build it ourselves" decisions without the engineering capability to execute, then failed to maintain accountability through to launch. He contrasts this with companies that were essentially using autonomous driving announcements as fundraising vehicles — deploying prototypes on curated routes to impress investors rather than solving the underlying engineering problem. Tesla, he argues, actually hired the engineers, built the compute (referencing Dojo implicitly), and did the work.
