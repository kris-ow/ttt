Analyst Larry Goldberg presents a Monte Carlo simulation model (20,000 runs) forecasting Tesla's robotaxi fleet and revenue growth through 2027. His central finding is that while absolute revenue and fleet numbers in 2027 remain modest, the quarter-over-quarter doubling growth rate is the more important signal for investors, and a meaningful share of the fleet may eventually be customer-owned rather than Tesla-owned.

## Robotaxi
1. **Fleet doubling every quarter, reaching ~50,000 by end of 2027 (P50 case):** Goldberg's base-case (median/P50) scenario shows the robotaxi fleet starting around 1,100 units currently (Tesla-owned), roughly doubling each quarter to about 2,200, 4,400, 8,800, and approximately 17,500 by Q3 2027, then jumping to roughly 50,000 by Q4 2027 as customer-owned cars begin joining the network. He attributes quarterly fleet growth multipliers of 1.6x (slow), 2.0x (normal), and 2.6x (fast) across his three growth modes, with Cybercab production pace as the binding constraint on fleet size.
2. **Customer-owned cars projected to join starting mid-2027:** Goldberg's model assumes customer-owned robotaxi cars begin joining the network in small numbers around Q2–Q3 2027 (a few hundred initially, limited to fans in Austin per Goldberg), growing to roughly 7,500 and then approximately 32,000 by Q4 2027, with Tesla taking a cut of each fare. He notes the model's results are not highly sensitive to whether cars are customer-owned or Tesla-owned — fleet size, not ownership structure, drives revenue.
3. **Cost per mile assumption: $2, with real-world Austin and Dallas rideshare pricing running higher:** The model uses a cost-per-mile range of $1.30–$2, down from an initial planning point of $2. Goldberg notes this may be the model's weakest input, citing his own recent experience in Austin where rideshare pricing (including an Uber ride outside the service area that cost roughly six dollars per mile) ran well above the model's assumption; he still believes $2 is reasonable near-term given Tesla's margin flexibility, but flags it as a likely underestimate.
4. **Fleet growth is the dominant driver of revenue, not pricing or safety-driver costs:** Goldberg states the correlation between fleet growth and revenue/profit outcomes is very high (he cites a correlation rank of 0.85), making vehicle fleet growth the single most important variable to track — far more than cost-per-mile or safety-driver-related costs, which he says have comparatively little effect on the model's outcomes.
5. **90th-percentile (optimistic) scenario reaches roughly 100,000–110,000 cars:** In the model's P90 case, the fleet roughly doubles the P50 case to about 110,000 cars by end of 2027, implying revenue around $2.5 billion for that year, versus roughly $348 million in the P10 (pessimistic) case.

## Financials
1. **2027 robotaxi revenue projected at ~$900 million to ~$1 billion in base case:** Goldberg's model shows a wide P10–P90 revenue range for fiscal 2027 of about $348 million to $2.5 billion (he also cites an upper figure near $2.517 billion), with the most likely (median) outcome around $900 million to $1 billion — a relatively small figure that he says barely moves Tesla's overall financial performance.
2. **Robotaxi network projected to break even and turn profitable by mid/late 2027:** The model shows the robotaxi business breaking even on network costs around mid-2027, becoming profitable in Q3 2027, and exiting 2027 at an annualized pace of about $3 billion in the base case (with an optimistic scenario approaching $11 billion annualized). Goldberg projects quarterly revenue reaching roughly half a billion dollars by Q4 2027, growing toward $1 billion in Q1 2028 and potentially $2–4 billion per quarter shortly after, driven by the fleet's ongoing doubling pattern — though he cautions against firm 2028 predictions.
3. **Key uncertainty drivers: production ramp pace and regulatory city approvals:** Goldberg identifies the two biggest swing factors in his model as how quickly Tesla can manufacture and deploy robotaxi vehicles, and how quickly regulators approve new cities/states for operation. He also flags the possibility of a safety-related incident causing a regulatory pause as a risk to the growth curve, though his model treats this as having limited overall revenue impact.
