Tesla's abrupt discontinuation of the Solar Roof has left third-party certified installers absorbing financial losses, according to reporting by Roofing Contractor and Electrek's own conversations with contractors.

## Energy
1. **Solar Roof discontinued after limited scale:** Tesla stopped supplying Solar Roof tiles in August, telling certified installers it would ship only conventional solar panels going forward; the tesla.com/solarroof page now redirects to Tesla's solar panel page and "Solar Roof" has been removed from the Energy navigation menu. Over roughly seven years, Tesla installed only about 3,000 Solar Roof systems in the US, peaking at 21–32 installations per week — over 95% short of Elon Musk's earlier promise of 1,000 weekly installations by 2019–2020. Electrek's earlier reporting (referenced but not detailed here) said Tesla internally deemed the product "not financially viable."

2. **Installers bore training and tooling costs:** Certified installers — including Elevated Roofing & Siding (Dayton, OH), PGT Home Energy Solutions (Tempe, AZ), Westfall Roofing (FL), and Saguaro Solar, Electric & Roofing (Tucson) — had to send crews to multi-week West Coast training and complete supervised installations before certification. Westfall Roofing president Ryan Westfall said this represented real investment in learning the system. Solar Roof installation took roughly two-plus weeks versus about two days for a conventional rack-mounted system, and contractors are now scrambling to redesign active projects or switch systems mid-pipeline.

3. **Installers describe six-figure losses:** Electrek spoke with installers who said they invested hundreds of thousands of dollars into building crews, tooling, and training around the Solar Roof and now have little to show for it. Several declined to criticize Tesla publicly, citing concern over jeopardizing their ongoing relationships selling Tesla's other energy products (solar panels, Powerwall).

4. **Warranty and long-term service doubts:** Tesla says it will honor existing Solar Roof warranties and continue providing replacement parts. Contractors expressed skepticism this will hold over the systems' 25-year life: PGT's Greg Field doubted long-term parts availability, and Exact Solar's Aaron Nichols warned of much higher future labor costs given the tiles' labor-intensive service and complex electrical connections. Todd Miller of Isaiah Industries said Tesla treated the Solar Roof primarily as solar technology rather than a roofing product, underestimating the complexity of the roofing business.

## Editorial Stance
Fred Lambert argues that Tesla's earlier shift to third-party certified installers, which once looked like a rational retreat from a "low-margin, labor-heavy, local business," instead let Tesla offload the hardest, least profitable parts of the Solar Roof business (installation, customer relationships, training/tooling costs) onto small local contractors before abruptly killing the product — leaving Tesla with a manageable warranty liability on ~3,000 roofs while installers are left with stranded investment and the burden of servicing a discontinued product for decades. He suggests this damages trust and could make contractors wary of partnering with Tesla or similar manufacturers again.
