SpaceX catches a rocket
booster taller than a
20-story building in
mid-air with two
giant
metal arms
called "
chopsticks."
Why not just
land it on
pylons like the Falcon 9?  Because the
supports are heavy.  Every
pound of support is a pound of
payload you
can't take.  In
addition, the supports need to
be inspected, assembled, and
maintained.  Therefore,
SpaceX moved the supports
to the tower.  So how do
you catch something
that big?
The booster
returns to the
launch pad
and steers itself using
lattice rudders.
Near the tower itself, it
applies final
braking with
the central
engines and slides
sideways between the open
levers.  The levers
close,
the accelerator
rests on them with
small
attachment points on top, and
the engines shut down.
Now it hangs on a
tower right above the
launch pad from
which it took off.  SpaceX
tried this for the first time
in October 2024.
Look.  It
worked on the first
try.  But capture
was never the
main goal.
The main thing is a quick
turnaround.  The booster,
standing on its own
launch tower, does not
need to be
trucked,
lifted, or
moved.  SpaceX wants the
Super Heavy
to land,
refuel, and
fly again, even on the same
day.  Think about an
airplane.  He pulls up
to the gate,
refuels, and
flies again.  Nobody
throws it away after
a flight.  This tower is like a
gate for missiles.  It
transforms them from something
you build to something
you manage.  And to
me, that first
catch was
the moment when space
began
to transform from
something we could barely
afford to do to something
we just did.  That's
why SpaceX isn't putting
it on the supports.
