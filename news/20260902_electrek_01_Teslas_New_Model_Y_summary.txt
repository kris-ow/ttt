Chinese owners of Tesla's Model Y L (the 3-row Model Y variant) are reporting rear suspension collapse issues on Chinese social media, prompting fans to devise a "finger test" to check whether a vehicle is affected.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Rear suspension collapse complaints:** Reports first surfaced on the Chinese product review site smzdm.com in early August, based on complaints from Xiaohongshu (dubbed "Chinese Instagram") spanning June through August. Smzdm found at least ten posts documenting the issue, with hundreds of comments from other owners reporting similar problems. Owners describe a "swaying" feeling at the rear of affected vehicles, and a low rear suspension can affect handling and cause premature tire wear.

2. **How owners are testing for the defect:** A quick "finger test" involves checking how many fingers fit between the wheel and wheel arch — 4 fingers is fine, 3 is concerning, fewer than 3 suggests the suspension has likely collapsed. A more precise measuring-tape method checks wheel arch height from the ground; normal range is 764-804mm, and per Tesla's own service guidelines, measurements below 764mm qualify for a warranty replacement.

3. **Affected batch and Tesla's response:** Most affected vehicles appear to be from the first delivery batch (September-October 2025). Most have been repaired under warranty, though the issue has reportedly recurred on some vehicles even after service. Tesla China has not made a public statement on the issue; social media users are advising owners to check and log suspension height monthly as a precaution, especially for older vehicles.

4. **Broader context of Tesla suspension issues:** The US NHTSA is currently probing Tesla over front suspension failures (a recurring issue), separate from this rear suspension problem. In Germany, the Model Y reportedly showed a high defect rate in annual vehicle testing, with suspension flagged as the main issue source.

## Editorial Stance
The author (Jameson Dow) speculates that it's unclear whether the rear suspension issue will affect the US-market Model Y L, launched in July, since Chinese models are built in Shanghai while US models are built in Texas, and past differences have existed between the two plants' builds. The author advises US and other overseas Shanghai-built Model Y L owners (e.g., in Australia and New Zealand) to also monitor their rear suspension as a precaution.
