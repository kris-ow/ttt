Herbert hosts financial analyst Cern Basher to discuss two of his articles: one reframing the Cybercab as a "passenger-mile manufacturing machine" optimized for lifetime cost-per-mile rather than sticker price, and a second cataloguing 420 things autonomous vehicles like Cybercab could reduce or eliminate across society. The core thesis is that nearly every unusual Cybercab design choice — two seats, no glass roof, no paint shop, front-wheel drive, self-closing doors, integrated Starlink — serves the singular goal of driving down cost per mile over a vehicle life that could reach hundreds of thousands or even a million miles, and that this cost structure (not just the price of driving) is what will let Tesla undercut Waymo and other robotaxi competitors.

## Robotaxi
1. **Lifetime cost-per-mile, not sticker price, is the real target:** Basher argues Tesla's design objective for Cybercab isn't minimizing unit cost but minimizing cost per passenger mile over the vehicle's economic life; spending more per vehicle at the factory (e.g., an extra $3,000) can be a rational trade-off if it extends vehicle lifespan from 300,000 to 500,000 or even a million miles, since a fleet serving fixed passenger-mile demand needs far fewer vehicles at higher per-vehicle durability.
2. **Modeled fleet cost savings from durability:** In Basher's model, a fleet built to serve 1 billion passenger miles costs about $100 million at 300,000-mile vehicle life ($30,000/vehicle, 3,333 vehicles), drops to $66 million at 500,000-mile life (33,000 vehicles at higher per-unit cost), and falls to $33 million at a million-mile life (1,000 vehicles) — a roughly 67% fleet-cost reduction versus the 300,000-mile baseline.
3. **Cost-per-mile curve from vehicle cost alone:** Per Basher's model, vehicle cost alone falls from roughly 10 cents/mile at 300,000-mile life (a $30,000 vehicle) to about 6.6 cents/mile at 500,000 miles and 3.3 cents/mile at a million miles.
4. **Full cost-per-mile stack across utilization rates:** Basher's fully loaded model (including charging, cleaning, parking, insurance, liability reserve, tires/maintenance, and vehicle/financing cost) shows total cost per mile of roughly 45–55 cents for a 300,000-mile, $30,000 vehicle; with $3,000 more spent at the factory pushing life to 500,000 miles and assumed improvements (10¢/kWh charging, cheaper automated cleaning, lower insurance/liability from FSD safety gains, longer-lived tires), total cost drops to roughly 30–40 cents per mile; at a million-mile vehicle life the model reaches about 25–30 cents per mile, with vehicle cost (~3 cents/mile) no longer the dominant line item — comparable to cleaning cost.
5. **Utilization matters less than expected:** Basher's model shows cost per mile is largely driven by mileage-related costs (charging, cleaning, tire wear) rather than utilization rate, so moving from ~30% to ~65% fleet utilization produces only a modest cost-per-mile improvement — a result Herbert pushed back on as counterintuitive but which Basher maintains.
6. **Waymo cost comparison (attributed estimate):** Basher and Herbert discuss that Waymo's higher per-vehicle cost (cited as roughly $120,000 vs. Tesla's ~$30,000), shorter vehicle life, and lack of optimization for automated charging/cleaning/repair mean Waymo likely cannot go below roughly $2/mile even if its self-driving technology matches Tesla's, versus a potential $1/mile or lower for Tesla — this is Basher's inference from his cost model, not a confirmed Waymo figure.
7. **Design rationale for a purpose-built two-seat robotaxi:** The two-seat Cybercab design (versus using existing four-seat Model Y for robotaxi service, which Tesla already does in four cities) reflects a deliberate choice to push cost per mile toward a target Tesla has cited of around 50 cents/mile or lower, since most rides carry one or two passengers; Basher notes no other AV maker has built a dedicated two-seat production robotaxi.
8. **Parts and manufacturing efficiency:** Cybercab reportedly has roughly half the parts of a Model 3; its electric motor was designed for sub-10-second automated assembly, and Tesla's "unboxed" manufacturing process is said to cut the production line's footprint by about 50%, roughly doubling line capacity in the same factory space.
9. **Cern Basher's "420 things" framework:** Basher's second article catalogs 420 items that autonomous vehicles could reduce or eliminate, grouped as 206 time/hassle items, 78 money/law items, 77 city/space items, and 59 life/body items; he estimates 70% of these affect individuals personally, 11% households, and about 20% society/institutions; by theme, roughly 25% are safety-related, 15% time-related, 14% space-related, and 9% each money- and labor-related, with parking (48 items) and unsafe driving behavior (47 items) as the largest single categories.
10. **Illustrative categories from the 420-item list:** Examples discussed include eliminating road rage, drunk/distracted/drowsy driving, teen-driver risk, car theft and carjacking, children left in hot cars, parking search and garage-related stress, insurance claims and emergency-service burden from crashes, elderly parents losing driving independence, and transportation access for disabled or low-income individuals; Elon Musk has stated a goal of making robotaxi rides cheaper than a bus.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Automatic collision avoidance rollout:** Tesla recently launched automatic collision avoidance (requiring FSD to be active currently, with wider rollout planned), where the car can autonomously avoid a collision beyond standard automatic emergency braking using its camera system — reducing expected accident rates further, per Basher.

## Electric Vehicles / Market & Competition
1. **Design tradeoffs for battery longevity:** Cybercab's battery and motor are reportedly tuned for longevity rather than performance — top speed is capped around 85 mph, and the car is not designed for rapid acceleration — which Basher and Herbert suggest supports higher lifetime mileage versus a performance-oriented consumer EV.
2. **Durability and repairability by design:** Cybercab uses a steel inner structure paired with color-molded recycled polymer exterior panels (no paint shop) that show the underlying color when scratched and can be swapped in minutes without repainting, intended to let the vehicle's body be refreshed indefinitely while the structural core lasts far longer than a typical consumer car.
