NHTSA denied a petition seeking a defect investigation into hidden manual door releases on the Tesla Model 3, but granted a separate petition to begin formal rulemaking on emergency door-egress standards for the entire auto industry.

## Autonomous Driving / Electric Vehicles / Market & Competition
1. **Petition denial details:** NHTSA's denial, published the day of the article, covers 179,031 model year 2022 Model 3 vehicles. The petition was filed November 7, 2025 by Tesla owner Kevin Clouse, alleging the Model 3's manual door release violated federal standard FMVSS 206 by not being readily accessible or clearly identifiable. NHTSA found the standard contains no requirements addressing labeling or location of a mechanical release, and as of March 13, 2026 identified only one matching consumer complaint — traced to Clouse's own VIN, describing a front-impact crash where the car lost power and the electric door mechanism failed. NHTSA noted the 2022 Model 3 owner's manual includes an "In Case of Emergency" section illustrating the manual release location, and concluded rulemaking, not a defect investigation, was the appropriate path.

2. **New rulemaking granted:** A separate petition filed November 4, 2025 asking NHTSA to mandate "a robust and obvious door egress system in all motor vehicles" was granted; NHTSA will begin formal rulemaking. The agency stated a concealed or hard-to-locate door release could prevent occupants from exiting during a crash or fire.

3. **Broader regulatory and legal context:** In September 2025, NHTSA opened an investigation into Model Y door handles after reports of children trapped in back seats when the 12-volt system died; Tesla subsequently said it was redesigning door handles to combine electronic and manual releases into one motion. In December, NHTSA opened a separate probe into Model 3 emergency door releases following reports of fatal crashes where occupants couldn't exit. In January, Congress introduced the SAFE Exit Act, citing 15 deaths linked to door-handle access. Tesla faces multiple fatal door-handle lawsuits and has already shipped a mechanical fix on cars sold in China.

4. **Practical impact:** A defect investigation could have forced a recall on a timeline of months; rulemaking instead sets an industry-wide standard but typically takes years and would apply only to future vehicles, not the Teslas currently on the road.

## Editorial Stance
The author (Fred Lambert) argues the denial is not a clean win for Tesla — NHTSA denied it on a technicality while simultaneously starting rulemaking, which it wouldn't do over a non-issue. The author criticizes NHTSA's "one complaint" framing given the parallel open Model 3 and Model Y investigations, pending lawsuits, and Congressional bill citing 15 deaths, and argues Tesla made a design choice to rely on electric door releases with poorly located manual backups — a problem Tesla already fixed in China. The author calls rulemaking the right long-term move but notes it won't recall any of the millions of Teslas currently on the road with the same issue.
