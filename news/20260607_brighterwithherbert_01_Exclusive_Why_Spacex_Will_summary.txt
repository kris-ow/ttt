This video is a deep dive into SpaceX's business model and upcoming IPO, featuring Aaron Bernett of Mach 33 Financial Group and venture investor Larry Goldberg. The core thesis is that most investors are misreading SpaceX as a distant-future bet, when in fact near-term revenue drivers — particularly Starlink and terrestrial AI compute — are already materializing, with the IPO potentially pulling forward growth that many assume is years away. While this is a SpaceX-focused discussion, passing references are made to Tesla's robotaxi business and Optimus as parallel opportunities for comparison.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX IPO framing and investor misperception:** The hosts argue that the most common mistake investors make with SpaceX is treating it as a 10-years-away story about Mars, moon cities, and orbital AI. Instead, Bernett contends the business is already generating meaningful revenue through Starlink and is approaching an inflection point. The IPO share price at the time of recording was described as $135, with speculation that it could close near $200.

2. **Starlink as the primary near-term revenue engine:** Bernett's model projects Starlink revenue of approximately $221–228 billion by 2030, driven by both broadband and direct-to-cell (Starlink Mobile) subscribers. The model shows a sharp acceleration in customer growth beginning around 2028–2029, coinciding with the deployment of V3 Starlink satellites via Starship. Bernett frames Starlink as having high IRR comparable to a telco oversubscription model, where the same infrastructure is sold to many customers simultaneously at very high margins.

3. **AI compute as a longer-term but massive opportunity:** Goldman Sachs is cited as estimating SpaceX's AI business at $322 billion by 2030 — a figure Bernett characterizes as significantly higher than his own more conservative model. Bernett's model includes both terrestrial and orbital AI data centers, with orbital compute beginning to generate meaningful revenue around 2028–2031. The model frames terrestrial AI centers as primarily for training large models, while orbital infrastructure is positioned for inference workloads. A key insight from the model: approximately 200 Starship launches would be required to put one gigawatt of AI compute into orbit — equivalent to Colossus 1 and 2 — and the model projects that cadence being reached around 2031.

4. **Capital allocation as the central modeling framework:** Rather than building a traditional top-down revenue model, Mach 33 uses an IRR-driven capital allocation framework. Each year, available capital and Starship launch capacity are allocated to the business line with the highest return on investment. Currently, that is Starlink; around 2030, the model projects that Starlink's IRR will compress (as the market saturates and broadband prices fall) and AI compute IRR will rise, triggering a capital shift toward orbital data centers and AI infrastructure.

5. **Terafab chip manufacturing as a key variable:** Bernett's team performed a bottom-up cost analysis of Terafab and estimated that at 100 wafer starts per month — described as roughly one-tenth to one-hundredth of Terafab's ultimate capacity — SpaceX could produce approximately 44 million chips (at ~70% yield). This was framed as sufficient for one chip per Optimus unit and two chips per robotaxi vehicle with chips left over. Chip availability is flagged as a key risk to the timeline for orbital compute.

6. **Valuation range and SpaceX reaching $10 trillion:** Using a traditional DCF (Gordon Growth Model), Bernett's team estimates SpaceX's end-of-2026 valuation at approximately $2.48 trillion, with an upper bound around $5.5 trillion using exit multiple methodology. The model projects SpaceX reaching $10 trillion in market capitalization somewhere between 2030 and 2035. Bernett frames this as a dollar-cost-averaging opportunity in the "core infrastructure of the future," not a trading vehicle.

7. **Launch cadence and Starship scalability:** The model projects roughly 600–700 total Starship launches by 2030, equating to approximately two launches per day across SpaceX's planned five launch towers in two locations. Larry Goldberg argues that launch revenue is being underestimated industry-wide, noting that as Starship becomes fully reusable (every component recovered intact) and costs collapse, the total addressable market for launch will expand dramatically beyond what Falcon 9 history would suggest.

8. **AI application layer as uncapped upside:** Both Goldberg and Bernett emphasize that the highest-margin opportunity is not infrastructure rental (like the Anthropic compute deal) but owning the AI application layer — what Goldberg calls "Macrohard." Bernett notes that today, roughly 80% or more of AI stack profit flows to chip manufacturers (Nvidia) and hyperscalers, but over time that margin shifts toward application owners. SpaceX's full vertical stack — chips, compute, connectivity, applications — positions it to capture margin at every layer.

9. **Tesla robotaxi parallel:** Goldberg draws an explicit parallel between Tesla's transition from EVs to robotaxi and SpaceX's transition from launch services to Starlink and AI compute. He also notes that $221 billion per year in Starlink revenue is roughly equivalent in scale to what 10 million robotaxis might generate, but argues Starlink carries far lower operational overhead than a physical robotaxi fleet.
