This video features Jo Bhakdi interviewing analyst Cern Basher about the potential Tesla-SpaceX merger, the upcoming SpaceX IPO, and what both events could mean for Tesla shareholders in the short and long term. The core debate centers on whether a merger would be net positive or negative for Tesla investors, with both hosts agreeing it is strongly beneficial long-term but potentially dilutive to near-term Tesla returns. The discussion also covers SpaceX's unique "infinite TAM" thesis built around orbital data centers, Starship's readiness, and the risk that neither company's shareholders can fully control the timing or terms of a merger.

## Market & Competition

1. **SpaceX IPO timing and initial valuation:** Cern Basher anticipates the SpaceX IPO is imminent, with a likely IPO valuation of approximately $1.75 trillion, potentially trading up to $2.25 trillion on listing enthusiasm. He cites index-fund buying as a likely driver of a post-IPO melt-up, but warns that without near-term cash flow catalysts, SpaceX stock could be vulnerable to a market downturn, as it would trade like a high-multiple growth or "fantasy" stock. Jo Bhakdi adds that the IPO could be "super hyped" if broader market conditions remain bullish, and both agree the share price could decline meaningfully in the period between IPO and the eventual cash-flow inflection point.

2. **SpaceX as a "one of one" company with an infinite TAM:** Both hosts argue SpaceX's opportunity is qualitatively unlike anything previously valued by markets. Cern Basher compares it to being able to buy all of North America's land in its early days, while Jo Bhakdi coins the analogy of a "magic moment" — similar to early colonial trade routes — where a ship goes out and comes back with more money than it left with. The key enabling business model cited is orbital AI data centers, which Jo Bhakdi argues is infinitely scalable because demand for AI compute has no natural ceiling. Cern Basher adds that beyond data centers, pharmaceutical manufacturing, Bitcoin mining, and other energy-intensive industries may also compete for Starship launches.

3. **National security as a hidden SpaceX revenue driver:** Jo Bhakdi highlights a reported Starlink defense contract worth $1.7 billion per year with the Pentagon for intelligence services, suggesting this is an underappreciated dimension of SpaceX's business. Both hosts discuss the strategic military value of space dominance — controlling satellites, being able to disable adversaries' satellites, and owning the "highest ground" — as a driver of large, near-certain government contracts. Cern Basher notes this is a dimension of SpaceX's business that could produce material, surprise contract announcements at any time.

4. **Chamath Palihapitiya as a credibility signal for the merger:** Jo Bhakdi opens by noting that Chamath has publicly stated the Tesla-SpaceX merger is "99% sure" to happen. Cern Basher treats this as a meaningful signal given Chamath's proximity to Elon Musk, drawing a parallel to Chamath's reported suggestion that SpaceX partner with Anthropic, which was followed just days later by an announcement of exactly such a deal. Both hosts interpret this as evidence Chamath may be "in the know" rather than speculating independently.

5. **Short-term versus long-term merger calculus for Tesla shareholders:** Jo Bhakdi states that after doing his own modeling, he believes the merger is likely short-term negative for Tesla shareholders — roughly in the 12-to-18-month window — because a hyped SpaceX IPO could attract capital flows away from Tesla. He estimates that Tesla alone would likely outperform a merged entity during the first 12 to 18 months post-IPO, but that beyond that inflection point, a merged entity would likely generate significantly more value. Cern Basher broadly agrees, framing this as a structural tension between Elon's very long-term orientation and shareholders who have already waited years for autonomy and robotaxi to be reflected in Tesla's share price.

6. **Merger ratio and Tesla valuation creating structural complications:** Jo Bhakdi raises the concern that if Tesla's robotaxi ramp causes Tesla's market cap to exceed SpaceX's — for example, reaching $1,000 or $1,500 per share — a merger of equals becomes structurally difficult or impossible. He suggests there could be a perceived incentive for Elon to prevent Tesla from rising too far ahead of SpaceX, which would complicate the merger math. Cern Basher dismisses the idea that Elon would actively hold Tesla back, both because it is not in his character and because doing so would expose him to serious legal liability, but acknowledges the structural incentive exists.

7. **Starship readiness as the binding constraint:** Both hosts identify Starship's readiness for reliable, reusable orbital launches as the single most important gating factor for SpaceX's commercial revenue ramp. Jo Bhakdi notes that Google and other potential data center customers will not sign trillion-dollar contracts until SpaceX can actually deliver at scale. The conversation references an upcoming Starship launch (described as a "big deal" happening "this Wednesday" from the video's publish date context, approximately mid-May 2026) as a near-term milestone. Cern Basher notes Elon has stated Tesla plans to build 10 more Starships and 5 more boosters in the current year, which he characterizes as "max capacity" and "not enough" for the scale of demand envisioned.

8. **Rebellionare and community debate:** Jo Bhakdi credits a community figure called "Rebellionaire" with sparking the public debate about whether the merger would betray Tesla shareholders. While both hosts take the concern seriously — particularly for older investors who have held Tesla for years and want near-term returns — they ultimately conclude that opposing the merger on short-term grounds is not a sound position, since the long-term compounding from a combined entity is likely superior, and the timing is ultimately Elon's decision to make.
