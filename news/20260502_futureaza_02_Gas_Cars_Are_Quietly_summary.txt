This video is a recorded community presentation (not a YouTube commentary channel) focused on the financial case for electric vehicle ownership, specifically comparing EV running costs against internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles. The presenter, speaking to a live audience, walks through each component of the AAA's estimated $12,000/year cost of car ownership — with particular emphasis on fuel costs, maintenance savings, and depreciation — and argues that EVs offer meaningful savings, especially for drivers who can charge at home.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **AAA Cost-of-Ownership Baseline:** The presenter references AAA's estimate that owning a car costs approximately $12,000 per year on average, with depreciation being the largest single component. The average new car transaction price is cited at $50,000, with straight-line depreciation of approximately $4,680/year over 10 years. The presenter's key recommendation is to buy pre-owned in order to let someone else absorb the steepest early depreciation.

2. **Fuel Cost Comparison — The "Factor of 8" Rule:** The presenter explains the EPA's MPG-equivalent metric, noting that one gallon of gasoline contains 33.7 kWh of energy. Because ICE engines operate at roughly 18% efficiency while EVs operate at 85–90% efficiency from the utility meter to wheels, EVs effectively use energy at about 8x the efficiency of gas vehicles in terms of miles per unit of energy cost. This "factor of 8" is presented as the key tool for comparing electricity costs to gasoline costs. Example comparisons given: a small gas car at 32 MPG vs. an equivalent EV at 4 miles/kWh; a Silverado at 16 MPG vs. 2 miles/kWh electric; a 60-passenger school bus at 6 MPG vs. 75 miles/kWh electric.

3. **Oregon Electricity Pricing vs. National Gas Prices:** At Portland General Electric's rate of 19 cents/kWh, the presenter calculates driving electric is equivalent to paying about $1.50/gallon of gasoline. Forest Grove Power and Light at 9 cents/kWh equates to roughly $0.69/gallon equivalent. Oregon's statewide average electricity rate is cited at 9 cents/kWh. By contrast, Oregon average gas prices were among the highest in the nation at the time of the presentation, while Oklahoma had the cheapest gas nationally at approximately $3-range pricing. Electricity rates have risen at roughly 8%/year over the past five years (including an ~18% jump in 2024 for PGE), but are described as more predictable than volatile gasoline prices.

4. **Public Charging Cost Comparison:** Public DC fast charging at Electrify America (e.g., Walmart locations) runs approximately 62 cents/kWh — equivalent to over $5/gallon. Tesla Superchargers in the Midwest were cited at 47 cents/kWh. Level 2 public chargers (e.g., Electric Avenue) run around 30 cents/kWh. Free charging options exist at some employers, city government facilities, and private residences. The presenter notes that unlike gasoline — where travel away from home tends to be cheaper — EV charging gets more expensive the farther you travel from home.

5. **Maintenance Savings:** AAA estimates $1,500/year in maintenance costs for average vehicles, though the presenter believes this is skewed toward older cars. EVs are described as having significantly lower maintenance needs: roughly 9 moving parts in an electric drivetrain vs. ~300 in a gasoline engine, reduced brake wear due to regenerative braking, and fewer fluids (no oil changes, transmission fluid, or separate brake fluid in many cases). Increased tire wear is acknowledged due to vehicle weight and aggressive acceleration capability.

6. **Insurance and Registration Considerations:** EV insurance is noted as potentially higher, partly because battery damage from puncture typically totals the vehicle. On registration, the presenter describes a complex Oregon legislative situation where an EV road-usage fee increase was passed while a parallel gas tax increase was repealed by petition, leaving EV owners facing higher registration fees. A potential mandatory road usage charge could add approximately $300/year, though this is framed as still only ~$30/month net.

7. **Upcoming Used EV Supply Glut:** The presenter flags an anticipated oversupply of used EVs coming to market over the next two to three years. Approximately 300,000 EV leases are expiring in the current year (2026), approximately 600,000 the following year (2027), and more in 2028 — totaling roughly one million lease expirations. Many lessees took federal tax credits and may not be able to afford buyouts, leading to a wave of off-lease returns. The presenter suggests this could make used EVs cheaper than equivalent used gas cars.

## Market & Competition

1. **Oil Price Volatility and Inelastic Demand:** The presenter uses the example of a Strait of Hormuz blockage reducing world oil supply by 20% causing over a 50% price increase to illustrate how inelastic gasoline demand is for single-car households. The 2022 Ukraine invasion and a 2026 U.S. military action involving Iran are cited as two recent events that caused significant gasoline and diesel price spikes. Diesel was disproportionately affected in both cases due to crude oil composition differences. Having a multi-fuel household (59% of American households own two or more vehicles) is presented as a hedge that makes overall fuel demand more elastic.

2. **Charging Network Reliability Improvements:** Consumer Reports data is cited as showing significant improvement in public charging network reliability. The presenter notes that routes through Montana and North Dakota now have far more charging stations than in prior years, and that payment/connectivity issues that plagued networks in the past are being resolved.
