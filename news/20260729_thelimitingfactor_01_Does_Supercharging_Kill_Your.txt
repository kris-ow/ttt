The first thing to note about the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Geotab data is that it s fleet data. That is, it s&nbsp;
collected from vehicles that are used for business&nbsp;&nbsp;
operations rather than personal vehicles.
Why does that matter? Because as I showed in my&nbsp;&nbsp;
last video on EV charging, around half of the&nbsp;
degradation that occurs in an EV battery pack&nbsp;&nbsp;
is simply due to the passage of time, or calendar&nbsp;
life, rather than being used, or cycle life.
&nbsp;
On average, most personal EVs are driven 11-13&nbsp;
thousand miles per year, whereas most fleet EVs&nbsp;&nbsp;
are driven 20-30 thousand miles per year or&nbsp;
more. If that holds true for Geotab s fleet data,&nbsp;&nbsp;
their vehicles would show more degradation per&nbsp;
year due to the extra charge and discharge cycles,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which would exaggerate the impact of&nbsp;
supercharging compared to a personal vehicle.
&nbsp;
The second thing worth noting about the Geotab&nbsp;
data is that it includes three classes of EV.
&nbsp;
Multi-purpose vehicles which are light&nbsp;
vans, passenger vehicles, and trucks.
&nbsp;
Notably, many of the multi-purpose vehicles have&nbsp;
terrible degradation rates compared to passenger&nbsp;&nbsp;
vehicles which is what most individuals typically&nbsp;
drive. Just by eliminating those vehicles,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it would significantly improve the 2.3% per year&nbsp;
average degradation rate to maybe around 2%.&nbsp;&nbsp;
If we filtered the data even further to&nbsp;
just Tesla vehicles, it would likely show&nbsp;&nbsp;
even lower average degradation rates, but&nbsp;
the data is unfortunately anonymized.
&nbsp;
With that said, interestingly, later in the&nbsp;
report, Geotab does mention Tesla above a graph&nbsp;&nbsp;
that shows how much more frequent DC fast charging&nbsp;
is becoming in their fleet data. They state, The&nbsp;&nbsp;
fluctuations seen in 2020 were largely driven by&nbsp;
the high concentration of Tesla models and their&nbsp;&nbsp;
early adoption of the high-power Supercharger&nbsp;
network. Since then, a broader range of vehicle&nbsp;&nbsp;
models has entered the market with the capability&nbsp;
to accept higher charging power rates.
&nbsp;
Why does that matter? Besides the graph&nbsp;
showing the increase in DC fast charger usage,&nbsp;&nbsp;
just below it was another graph showing that&nbsp;
average peak charging power also increased&nbsp;&nbsp;
between 2020 and 2025. Between 2020 and 2025 the&nbsp;
vast majority of Tesla s chargers were 250 kW&nbsp;&nbsp;
and remained unchanged during that time period.&nbsp;
And, in another part of the Geotab report,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they state that degradation rates improved&nbsp;
between 2020 and 2023, from 2.3% to 1.8%,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and have now reverted to 2.3%.
What all this indicates to me&nbsp;&nbsp;
is that it s the non-Tesla vehicles and more&nbsp;
aggressive usage patterns that are driving&nbsp;&nbsp;
the higher degradation rates in Geotab s fleet&nbsp;
data. And Geotab comes to a similar conclusion,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but with more generic wording about high power&nbsp;
charging in newer EV models and degradation&nbsp;&nbsp;
rates varying significantly by model.
The third thing worth noting about the Geotab&nbsp;&nbsp;
report, which is the main thrust of the report,&nbsp;
is that high-power DC fast charging above 100kW&nbsp;&nbsp;
is the single largest stressor, leading to&nbsp;
degradation rates of up to twice that of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
less frequent, lower power charging group (3.0%&nbsp;
vs 1.5% per year). Let s take a closer look.
&nbsp;
I m not sure why a 100 kilowatt peak&nbsp;
charge rate was chosen as the cut-off,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but in my view, it wasn t the best way to&nbsp;
parse the data. That s for two reasons:
&nbsp;
First, a 100kW charge rate for an 80kWh battery in&nbsp;
a Model 3, for example, would have a drastically&nbsp;&nbsp;
different stress load than a 100 kW charge&nbsp;
rate for a 120 kWh battery in a Cybertruck.
&nbsp;
A better measure may have been to use c-rate,&nbsp;
which would be the charging power divided by the&nbsp;&nbsp;
battery pack size. For the Model 3 vs Cybertruck,&nbsp;
that would be 1.25c vs .83c, a 50% difference in&nbsp;&nbsp;
stress for the same 100kW charge rate. By picking&nbsp;
a c-rate, such as 1c, that would have eliminated&nbsp;&nbsp;
any bias in the data for or against larger&nbsp;
and smaller battery pack sizes by using an&nbsp;&nbsp;
equivalent charge rate for each battery pack.
Second, EV makers design the charging curve&nbsp;&nbsp;
of their vehicles to balance charging speed&nbsp;
and longevity. The reason you see different&nbsp;&nbsp;
charge rates at different states of charge&nbsp;
is because the battery cells are capable of&nbsp;&nbsp;
absorbing more power at lower states of charge&nbsp;
and less power at higher states of charge.&nbsp;&nbsp;
That s primarily because at the molecular level,&nbsp;
the anode is filling up with lithium and there&nbsp;&nbsp;
are fewer available slots for additional lithium&nbsp;
to slide into, creating a kind of traffic jam.
&nbsp;
So, my understanding is that whether you charge&nbsp;
at 250kW at a 5% state of charge or 50 kW at an&nbsp;&nbsp;
80% state of charge, you re placing roughly&nbsp;
equal stress on the battery for each unit of&nbsp;&nbsp;
energy transferred because it s still charging&nbsp;
along the same speed versus longevity slope.&nbsp;&nbsp;
I could be wrong, but there were no notes&nbsp;
made in Geotab s report to address that.
&nbsp;
In order to tease that out, Geotab could pull&nbsp;
data for one vehicle model and trim at a time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and compare how they perform at different states&nbsp;
of charge and at different charging power levels.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Instead, the report looks at a&nbsp;
bucket of data for nearly two dozen&nbsp;&nbsp;
different vehicle types and usage patterns.
On that note, besides the 100kW charging power&nbsp;&nbsp;
threshold, the Geotab report also divided the&nbsp;
fleet data by how often DC fast charging was used,&nbsp;&nbsp;
regardless of charging power. The&nbsp;
cut off was 12% of charging sessions,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which for the average personal vehicle driven&nbsp;
12,000 miles per year or 250 miles per week would&nbsp;&nbsp;
mean fast charging about once every two months.
That s a good cut-off for a low usage cohort,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but the leap from 12% of sessions to the remaining&nbsp;
88% of potential sessions creates a discontinuity.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In other words, it s comparing the best and the&nbsp;
worst cohorts, with no moderate usage cohort.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For example, if the less than 12% cohort averages&nbsp;
6% of sessions and the remaining 88% averages 30%&nbsp;&nbsp;
of sessions - which is quite possible based&nbsp;
on the overall average of 25% - that would&nbsp;&nbsp;
mean a 5x difference between the low and&nbsp;
high usage cohorts. That s quite a spread,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and a moderate usage cohort would tell us&nbsp;
more about the degradation vs usage slope.
&nbsp;
In summary, let s review the three key points&nbsp;
from the Geotab data, and its implications.
&nbsp;
First, the simple fact that it s fleet data&nbsp;
may have exaggerated the impacts of charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
compared to personal use. That s because&nbsp;
fleet vehicles are driven more miles per year,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which means more charge and discharge cycles,&nbsp;
resulting in proportionally higher degradation&nbsp;&nbsp;
due to usage rather than calendar aging.
Second, nearly two dozen vehicles are included&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the data, which includes vehicles with wildly&nbsp;
differing degradation rates. Newer multi-purpose&nbsp;&nbsp;
vehicles are increasing the average degradation&nbsp;
rate, which means the data isn t representative&nbsp;&nbsp;
for Tesla vehicles, which are typically&nbsp;
among the best in terms of degradation.
&nbsp;
Third, the selection criteria used to divide the&nbsp;
different fast charging cohorts was imprecise&nbsp;&nbsp;
and discontinuous. That is, fast charging power&nbsp;
in kilowatts isn t a reliable indicator of how&nbsp;&nbsp;
hard the battery is being pushed. And in terms of&nbsp;
fast charging frequency, it was comparing the most&nbsp;&nbsp;
stressful use cases to the least stressful,&nbsp;
which likely exaggerated the results.
&nbsp;
To be clear, Geotab provided very&nbsp;
useful information in this report,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it s important to understand the context&nbsp;
and the audience it was designed for,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is very different from the context and&nbsp;
audience that would be watching this video.
&nbsp;
Geotab is correct that across all vehicles and&nbsp;
all use cases, that fast charging does accelerate&nbsp;&nbsp;
degradation. That s important if you re managing a&nbsp;
fleet of vehicles. But if your EV is for personal&nbsp;&nbsp;
use, with average usage patterns, and if you&nbsp;
select a good quality passenger vehicle, it all&nbsp;&nbsp;
but eliminates the effect of supercharging.
That s why in the last charging video I&nbsp;&nbsp;
stressed the importance of selecting&nbsp;
a vehicle with a good track record,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and showed this graph from Recurrent. Recurrent&nbsp;
aggregates charging data from Tesla owners,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and that includes not only individual owners, but&nbsp;
also company owned vehicles. As you can see, there&nbsp;&nbsp;
s a small but negligible impact from supercharging&nbsp;
during the first three years of ownership.
&nbsp;
Here s their data in more detail and over a time&nbsp;
period of about 3 times longer, around 8 years,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but rather than using state of charge, the graph&nbsp;
instead compares the ratio of real world range&nbsp;&nbsp;
to EPA range. The infrequent fast chargers in&nbsp;
blue fast charged less than 30% of the time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while the frequent fast chargers in orange&nbsp;
fast charged more than 70% of the time.
&nbsp;
The area in orange for frequent fast charging&nbsp;
has much greater variability because it only&nbsp;&nbsp;
contained 344 out of the 13,000 total vehicles,&nbsp;
less than 3%, so the data is less smooth. But,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the overall degradation is similar because&nbsp;
the blue and orange areas mostly overlap.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The orange area for frequent fast&nbsp;
charging tends to drop slightly lower,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it s a fraction of the total variability.
How is Tesla able to achieve such similar&nbsp;&nbsp;
degradation despite massively different&nbsp;
supercharger usage? In short, good data,&nbsp;&nbsp;
good thermal management, high quality&nbsp;
materials, and probably most importantly,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they don t design the charging curve of their&nbsp;
vehicles to achieve blistering charging speeds&nbsp;&nbsp;
and instead focus on longevity. That is, if&nbsp;
Tesla wanted to, they could provide higher&nbsp;&nbsp;
charging speeds tomorrow with a software update,&nbsp;
but longevity would suffer, so they don t.
&nbsp;
That s not to say EV and battery makers can&nbsp;
t increase charging speed without additional&nbsp;&nbsp;
degradation, they can. But, caveat emptor. If&nbsp;
an EV maker claims amazing charging speeds and&nbsp;&nbsp;
longevity is important to you, it s worth&nbsp;
waiting to purchase their vehicle until the&nbsp;&nbsp;
data starts coming in. That s the only way to know&nbsp;
how much of the charging speed increase was from a&nbsp;&nbsp;
genuine technology improvement or by tweaking the&nbsp;
charging curve to favor power over longevity.
&nbsp;
And, that s one of the main points of the Geotab&nbsp;
report. Newer EVs are coming out with higher&nbsp;&nbsp;
charging rates, and their customers are using&nbsp;
fast charging more. As a result, degradation&nbsp;&nbsp;
rates are increasing. That may be exaggerated by&nbsp;
the potential fleet effect with the Geotab data,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so it may be the canary in the coal mine.

As a final note, some people will likely point&nbsp;&nbsp;
out that Tesla s charging advice states that you&nbsp;
should charge your vehicle at home when possible,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and try to use superchargers just for&nbsp;
road trips and long drives. In my view,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Tesla should just leave that advice out of&nbsp;
the owner s manual. That s for two reasons:
&nbsp;
First, while it is true that if most of your&nbsp;
charging is done at a supercharger, it can&nbsp;&nbsp;
reduce cycle for some people, but people generally&nbsp;
supercharge because they must supercharge. That s&nbsp;&nbsp;
because they re either on a road trip or they don&nbsp;
t have a supercharger at their home or apartment.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Outside of those two situations, people don t&nbsp;
typically supercharge because it s expensive&nbsp;&nbsp;
and inconvenient compared to charging at&nbsp;
home. That is, what s the cohort of people&nbsp;&nbsp;
that don t have to supercharge but are opting&nbsp;
to anyways? I imagine it s nearly non-existent.
&nbsp;
Second, it s often the people that drive the&nbsp;
most that use superchargers the most, which&nbsp;&nbsp;
counterintuitively may mean they end up getting&nbsp;
more miles out of their vehicle than a typical&nbsp;&nbsp;
EV owner. That s because driving more miles per&nbsp;
year means they ll be experiencing less calendar&nbsp;&nbsp;
life degradation and replacing it with cycle&nbsp;
life degradation instead. In other words, they&nbsp;&nbsp;
may be just trading one type of degradation for&nbsp;
another and it s not an actual loss in utility.
&nbsp;
To tease that out, it would be great to see Geotab&nbsp;
provide data not just for degradation per year,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but also degradation per 10,000 miles for&nbsp;
different vehicle types and use cases.
&nbsp;
Either way, if my assumptions are correct,&nbsp;
the supercharger advice in the Tesla owner s&nbsp;&nbsp;
manual may be creating some undue anxiety without&nbsp;
providing any tangible benefit to customers.
