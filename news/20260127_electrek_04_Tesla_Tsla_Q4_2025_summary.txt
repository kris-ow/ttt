Electrek previews Tesla's Q4 and full-year 2025 earnings (to be released Jan. 28), highlighting declining deliveries and earnings for a second consecutive year despite growth in energy storage, and previews top shareholder questions ahead of the call.

## Financials
1. **Revenue and EPS estimates point to second straight annual decline:** Wall Street consensus for Q4 2025 revenue is $24.766 billion; for the full year 2025, analysts expect revenue of around $95 billion, down about 3% from 2024. Wall Street consensus EPS for Q4 2025 is $0.45, which would be a 40% drop from $0.73 per share in Q4 2024; full-year 2025 EPS is expected at $1.63, down 33% from 2024.

## Electric Vehicles
2. **Deliveries fell 16% YoY in Q4, 8.6% for the year:** Tesla's Q4 2025 deliveries were down 16% year-over-year. For full-year 2025, Tesla delivered 1.636 million vehicles, down 8.6% from 2024 — the second consecutive year of declining deliveries, a sharp contrast to earlier company ambitions of selling closer to 5 million cars this year.

## Energy
3. **Energy storage deployment hit a record, the one bright spot:** Tesla deployed a record 14.2 GWh of energy storage in Q4 2025. For the full year, it deployed 46.7 GWh, up 49% year-over-year, described as the one consistent bright spot in Tesla's business.

## Robotaxi / Autonomous Driving / Humanoid Bots
4. **Top shareholder questions touch robotaxi, FSD, Optimus, and new models:** The most upvoted shareholder question (via Say) asked whether long-term Tesla shareholders would be prioritized in a rumored SpaceX IPO; other top questions asked when FSD will be fully unsupervised, what's bottlenecking robotaxi/FSD deployment, how many Optimus units are deployed in Tesla factories and what tasks they perform, and whether new vehicle models are planned to expand Tesla's addressable market. The article notes Musk previously said Tesla would have between 5,000 and 10,000 Optimus robots in 2025, and states Electrek has reported the actual number is nowhere near that.

## Editorial Stance
The author argues Tesla's automotive business is in genuine decline while the broader EV market and competitors like BYD grow, that Musk's "AI/robotics company" framing is a years-long pattern of unmet promises (dating to 2016), and that Tesla's limited robotaxi deployment in Austin reflects safety/liability caution rather than technological leadership. The author is skeptical that Musk's answers on FSD timelines, robotaxi bottlenecks, and new vehicle segments will contain substantive new information.
