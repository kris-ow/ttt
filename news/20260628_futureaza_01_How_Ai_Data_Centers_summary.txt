This video features FutureAzA host Brian and guest Herbert from Brighter discussing the accelerating demand for Tesla's Megapack energy storage systems, driven primarily by AI data center buildout and a wave of large commercial and utility-scale deals. The core thesis is that Megapack is far more than a commodity battery product — it is a hardware-plus-AI-software platform (via Tesla's Autobidder system) that is becoming essential infrastructure for any serious AI data center operation. The hosts argue Wall Street has been slow to appreciate the scale of this opportunity and may still be underestimating it.

## Energy

1. **Santa Barbara City College microgrid as a demand signal:** Santa Barbara City College is using a $4.2 million grant to build a new microgrid comprising 1.6 megawatts of solar and two Megapacks. The hosts treat this as a meaningful signal that distributed Megapack deployments are expanding well beyond utilities into campuses, businesses, and commercial operators — with Target, Walmart, and PepsiCo (which has purchased Tesla Semis) cited as the kinds of companies expected to follow.

2. **Tesla's largest U.S. energy storage facility:** Tesla's 2.3 gigawatt-hour energy storage facility in Maricopa County, Arizona — a $560 million project comprising 594 Megapacks — was highlighted as having been, at the time of its opening (announced in January 2025 per the hosts), the largest Megapack installation in the world. The hosts note this record has since been surpassed.

3. **$5 billion Megapack deal with Nat Power in Europe:** Tesla has signed an agreement with independent energy firm Nat Power to supply more than 25 gigawatt-hours of Megapack battery storage systems across Italy and the UK, in a deal valued at up to $5 billion. The hosts describe this as Tesla's largest reported European deal to date, and the first multi-region, multi-country, multi-project deal of its kind in Europe. Nat Power has indicated intent to 4x the scale of the project over its 20-year operating period, which the hosts suggest could grow the deal's value to $15 billion. Critically, the deal includes Tesla's Autobidder energy trading and optimization software alongside the hardware.

4. **Autobidder as a key competitive moat:** Herbert explains that Tesla's Autobidder is an AI-driven system that autonomously decides when to buy and sell energy on the customer's behalf, optimizing charge and discharge cycles based on real-time pricing. The hosts argue this transforms Megapack from a commodity battery into a differentiated platform. They draw an analogy to fuel-efficient aircraft: a cheaper but less intelligent competitor may have higher total cost of ownership over time, making the upfront price comparison misleading. Herbert describes Autobidder as industry-leading based on his research.

5. **Megapod concept as a distributed AI infrastructure play:** Herbert introduces the concept of "Megapods" — a vision in which Tesla Supercharger locations are paired with Megapacks, solar, and distributed AI data center compute (chips). The hosts see this as an emerging use case beyond vehicle charging, with Brian noting that Tesla's Megapod announcement referenced "millions" of units as the scale target, though deployment would be limited to sites where Tesla owns or controls the land.

6. **Manufacturing capacity and factory expansion:** The hosts cite Giga Nevada's annual Megapack production capacity at 40 gigawatt-hours and Giga Shanghai's at 40 gigawatt-hours. They note that Tesla's Houston facility is at 50 gigawatt-hours and is moving to Version 3 Mega Blocks. Herbert adds that the 40 gigawatt-hour figure for existing factories may not be their maximum capacity — Brian credits Herbert with first reporting that capacity could potentially triple — and both expect additional Megapack factories to be announced.

7. **AI data centers as the structural demand driver:** Brian describes driving through "data center row" in Hillsboro, Oregon and observing Megapacks installed at multiple facilities. Herbert argues that every AI data center structurally requires battery storage because AI compute workloads create volatile, spiky electricity demand that must be smoothed, and because precision computing equipment requires stable, clean power. Herbert references a recent report that a Meta data center purchased Megapacks, and notes that xAI has also purchased them — framing these as proof-of-concept for a universal AI data center requirement.

## Financials

1. **Over $500 million in Megapack and vehicle revenue from other Musk companies:** Tesla's financials included over half a billion dollars in revenue sourced from other Elon Musk-affiliated companies. This comprised Megapack deployments to xAI, $78 million in additional revenue from xAI (described as a separate line item), and $143 million from Cybertruck purchases by SpaceX and xAI. Analysts flagged non-recurring tariff and warranty adjustments that boosted margins by $480 million in the same reporting period. The hosts argue the Megapack revenue to xAI will be recurring, while Cybertruck fleet purchases are unlikely to repeat at the same scale.

## Market & Competition

1. **Competitive landscape: limited serious alternatives to Megapack:** Brian asserts that in the U.S. market, Chinese battery energy storage solutions are not a practical option, and that LG is the only other serious competitor — but characterizes LG's track record on safety as problematic (referencing fires). Herbert frames the competitive dynamic as straightforward: any well-resourced AI company racing toward AGI will buy the best-available energy storage, and if Megapack is the best, that is what they will buy regardless of corporate relationships.
