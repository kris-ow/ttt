This video is an interview on the channel FutureAzA with Preksha Shrestha, CEO of Gadi Charge, a Nepalese EV charging network. The conversation covers the company's rapid buildout of charging infrastructure across Nepal, its partnership with Huawei for liquid-cooled charging hardware, pricing, financing, and competitive strategy — none of which involves Tesla or SpaceX.

## Market & Competition
1. **Nepalese EV charging startup scales rapidly:** Gadi Charge, founded about a year prior with operations beginning roughly 15 months before this interview, has grown to over 90 locations across Nepal with more than 250 charging points (the CEO later cites approximately 252 points), up from 85 locations as of around June 2025 per the host. The company partnered with Huawei, which supplied liquid-cooling charger technology, scaling a station design from an initial 30 kW concept to a half-megawatt, 10-connector hub within 3–4 months.
2. **Nepal's EV adoption and market shift:** The CEO states Nepal ranked second in the world (behind Norway) for EV adoption in the prior year, with three out of four imported vehicles being electric. The connector mix shifted from roughly 60% GBT (Chinese standard) / 40% private cars to a 50/50 split, partly attributed to a government-declared two-day weekend increasing private EV travel, driving demand for more CCS2 chargers.
3. **Pricing and financing model:** Average charging price is cited as about 15 rupees (roughly 15 US cents) versus 10–12 rupees for home charging; the Nepalese government eliminated tariffs on home charging while giving commercial operators a separate, time-of-day-differentiated tariff. The company's expansion was aided by viability-gap funding from British and German aid programs, which the CEO says reduced investment risk and shortened payback periods for operators and franchisees.
4. **Technology differentiation:** The company highlights a Huawei-based "power distribution model" charger that can combine multiple power units (e.g., 180 kW and 80 kW) to deliver up to roughly 160 kW through a single connector, with software-based switching between GBT and CCS2 connector types without full hardware replacement — framed as protection against technology obsolescence. A proprietary charger management system (using the OCPP protocol) and consumer app provide real-time transparency on energy delivered and revenue, which the CEO says reduced theft/skimming by station staff.
