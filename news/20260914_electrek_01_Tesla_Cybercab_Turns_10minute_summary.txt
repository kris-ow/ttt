The article reports on a Reddit post describing a Tesla Cybercab robotaxi ride in Austin that took 70 minutes for a trip that would normally take 10 minutes by highway, illustrating a persistent limitation: Tesla's robotaxi software avoids highways and, per riders in the thread, at-grade railroad crossings.

## Robotaxi
1. **A 10-minute trip became a 70-minute detour:** A rider booked a Cybercab from 99 Ranch Market to the Domain in north Austin, normally about a 10-minute highway trip; instead the car went far south through East Austin, taking 70 minutes and costing $20 (roughly market rate). Tesla's app shows a price estimate at booking but no time estimate, so riders don't learn about long routes until underway.
2. **Likely cause — no highways, no at-grade rail crossings:** Riders in the thread, and the original poster, concluded the detour happened because Tesla's robotaxi avoids highways (e.g., MoPac, US-183) and at-grade railroad crossings; the car reportedly ran parallel to Capital Metro's Red Line until it could cross via a bridge in East Austin.
3. **Applies fleet-wide:** The article states Tesla runs the same surface-street-only software on its Model Y robotaxis and the Cybercab, so any rider in Austin could encounter a similar long detour, not just Cybercab riders.
4. **Context — Cybercab's rocky debut:** The Cybercab began carrying public riders in Austin on September 3; the article notes NHTSA opened an audit the same day into Tesla's self-certification of the steering-wheel-and-pedal-free two-seater, and that days earlier one of Tesla's Austin robotaxis reportedly drove through a set of bollards.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Comparison to Waymo and crash-rate claim:** The article contrasts Tesla's surface-streets-only, vision-based system with Waymo, which it says already operates fully driverless rides on freeways. It repeats Electrek's prior claim that Tesla's fleet crashes roughly once every 57,000 miles, about four times worse than an average human driver, framing the long/odd routes as evidence of a safety-driven limitation.

## Editorial Stance
Electrek's Fred Lambert argues the "held hostage" framing is unfair but the incident is a real data point showing Tesla's robotaxi is far from the "goes anywhere a human can, cheaper" product Musk has promoted; he calls the current service "not ready for primetime" while noting Tesla has a pattern of releasing half-baked features, and says this reflects the honest current state of the technology rather than a failure of the engineers.
