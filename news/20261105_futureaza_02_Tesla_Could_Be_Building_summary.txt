This FutureAzA discussion features the host and a guest ("Dr. Know-It-All") analyzing Tesla's Optimus humanoid robot program, arguing that small-scale production is likely already happening quietly at Fremont ahead of the Texas factory's scale-up, while flagging durability, hand/tendon design, and training-data bottlenecks as the real open questions rather than production timing itself.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Fremont converted Model S/X line used as Optimus pilot line:** The guest states Tesla converted a former Model S and Model X production line in Fremont into an Optimus pilot line, and believes Optimus units are already coming off that line now, even though the public doesn't see them — giving Tesla "a path to a reasonable amount per week" in Fremont before the Texas plant comes online.
2. **Texas plant is for scale-up, not initial production:** Both speakers argue the Texas robot factory, still under construction, is designed to scale up Optimus manufacturing rather than host the first production units. The host says construction-pace observation suggests actual production equipment likely won't even be installed until around the middle of next year, and that meaningful output is further out still; he expects significant design changes between the "1st and 1.1" hardware generations.
3. **Hands and tendons remain the hardest unsolved problem:** The guest says sources ("my birds") indicate hands are the main hardware bottleneck, partly because Tesla couldn't use off-the-shelf actuators due to precision and durability needs. The last public look at Tesla's Optimus hand was over two years ago (shown October 10) and used a tendon-based design; it's unknown whether Tesla has kept or abandoned that approach — notably, Figure Robotics' Brett Adcock reportedly concluded tendons were a bad idea, and it's unclear if Tesla reached the same view.
4. **Durability/actuator wear is a bigger constraint than people realize:** The guest contrasts continuous, steady-speed motors (like a vehicle motor or refrigerator compressor) with humanoid actuators, which undergo constant start-stop, torque-reversal motion that's far more damaging to component life — suggesting Optimus longevity is still an open engineering question Tesla is actively working through.
5. **Training-data ("brain") readiness is reportedly behind schedule:** The guest says his sources indicate Optimus's neural network training is lagging — not a compute shortage but a lack of properly trained behavior — which is why Tesla is reportedly paying people to wear gloves and head-mounted cameras to generate motion-capture training data (unconfirmed by the guest directly; described as coming from unnamed sources). This is framed as a fundamentally harder data problem than Tesla's vehicle fleet, since drivers already had to drive anyway, whereas data-suit wearers must be paid to generate equivalent humanoid training data.
6. **Debate over whether Tesla is rushing to scale prematurely:** The host raises Elon Musk's own production philosophy — that optimization should be the last of five steps, not moved up early — and both speakers debate whether Tesla may be pushing toward mass production of Optimus too soon, which could be either a smart way to surface real-world problems at volume or a costly mistake if done before the design is mature.
7. **First production units likely stay internal:** The speakers agree the first roughly 10,000–20,000 Optimus units (described as an apparent target, not confirmed guidance) will likely be used internally by Tesla rather than sold externally, both to work out early-production issues and to avoid revealing trade-secret details of the robot or its capabilities to outside customers.
