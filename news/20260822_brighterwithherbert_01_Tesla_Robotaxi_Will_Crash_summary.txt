This episode features CFA and investment advisor Cern Basher walking through his analysis of the liability economics behind Tesla's Robotaxi scale-up, arguing that scaling a system safer than human drivers necessarily produces more absolute accidents even as the rate improves — but that liability cost per mile falls low enough to remain a manageable line item rather than an existential threat. The conversation covers safety math, fleet-wide learning effects, current mile/fleet data, and the "customer experience" gap that remains alongside safety.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Scale multiplies both crashes and safety gains:** Basher's framework shows that even a Full Self-Driving fleet that is 5x, 10x, or 20x safer than human drivers will still produce a rising absolute number of deaths as miles driven increase (on a log scale chart), since roughly 40,000 people die annually on US roads today across about 3.3 trillion miles driven; at a hypothetical trillion miles, a system 10x safer than humans would still produce around 1,190 deaths versus roughly 10,000 for human drivers over the same miles.
2. **FSD's "avoidance" ability may be more important than raw driving skill:** Both hosts emphasize that FSD's superhuman reaction times — avoiding deer, kids, out-of-control spinning cars, and red-light runners — may be an underappreciated driver of its safety edge over humans, since human reaction time and 360-degree awareness are inherently limited.
3. **Nature of accidents will differ from human-caused crashes:** Basher argues FSD-caused accidents won't stem from drunk driving, distraction, or fatigue (which the hosts note account for large shares of human crashes, citing 30% for drowsy/distracted driving) but instead from software edge cases, meaning municipalities may need to improve road markings/signage to help autonomous systems.
4. **Fleet-wide learning is a key advantage over individual human drivers:** Because a software fix can be pushed to the entire fleet overnight once an issue is identified, one bad algorithm becomes a fleet-wide problem but also a fleet-wide (and fast) fix — unlike human drivers, who don't collectively learn from each other's mistakes.
5. **Data volume distinctions matter for proving safety:** As of the last Tesla earnings call in late July, unsupervised/autonomous paid miles stood at 380,000, growing at a stated double-digit rate; Basher estimates the total may be around 650,000–700,000 currently. Supervised FSD miles have reached about 13.6 billion, which Basher says still provides valuable indirect safety validation even though it isn't the same as fully autonomous data. He notes that with a US national human fatality rate requiring roughly 2.5 million miles to expect about 0.3 fatalities, Tesla needs a much larger sample of autonomous miles to statistically prove safety claims.
6. **Public reporting standards need refinement:** Basher argues that many reported robotaxi "crashes" are minor bumps and scrapes (comparable to a shopping-cart ding) rather than genuine collisions, and calls for Tesla to eventually publish a clearer metric — "Tesla at-fault collisions per million miles" — for real transparency, though he says it's too early to press for that now. He also notes that, per Austin/Texas public incident data reviewed by others, most robotaxi-involved incidents are caused by other drivers, not the robotaxi.
7. **Experience reliability is distinct from safety reliability:** The hosts distinguish physical safety from "experience" issues — citing a report that a robotaxi in Austin sat stopped at an intersection for eight minutes while other drivers honked, and past instances of vehicles struggling with precise pickup/drop-off — arguing these customer-experience failures, not safety, may currently be the bigger adoption risk.

## Robotaxi
1. **Liability cost-per-mile is the metric that matters, not crash headlines:** Basher's central financial argument is that at scale (e.g., 100 billion annual miles), a liability cost of 3 cents per mile would total $3 billion a year — manageable if the network earns revenue in the tens of billions — while 7 cents per mile would be $7 billion; either way it remains "well within the operating capability" of the business given ride revenue per mile.
2. **Liability functions like insurance reserving, not open-ended risk:** Tesla can build statistical models and reserve for expected liability the way airlines, banks, and insurers already do for their own predictable risks (maintenance failures, loan defaults, claims), converting robotaxi liability into a plannable operating expense rather than unbounded exposure.
3. **A software defect is a different risk category than a bad human driver:** Because the same software issue can exist simultaneously across the entire fleet, Basher warns a single flawed behavior (e.g., unexpected braking at crosswalks after an FSD update) could cause many incidents at once — a scale of exposure with no equivalent among individual human drivers, even though it's also fixable fleet-wide via update.
4. **Tesla's real-time vehicle data could streamline claims and reduce disputes:** Because Tesla vehicles have cameras and log steering, braking, and acceleration data in real time, Basher expects faster, more evidence-based accident claims resolution, potentially reducing lawsuits; he also floats the idea that Tesla could partner with insurers (sharing data while an insurer underwrites and holds capital) rather than self-insuring entirely — drawing a comparison to Lemonade, which he says already offers roughly 50% lower premiums to FSD users in about five states based on safety data.
5. **Lower repair costs from vision-only hardware:** Basher contrasts Tesla's low-cost, easily replaceable camera-based sensors (cited around $25 to replace) against Waymo's more expensive, exposed lidar/radar hardware, arguing this keeps Tesla's cents-per-mile damage costs lower.
6. **Deliberate slow rollout may be a data-accumulation strategy:** The hosts speculate Tesla's cautious, geofenced rollout pace (rather than a large immediate fleet expansion) reflects a strategy of accumulating enough driving data to defend safety statistics before an inevitable high-profile accident occurs, even though this frustrates investors and the public who see FSD/robotaxi performing well already in personal use.
7. **"Strange endgame" — society may judge robotaxi deaths against a counterfactual, not zero:** Basher raises that even if a robotaxi network is associated with headline-grabbing death tolls, the relevant comparison is deaths that would have occurred with human drivers over the same miles; he suggests the real question is what number of lives saved (he floats a hypothetical of up to 20,000 lives/year if the fatality rate were halved) society and regulators will accept as sufficient before treating the technology as proven safe — a political and public-acceptance question, not just a statistical one.
