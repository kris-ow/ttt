NHTSA issued a formal Special Order on September 10 demanding, under oath, that Tesla justify how the Cybercab — which lacks a steering wheel, pedals, and mirrors — complies with federal safety standards, escalating from the Audit Query opened days after Tesla began commercial Cybercab service in Austin on September 3.

## Autonomous Driving / Robotaxi
1. **Special Order details and deadline:** The 21-request order, signed by NHTSA Chief Counsel Peter Simshauser and addressed to Tesla's head of litigation and associate general counsel for regulatory affairs, gives Tesla until September 30 to respond; failing to answer fully and truthfully carries penalties of up to $139 million, and falsifying or withholding information can bring criminal penalties including a fine or up to 15 years in prison. The response must be signed under oath by a responsible officer.

2. **Self-certification challenge:** Under the US system, automakers self-certify compliance with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS) rather than seeking pre-approval; Tesla certified the Cybercab as compliant with all applicable FMVSS, but NHTSA's order questions whether that certification can be defended given most standards assume a human driver. Request 10 asks Tesla to specify which standards it believes don't apply to a driverless car; NHTSA states that "inapplicable" is not something a manufacturer can determine unilaterally.

3. **Foot-brake requirement (FMVSS 135):** FMVSS No. 135 requires service brakes to be activated by a foot control, which the Cybercab lacks. NHTSA notes it has publicly stated a manufacturer without a foot-activated brake could not certify to this standard, and asks Tesla to explain how it did so. NHTSA proposed amending this rule for automated vehicles in June, but the article notes a proposal is not yet law.

4. **"Make inoperative" concern:** NHTSA's requests probe whether the Cybercab can be driven by a human using temporary attached controls (which the article states Tesla uses) and whether Tesla relied on those temporary controls to certify compliance before removing them — potentially violating the Safety Act's prohibition on certifying a vehicle with required safety equipment installed and then removing it before delivery. Additional standards cited as at issue include FMVSS 101 (controls/telltales), 102 (shift-position display), 108 (turn signals), 111 (mirrors), and 126 (stability-control telltales).

5. **No Part 555 exemption:** NHTSA's 2022 rule stated further FMVSS changes would likely be needed before a fully automated vehicle could be manufactured for sale absent a Part 555 exemption. Tesla does not hold such an exemption, and NHTSA is asking how the Cybercab is legal to sell without one.

6. **Comparison to Zoox:** In July, NHTSA granted Amazon's Zoox a temporary Part 555 exemption covering eight federal standards (including the foot-brake and mirror rules now at issue for Tesla), capping Zoox at 2,500 vehicles per year through 2028; Zoox obtained a demonstration exemption first, then a commercial one. Tesla instead self-certified the Cybercab as compliant and began charging for rides on September 3 without applying for an exemption.

## Editorial Stance
Author Fred Lambert argues Electrek has warned for over a year that Tesla was mass-producing a vehicle not cleared for sale or self-driving use, noting the first Cybercab rolled off the line in February before autonomy or homologation questions were resolved. He asserts NHTSA is not hostile to driverless cars generally (citing its June foot-brake proposal and pullback of AV STEP oversight) but says the agency's message is that wanting rules changed isn't the same as rules changing. He characterizes Tesla as acting as if it doesn't believe the rules will be enforced, and links this to Musk's political spending, calling the situation "unfortunately" advantageous for Tesla under the current administration — a subjective opinion, not a reported fact.
