Neuralink's latest update focuses on
something that will decide whether this
technology can actually scale up to help

surgery itself. Neuralink's system works

Those signals are then translated into

precision needed to avoid damaging brain
tissue. And this is why Neuralink built
a specialized surgical robot. Once a
surgeon prepares the skull, the robot
takes over the most delicate part of the
job. It picks up each thread from the
implant and inserts it into the brain
with incredible accuracy.
The robot doesn't just move in a
straight line, it adjusts its movements
in real time to avoid tiny blood
vessels. This isn't just about being
precise, it's about being consistent
every single time. And the robot uses
advanced cameras and sensors, including
something called optical coherence
tomography. You can think of this like a
high-tech x-ray for the robot's eyes,
allowing it to see exactly where the
blood vessels are located beneath the
surface of the brain.
The robot can then move along multiple
paths to reach different parts of the
brain. And this flexibility is vital
