The host and a robotics-focused YouTuber, Herbert, discuss what Optimus will realistically be able to do when Tesla shows it next, which the host expects in the first or second quarter. They argue that the first uses will be industrial and tied to Tesla's own operations, such as Cybercab charging and delivery, while a general-purpose home robot is years away. Their thesis is that Tesla will lag rivals on early demos but is aiming at a universal, mass-produced bot with its own supply chain.

## Humanoid Bots
1. **Industrial first, home later:** Herbert believes home robots are five years or more away. He cites cost, since a robot would have to be sold to consumers at $30,000–$50,000 or to industrial buyers paying $100,000 a year or more. He also cites the much wider task range at home and safety around children, the elderly and people in wheelchairs, where heavy steel-framed robots are risky. He says factories will pay for basic item-moving, so that is where humanoids go first. The host pushes back with an argument from the CEO of 1X that varied home tasks give broader training than narrow factory pick-and-place.
2. **Dexterity and the "Optimus Academy" data problem:** The hosts relay Elon Musk's point that hands are the hard part, because rubber-on-steel fingers slip where muscle can grip. They also relay his estimate that 10,000–20,000 humanoid robots in an "Optimus Academy" would need months of real-world training. Herbert notes that other companies claim large simulation engines or a few hundred to a thousand robots would suffice. If Musk is right, he says, many tasks will not arrive for at least six months to a year, and probably longer.
3. **What a demo might show:** The hosts expect the next showing to be of third-generation robots, produced in Fremont. They say Musk wants to prove the bot is not pre-programmed and can handle a brand-new environment. Herbert thinks Optimus could play a well-defined piano piece, though not well, and could not play the violin or flute (no lips). He doubts it could pour concrete, install cabinets with tiny nails, or do delicate battery changes with a small screwdriver. He says none of these will happen this year or next.
4. **Tesla's strategy versus rivals:** Herbert says Agility's Digit and Chinese makers such as Unitree may ship or demo first, as Waymo did in robotaxi. He describes Unitree's robots as research kits, though he notes they have started appearing in Chinese stores. He says Tesla is targeting a universal bot that can learn any task, built in volume. He cites a factory planned for 10 million units and in-house design of nearly every component, including batteries and the "brain." He says this is why Tesla takes longer.
5. **Timeline and usefulness bar:** Herbert says Musk indicated actual sales will not begin until late next year, with another three to six months after that. He expects 2027 to be about learning, with rumors of use in Tesla, SpaceX and other Musk factories. He thinks 10–30 tasks on day one would already be useful for a narrow market. He argues a useful task requires roughly 99.99% accuracy, longer working hours than a human including charging, and a low enough cost to replace labor.

## Robotaxi
1. **Optimus charging Cybercabs:** The hosts see charging robotaxis and Cybercabs as an ideal first task. They say Lars Moravy has confirmed in an interview that Optimus will plug chargers into the cars. The host recalls asking the head of the Cybercab program about wireless charging, who said there are many ways to automate charging. The host says Tesla already employs hundreds of people wearing cameras and backpacks that use the same compute as Optimus to capture training data. He says they are hired near service centers as well as factories.
2. **Cybercab delivery with an Optimus in the trunk:** The hosts say Tesla designed Cybercab with a large trunk and no rear window for night-time package delivery. They say Moravy told Jay Leno that Tesla would do deliveries. Herbert argues an Optimus riding in the car could handle the last step, taking the package from trunk to door. He frames this as a vision and navigation problem where Tesla is strongest, and an outdoor task that keeps the bot away from people. He expects it eventually but not immediately. He also expects integration with Grok and with Semi trucks, for example placing emergency stop signs.
3. **Internal use cases:** Herbert says Tesla has many internal tasks suited to robots, unlike most humanoid makers apart from Hyundai-owned Boston Dynamics. He cites building a Moon or Mars colony, where a dozen Optimus units could assemble parts ahead of human arrival.
