Electrek editor Fred Lambert — who has tested every version of Tesla Autopilot and FSD since before the original launch — argues that FSD v14 is the most capable version yet, and that its near-perfect performance is creating a dangerous complacency problem that Tesla's marketing is actively worsening.

## Autonomous Driving

1. **FSD v14 performance and the complacency trap:** Lambert reports that after daily driving FSD v14 for several weeks, he now routinely travels hundreds of miles without needing to intervene. Emerging data suggests FSD v14 can travel roughly 2,000 miles between critical interventions — equating to approximately 30–40 hours of driving. He invokes the "ironies of automation" concept (documented since the 1980s) and the "vigilance decrement" effect, noting research shows drivers need 5–8 seconds to mentally reengage after an automated system requests a takeover. Lambert warns that even a 3x safety improvement over prior versions doesn't eliminate the risk of a single moment of inattention.

2. **Real-world incident and third-party warnings:** Lambert cites the crash of Raffi Krikorian — former head of Uber's self-driving division — who totaled his Tesla Model X after FSD's near-perfect performance lulled him into a false sense of security before the system failed in a turn. He also references IIHS warnings about Level 2 system design, NHTSA's expanded crash reporting requirements for Level 2 ADAS, and a Reuters report in which seven of nine former Tesla AI data labelers said they would not trust FSD to drive them.

3. **Tesla's marketing amplifying the risk:** Lambert details a pattern of Tesla's official X account and Elon Musk framing FSD as a substitute for an attentive driver. Specific examples cited: Tesla called FSD "your personal chauffeur" and said it "gives back your freedom" and "takes the stress out of driving." Tesla amplified testimonials from a 71-year-old with failing eyesight, a 93-year-old driving to church, and a man born without arms — all framing FSD as providing independent mobility for people who cannot otherwise drive. Musk replied "yes" when asked if FSD allows texting depending on traffic context, despite texting while driving being illegal in nearly all 50 states and Tesla's own terms requiring full driver supervision. Musk also posted that "by V14.3, your car will feel like it is sentient" — which Lambert characterizes as a psychological prompt to over-trust the system, not a technical claim.

## Editorial Stance
Lambert argues Tesla is actively choosing to worsen the complacency problem through its marketing rather than address it, and calls on the company to strengthen driver monitoring, stop promoting FSD as a driver substitute, and be honest about the system's real limitations. He warns that when a complacent FSD driver misses a critical intervention, third parties on public roads bear the consequences without having consented to Tesla's beta test.
