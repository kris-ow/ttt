This video argues that Tesla is winning the robotaxi "cost war" against Waymo, citing a Lars Moravy factory tour, this week's Texas fleet registration data, a Houston price comparison, Alphabet's Q3 "Other Bets" losses, and the regulatory landscape ahead of the midterm elections. The host's framing throughout: robotaxi will become a cost-per-mile business, and Tesla's vertically integrated manufacturing gives it a structural cost advantage over Waymo's reliance on third-party automakers.

## Robotaxi
1. **Lars Moravy details Cybercab production advantages on Munro Live:** In an interview, Tesla VP of Vehicle Engineering Lars Moravy said competition "comes down to cost per mile" and that better service at lower cost per mile would make Tesla "very hard to beat." He described the Cybercab assembly line as having about 25 stations versus 5-10x that for the standard Model Y line, 78% automation overall (100% on the frame line), roughly half the floor space per car, and no paint shop (color is molded into plastic panels). He said the line is running ahead of robotaxi rollout — "we don't actually launch the line every day" — and that Tesla has "a scale they can't even imagine" with a goal of building thousands of units per week.
2. **Battery and durability design for high-mileage service:** An engineer stated the Cybercab is designed for at least half a million miles of service life. Battery engineering lead Robbie Schumpf reportedly graded the Model Y battery building block a "D" (good for the customer but hard/expensive to build), the Cybertruck battery a "B-minus," and the Cybercab pack an "A-minus," with a modular design allowing single-section replacement. The drive inverter was also redesigned for top-down removal without lifting the car.
3. **Texas robotaxi registrations show Cybercab overtaking Model Y:** Tesla's Texas robotaxi registrations grew from 858 (last Thursday, ~350 Cybercab/~500 Model Y) to 1,080 by Tuesday, with all new registrations being Cybercabs; one tracked source cited 573 total Cybercab registrations and 222 added in the past week, and Robotaxi Radar said Tesla added over 470 units in October. Separately, street spotters counted roughly 1,020 Cybercab sightings around Austin in a day, with about 975 driverless (~96% driverless share, a new high).
4. **Houston and multi-city expansion with operational hiccups:** Tesla expanded its Houston service area roughly 10x this week; one rider reported smooth two-hour trip with under-15-minute waits but no highway coverage yet, while another analysis found the real working geofence about 26% smaller than the mapped area, and a separate rider had two of three trips canceled due to wrong-way navigation. Cybercabs were also spotted testing on Austin freeways, north of Dallas, in Atlanta, and in Pittsburgh, with Jacksonville listed as a future city; Tesla reportedly plans to add Cybercabs to the Dallas fleet in November and posted robotaxi charging supervisor job listings in six cities.
5. **Operating hours extended to 20 hours/day:** Tesla extended robotaxi operating hours to 6am–2am (20 hours/day) in at least one market; Tesla AI software lead Ashok Elluswamy tweeted about the extended hours ("now it's time to close the bars"), which the host frames as spreading fixed production costs over more trips.
6. **Houston price comparison favors Tesla; mixed results elsewhere:** In a same-time, same-route rush-hour comparison in Houston, a Tesla Model Y robotaxi ride cost about $18 versus almost $32 for Waymo (~43% cheaper), with comparable wait and trip times; a separate Houston comparison put Tesla at about $3/mile versus $5 for UberX. In Miami, the Model Y was reportedly cheapest and fastest among options. However, in Austin, UberX was cheaper than Tesla during both rush hours on a given day (by $2.50 in the morning, 75 cents in the evening), and Waymo reportedly won recent price comparisons in Tampa and Dallas.
7. **Accessibility milestone:** A blind rider posted a video describing his first robotaxi ride as fully accessible end-to-end, noting the car honked on arrival and Grok announced travel time via voice; a later software update reportedly added haptic feedback to help locate the car and screen-reader music control.
8. **Texas fleet comparison across operators:** As of October 29, Texas AV fleet registrations stood at Waymo 1,259, Tesla 1,080, AVRide 344, and Zoox 63 — Waymo still leads by about 180 vehicles in Texas.
9. **Cost-per-car estimates favor Tesla by a wide margin:** Using rough third-party estimates (~$100,000 per Waymo vehicle vs. ~$25,000 per Cybercab), the implied cost would be about $175 million per 1,000 Waymo vehicles versus about $25 million per 1,000 Cybercabs; these are described as estimates since neither company discloses actual vehicle costs, and Tesla's own public target is under $30,000 per Cybercab. A separate analyst estimate pegged Waymo's newest Hyundai Ioniq 5-based design at roughly 2.8x the production cost of a Cybercab.

## Autonomous Driving
1. **Tesla safety-page milestone:** Tesla's Robotaxi safety page reportedly passed 4 million driverless miles as of Friday, up from 1 million on September 3rd; the host notes this is still well under 1% of total FSD miles driven. Tesla's reported data (per its own NHTSA-based safety page) for the period late January to mid-October shows zero injuries, no airbag deployments, and no pedestrian/cyclist collisions, versus an estimated nine fatal accidents the company says human drivers would have had on the same roads over that period — this is Tesla's own comparison.
2. **1.74 million FSD subscribers disclosed:** Tesla's board letter (Thursday) stated that 1.74 million people currently pay for FSD, the software underlying the robotaxi fleet.

## Market & Competition
1. **Waymo's Q3 losses and capital raises:** Alphabet's "Other Bets" division (which includes Waymo and is not broken out separately) reported $372 million in Q3 revenue (+8% YoY) and an operating loss of about $2 billion, versus $1.4 billion a year earlier; year-to-date "Other Bets" losses total about $6 billion. Alphabet's CFO attributed losses to continued investment in Waymo and other projects. Separately, Waymo raised $16 billion in a prior private round at a $126 billion valuation (with Alphabet reportedly investing about $13 billion), and took its first-ever debt — a $5 billion loan — earlier this month, summing to roughly $21 billion in funding this year.
2. **Waymo's scale and multi-OEM sourcing strategy:** Waymo has covered 300 million fully autonomous miles, operates in 15 US cities, and as of spring was running over 500,000 paid trips per week; this week it added about 250 square miles of coverage across Los Angeles, Nashville, Orlando, San Diego, and the Bay Area. Waymo sources vehicles from Hyundai, China's Zeekr, and — per a Friday announcement — Toyota, which will build Waymo's bZ4X-based autonomous fleet at its Takaoka plant starting with testing/validation in 2027 in Japan before the US. Reports say Waymo aims to roughly double its fleet to 8,000 vehicles by year-end, many being Zeekr robotaxis assembled in China.
3. **Regulatory landscape is the key swing factor:** Friday, October 30 was the deadline for Tesla to submit sworn written responses to NHTSA's special inquiry (21 questions) on how a steering-wheel-and-pedal-free vehicle meets federal safety standards designed around human drivers; responses were not yet public as of recording. Separately, NHTSA opened a preliminary investigation on October 9 into claims of Tesla robotaxis speeding in school zones (four incidents, no injuries/accidents), with a decision expected around January 21. The bipartisan SELF DRIVE Act (Bob Latta/Debbie Dingell), which would set uniform AV rules and raise the exemption cap from 2,500 to 90,000 vehicles, narrowly passed a House subcommittee 12-11 in February with no movement since; the host is uncertain it can pass the Senate (requiring 60 votes) given a prior version died in the Senate after passing the House in 2017. New Jersey's pending robotaxi pilot bill would require backup sensors beyond cameras, which camera-only Tesla vehicles would not meet. NHTSA updated lighting regulations on Friday to add blue lights signaling driverless operation, which the host frames as a positive regulatory signal for Tesla.
