Let s start with Tesla s goals for the&nbsp;
4680 project, in order of importance.
&nbsp;
First, it was to address strategic risks. The&nbsp;
4680 project wasn t just about a new battery cell,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it involved rebuilding the entire battery supply&nbsp;
chain outside of China, which has a stranglehold&nbsp;&nbsp;
on not just battery cell production, but also&nbsp;
cell components, and materials processing. That&nbsp;&nbsp;
s a massive strategic risk for Tesla because they&nbsp;
use batteries in all of their major products.
&nbsp;
Second, the 4680 project was an opportunity&nbsp;
to cut costs. Manufacturers at each step of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the battery supply chain from lithium&nbsp;
mine to battery line take a profit,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which adds to the cost of the finished battery&nbsp;
pack. Additionally, many of those companies aren t&nbsp;&nbsp;
using the most efficient manufacturing processes.&nbsp;
By vertically integrating, it offered Tesla the&nbsp;&nbsp;
opportunity to cut battery costs by almost 60%.
Third, Tesla believed that they could produce&nbsp;&nbsp;
some of the highest performance&nbsp;
battery cells on the market.
&nbsp;
Why did I place strategic risks first,&nbsp;
cost cutting second, and performance third?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Strategic risks are first because if there was a&nbsp;
geopolitical dispute and China pulled the supply&nbsp;&nbsp;
chain rug cost and performance wouldn t matter&nbsp;
because Tesla couldn t produce any vehicles. Cost&nbsp;&nbsp;
is second because it helps move units and improve&nbsp;
profitability. Performance is third because Tesla&nbsp;&nbsp;
always has the option to purchase the highest&nbsp;
performance battery cells on the market to ensure&nbsp;&nbsp;
their products are competitive. Any better&nbsp;
than state of the art would be a bonus.
&nbsp;
So, how is Tesla progressing&nbsp;
against those goals?
&nbsp;
First, they ve built an alternative supply chain&nbsp;
for the 4680, which has eliminated the strategic&nbsp;&nbsp;
risk. Second, the 4680 is now the cheapest&nbsp;
high nickel battery cell available to them&nbsp;&nbsp;
in North America, which is saving them money but&nbsp;
probably at most 10-20% rather than 50-60%. Third,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the performance of the 4680 is comparable&nbsp;
to battery cells from Panasonic and LG Chem,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but certainly not yet the best in the industry.
That is, Tesla s achieving their goals for&nbsp;&nbsp;
the 4680 in order of priority, and it s still&nbsp;
early days for them as a cell manufacturer.
&nbsp;
With an understanding of the goals&nbsp;
of the 4680 project in place,&nbsp;&nbsp;
let s look at the reasons why people argue that&nbsp;
the 4680 cell is now or soon to be obsolete.&nbsp;&nbsp;
From what I can tell after reading dozens of&nbsp;
comments and posts on the topic, those fall into&nbsp;&nbsp;
two broad categories, performance and cost.
Starting with performance, there are a number&nbsp;&nbsp;
of companies claiming that they ll have&nbsp;
high performance solid state batteries&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the market in the next year or two that&nbsp;
will exceed the performance of the 4680 on&nbsp;&nbsp;
specs like energy density and charging speed.&nbsp;
But, does that make the 4680 obsolete? No.
&nbsp;
Tesla currently purchases around 99% of their&nbsp;
batteries from other battery makers and they ve&nbsp;&nbsp;
decided against using the fastest charging&nbsp;
and highest energy density battery cells&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the market. That s because although other&nbsp;
battery cells might perform well in charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
speed or energy density, they re deficient&nbsp;
in other areas such cost, cycle life, or aren&nbsp;&nbsp;
t yet available in large enough volumes.
The upcoming solid state battery chemistries&nbsp;&nbsp;
are no different. There s no indication that any&nbsp;
of them are cheap enough or scalable enough for&nbsp;&nbsp;
multi-million unit mass market vehicles. So,&nbsp;
I don t expect them to significantly displace&nbsp;&nbsp;
liquid electrolyte batteries like the 4680 in the&nbsp;
next decade, particularly in light of what BYD and&nbsp;&nbsp;
CATL are achieving with LFP battery chemistries.
On that note, are the CATL Shenxing battery and&nbsp;&nbsp;
BYD Blade 2.0 battery 4680 killers? No, because&nbsp;
they re too expensive to import into the United&nbsp;&nbsp;
States, which is where 4680 production is located,&nbsp;
and there are legal, political, and financial&nbsp;&nbsp;
barriers that make direct Chinese ownership of&nbsp;
U.S. factories difficult to say the least.
&nbsp;
As for Tesla vehicles produced in China, Tesla&nbsp;
already uses battery cells from CATL. So when the&nbsp;&nbsp;
time is right and if Shenxing batteries don t come&nbsp;
with any major drawbacks, Tesla will likely start&nbsp;&nbsp;
using them. As for vehicles in Europe, depending&nbsp;
on the vehicle model and trim, Tesla can use a&nbsp;&nbsp;
combination of the 4680 and whatever other battery&nbsp;
cells are available locally or for import.
&nbsp;
Let s move on to the second argument for the&nbsp;
4680 being obsolete cost competition from sodium&nbsp;&nbsp;
ion batteries and the decreases in&nbsp;
lithium ion battery prices we ve seen&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the 6 years since battery day.
First, as for sodium ion batteries,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they don t meet the minimum range requirements for&nbsp;
a Tesla vehicle. Despite their solid gravimetric&nbsp;&nbsp;
energy density, their volumetric energy density&nbsp;
is less impressive. Depending on the chemistry&nbsp;&nbsp;
that can mean EV s with sodium ion batteries&nbsp;
can expect to have around a 1/3 less range than&nbsp;&nbsp;
those with iron based lithium ion batteries.&nbsp;
That s okay for a budget vehicle, but not an&nbsp;&nbsp;
entry level luxury vehicle like Tesla sells.
But, what about sodium ion batteries for grid&nbsp;&nbsp;
storage applications where energy density is less&nbsp;
important and vehicles that don t need a lot of&nbsp;&nbsp;
range like the Cybercab? Tesla can develop their&nbsp;
own in-house sodium ion chemistry and put it in&nbsp;&nbsp;
prismatic battery cells for grid storage and&nbsp;
the 4680 for vehicles. More on that later.
&nbsp;
Next, as for the argument that the 4680 no&nbsp;
longer offers a cost advantage for Tesla&nbsp;&nbsp;
due to falling battery prices, it stems&nbsp;
from a misunderstanding of what s causing&nbsp;&nbsp;
the battery cost reductions and how Tesla&nbsp;
expects to achieve their cost reductions.
&nbsp;
Starting with Tesla s cost reductions, 14% of&nbsp;
the expected cost savings of the 4680 was from&nbsp;&nbsp;
better cell design, and the battery cells&nbsp;
used by the rest of the industry haven t&nbsp;&nbsp;
really changed much in the past 6 years. So, the&nbsp;
cell design cost reduction is fairly durable.
&nbsp;
18% of the expected cost savings was&nbsp;
from switching to the dry electrode&nbsp;&nbsp;
coating process and other improvements&nbsp;
to the way the battery is manufactured.&nbsp;&nbsp;
That means most of the cell factory cost&nbsp;
reduction is fairly durable until other companies&nbsp;&nbsp;
start using the dry electrode process.
5% of the cost savings was from adding silicon,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which Tesla hasn t done yet and could&nbsp;
be achieved by any other manufacturer,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so it isn t durable and can be crossed off.
12% of the cost savings was from manufacturing&nbsp;&nbsp;
the cathode material in-house and I m assuming&nbsp;
that also included the cost savings from Tesla&nbsp;&nbsp;
s in-house lithium production for the cathode.&nbsp;
Those cost savings were mostly from eliminating&nbsp;&nbsp;
the margins of cathode and lithium suppliers,&nbsp;
so cost the cost savings is durable.
&nbsp;
7% of the cost savings was from cell to&nbsp;
vehicle integration, which isn t durable&nbsp;&nbsp;
because other battery manufacturers have&nbsp;
done it, so it can also be crossed off.
&nbsp;
So by my estimate, up to 44% of the&nbsp;
56% of Tesla s original cost reduction&nbsp;&nbsp;
estimates are still durable and achievable.
As for what s caused the cost reductions for&nbsp;&nbsp;
the average price of lithium ion batteries,&nbsp;
much of it was due to lower materials costs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
overcapacity, intense price competition, and a&nbsp;
preference for LFP batteries, which use cheaper&nbsp;&nbsp;
iron-based cathode material that Tesla can and&nbsp;
will likely also use in their batteries.
&nbsp;
That is much of the cost reductions in lithium&nbsp;
ion batteries over the past 6 years have been&nbsp;&nbsp;
a result of market conditions rather than&nbsp;
fundamental manufacturing improvements. Yes,&nbsp;&nbsp;
there will have been fundamental cost reductions&nbsp;
to due Wright s Law scaling affects as well,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but some of those will also apply to Tesla, such&nbsp;
as cheaper cell components like electrode foils,&nbsp;&nbsp;
separators, and electrolyte.
I d be surprised if more than a third&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the cost reduction in lithium ion batteries was&nbsp;
due to fundamental manufacturing improvements. I&nbsp;&nbsp;
ve likely already accounted for those by removing&nbsp;
the cell to vehicle integration and anode material&nbsp;&nbsp;
from Tesla s list of cost reductions. That&nbsp;
means around 44% of the cost reduction still&nbsp;&nbsp;
stands as a good estimate for how much Tesla&nbsp;
can save by manufacturing batteries in house.
&nbsp;
Let s move on to the final, and most&nbsp;
important points about the 4680 and&nbsp;&nbsp;
Tesla s dry electrode coating process.
First, the 4680 is just a form factor,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so Tesla can fill that can with any chemistry such&nbsp;
as LFP or sodium ion. The potential exception is&nbsp;&nbsp;
solid state batteries, which typically have&nbsp;
a brittle separator that must be packaged in&nbsp;&nbsp;
a prismatic or pouch format so that it doesn t&nbsp;
have to be rolled, which would cause it to crack&nbsp;&nbsp;
and short circuit the battery cell.
Second, the dry electrode process is&nbsp;&nbsp;
also compatible with every major battery&nbsp;
chemistry and it s form factor agnostic.&nbsp;&nbsp;
That means after Tesla gets their high nickel&nbsp;
4680 into large scale production, there s&nbsp;&nbsp;
nothing stopping them from making dry electrode&nbsp;
LFP, sodium ion, or solid state batteries in&nbsp;&nbsp;
whatever form factor is best to give them more&nbsp;
options with regards to cost and performance.
&nbsp;
That point is worth reiterating because it&nbsp;
blows the obsolete narrative out of the water.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The dry electrode coating process isn t in&nbsp;
competition with other battery chemistries&nbsp;&nbsp;
because it s not a chemistry, it s a fundamental&nbsp;
improvement to the way batteries are manufactured.&nbsp;&nbsp;
That means eventually, all battery manufacturers&nbsp;
will switch to a dry electrode coating process.
&nbsp;
In summary, Tesla started the 4680&nbsp;
project for supply chain stability,&nbsp;&nbsp;
cost reductions, performance improvements.
&nbsp;
They ve achieved supply chain stability&nbsp;
and are already achieving cost reductions,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with more likely to come as they ramp&nbsp;
production of the 4680 supply chain&nbsp;&nbsp;
and deploy other chemistries like LFP.
As for performance, if that were Tesla s&nbsp;&nbsp;
only goal, they would have never started the&nbsp;
4680 project. That s because they could have&nbsp;&nbsp;
just purchased high performance battery cells&nbsp;
from other manufacturers and avoided half a&nbsp;&nbsp;
decade of manufacturing hell. But even when&nbsp;
given the chance, Tesla often doesn t purchase&nbsp;&nbsp;
the highest energy density and fastest charging&nbsp;
battery cells from their battery suppliers.
&nbsp;
Why? Because what matters the most to&nbsp;
customers is safety, price, and reliability,&nbsp;&nbsp;
followed by the quality of the charging network,&nbsp;
charging speed, and range for EV customers. But,&nbsp;&nbsp;
what s interesting about charging speed and range&nbsp;
is that most customers are only looking for a top&nbsp;&nbsp;
up in less than 30 minutes when fast charging&nbsp;
and more than 300 miles of real world range,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which most EV makers can now achieve.
As long as those minimums are met,&nbsp;&nbsp;
further performance increases have diminishing&nbsp;
returns when it comes to moving vehicle units,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and other considerations become more important.&nbsp;
Yes, there is a small customer segment that&nbsp;&nbsp;
wants the hottest technology in their car like&nbsp;
solid state batteries or as much fast charging&nbsp;&nbsp;
and range as possible, but it s a minority.
With all that said, due to the benefits of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
dry electrode coating process that I ve mentioned&nbsp;
in other videos, Tesla still has a great chance of&nbsp;&nbsp;
deploying battery cells that achieve much higher&nbsp;
energy density and faster charging speeds.
&nbsp;
Either way, ultimately, the entire battery&nbsp;
industry, all battery chemistries, and all battery&nbsp;&nbsp;
form factors were all eventually going to head in&nbsp;
the direction of dry electrode coating anyways,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it s just that Tesla, as a freshman&nbsp;
battery manufacturer, pulled a rabbit&nbsp;&nbsp;
out of the hat and got there first.
