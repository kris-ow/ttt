This episode of futureaza features a conversation with Phil Beisel, an early Rivian employee (California-based software hire, joined circa 2017) who worked on the R1T, discussing why software-defined vehicle architecture is so hard to build and why Tesla and Rivian are among the only companies that have done it well. The core thesis: Tesla and Rivian both built proprietary, vertically integrated hardware-and-software stacks from a clean sheet, in contrast to legacy automakers who bolt supplier-built infotainment systems onto old CAN-bus architectures — a structural gap Beisel argues legacy OEMs can never close without a top-down, CEO-level mandate to go vertical.

## Electric Vehicles
1. **Software-defined architecture as a Tesla/Rivian differentiator:** Beisel explains that Tesla and Rivian both designed their own high-end ECUs, electrical architecture, and networking (Ethernet-based, including Tesla's proprietary "Ether loop") rather than relying on supplier-integrated systems like traditional OEMs, giving them an "iPhone on wheels" model where hardware is a slave to software and nearly every ECU can receive over-the-air updates.
2. **Rivian's VW deal cited as validation:** Beisel notes Volkswagen sought to license or copy Rivian's software architecture, calling it a "bonus" to Rivian's reputation and evidence that legacy automakers see in-house vertical integration as unattainable on their own.
3. **Legacy automakers structurally can't catch up:** Beisel argues companies like Toyota will not meaningfully improve their software because they are integrators of supplier components rather than software companies; he claims only sustained top-level executive mandate (CEO or near-CEO level, comparable to what he says Musk has directed at Tesla) can force an organization to hold the line on full vertical integration against internal pushback to buy off-the-shelf supplier systems.
4. **Anecdote on Kia's light-show cost estimate:** The host relays a secondhand account from a Kia software engineer's post that, after Tesla introduced the light show feature on the Model X, a Kia executive asked what it would take to replicate it, and engineers estimated a cost around $27 million and 3–5 years — illustrating the cost of lacking in-house parts/software control (unconfirmed, secondhand account).
5. **Tesla vs. suppliers — "buys parts, not systems":** The host relays a claim from a Michigan-based auto parts/trucking-systems supplier that Tesla is more reliable than competitors using similar components because "Tesla buys parts, not systems" — i.e., Tesla integrates and controls the full system itself rather than buying pre-integrated modules; Beisel confirms this characterization and adds that Chinese automakers, being newer, have followed a similar clean-sheet approach unburdened by legacy systems.
6. **Phantom drain explained as a software/power-state challenge:** Beisel describes phantom battery drain as arising from the difficulty of managing power states across hundreds of ECUs in software-defined vehicles that stay network-connected (cellular/Wi-Fi/Bluetooth) for remote app access; he compares it to iPhone battery drain after a new iOS release, noting new vehicle software often introduces unexpected power bleed that takes engineering cycles to fully profile and fix. The host notes early Cybertrucks had phantom-drain issues severe enough that Level 1 charging couldn't keep up, since resolved by a software update.
7. **RFID key proximity drain (unconfirmed anecdote):** The host mentions hearing that a physical RFID key stored too close to a garage overnight can cause repeated wake events and drain, though he's unsure whether manufacturers have systematically addressed this.

Notable quotes and data:
- Beisel: "Tesla doesn't buy systems. Tesla buys parts. Tesla is the system" (relaying a parts supplier's explanation, which he confirms).
- Beisel on legacy OEM software: "it will not ever get better. Period. End of story" absent a full in-house vertical integration shift.
- Secondhand Kia anecdote (unconfirmed): replicating Tesla's light-show feature was estimated at roughly $27 million and 3–5 years.
- Beisel: over-the-air updates at Rivian/Tesla are frequent and comprehensive, while other manufacturers' OTA updates are "once a year, maybe less" and sometimes require a service-center visit.
- Host anecdote: early Cybertrucks experienced phantom drain severe enough that Level 1 (120V) charging never caught up to the drain rate, later fixed via software update.
- Beisel: car wake-up on app open works via a small always-listening cellular module receiving what he describes as effectively a private SMS message to wake the rest of the system.
