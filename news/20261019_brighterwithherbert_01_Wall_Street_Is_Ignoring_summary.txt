This video covers a series of statements Elon Musk made over the prior week responding to analysts and commentators about SpaceX, interpreted by host Herbert alongside guest Larry Goldberg (a serial entrepreneur and venture capitalist) as a string of bullish signals. The core thesis is that Wall Street is underpricing SpaceX's orbital/distributed computing ambitions (via the Terafab chip-manufacturing project and orbital data centers), while Starlink, Starship launch cadence, and data-center financing dynamics all point toward a much higher valuation than currently assigned.

## SpaceX — Corporate & Valuation
1. **Musk's $100 trillion decade-long valuation call:** Responding to Ron Baron's prediction of a $20–50 trillion valuation for SpaceX, Musk said that estimate was "too little" and suggested SpaceX is likely to exceed $100 trillion in under 10 years, barring a major world war. Goldberg pushed back on the timeline, saying he'd place that outcome more realistically 5–10 years further out, calling $100 trillion "a lot of money" to reach soon, though he added that reaching it would help resolve the U.S. deficit problem (a point Musk has made).
2. **Guest's zero-value treatment of Terafab in his own model:** Goldberg said that in his own SpaceX valuation (built via a Monte Carlo model with 20,000 simulations, discussed in a prior episode), he currently assigns Terafab zero value — treating it only as a capital cost — because its outcome is too uncertain to forecast, while acknowledging that if Terafab succeeds it "could be worth more than all of Elon's other companies combined" and could move SpaceX's valuation by an order of magnitude.
3. **Data-center debt market stress as a read-through risk:** The host and Goldberg discussed reports that CoreWeave's unsecured bonds are trading at a steep discount (reaching junk-like levels) and Oracle's unsecured bonds are trading similarly, which Musk used to draw a distinction between raising capital for complex power/cooling infrastructure and actually executing it reliably over time. Goldberg flagged financing risk for SpaceX's own orbital data-center buildout, noting unconfirmed reports of a roughly $40 billion debt deal rumored with Apollo, and listed SpaceX's main risks as: cost/financing of orbital data centers, cost of launching mass to orbit, and proving the technology actually works before lenders or customers commit money.

## SpaceX — AI & Compute
1. **Terafab framed as a top-20 GDP-scale chip project:** Musk described Terafab's output as potentially rivaling the economic output of a top-20 GDP country, and said it represents a step toward a civilization harnessing stellar energy (a Kardashev Type 2 reference). Goldberg agreed with the general scale claim (without independently verifying the figure) and said Terafab would be a vertically integrated, end-to-end chip manufacturing operation — from raw material to finished chip — potentially including partners like ASML, TSMC, Intel, and Nvidia, though no confirmed partnership was cited beyond this framing.
2. **Orbital distributed inference as SpaceX's most overlooked growth driver:** Morgan Stanley's Adam Jonas wrote that SpaceX's distributed cloud inference business — AI compute run from orbit — is the most under-appreciated part of the SpaceX story, a view Musk endorsed as "very big." A separate commentator (referred to as Aaron Burnett in the transcript) highlighted planned orbital compute racks using Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin chips, expected in the first quarter of next year, with ten chips per satellite rack and multiple satellites linked via laser into a coherent, single-computer-like structure for low-latency inference comparable to supercomputer-scale workloads.
3. **Unlimited scalability as the core value driver:** Goldberg argued that distributed computing in space is central to SpaceX's valuation because orbital compute capacity can be expanded essentially without limit, unlike ground-based data centers constrained by land, power, and cooling — a dynamic he said the market currently does not price in, since valuations today reflect only ground-based data center economics.

## SpaceX — Starlink
1. **Analyst revenue growth estimate endorsed by Musk:** An analyst estimate (shared by a commentator referred to as John Ehrlichman) projected Starlink revenue growing roughly 925% over five years (more than 10x), which Musk affirmed with a simple "of course."
2. **Jefferies aviation penetration forecast:** Jefferies estimates Starlink will be installed on about 23% of aircraft by 2028, up from roughly 9% currently, citing SpaceX's scale advantage (reported at 11,200+ satellites versus a few hundred for the nearest competitor), a lighter antenna (34 kg vs. 136 kg for legacy systems), and much faster installation times. United Airlines was cited as having Starlink on 40% of its fleet currently, expected to reach 100% by the end of 2027.
3. **Anticipated national competition, limited competitive threat:** Goldberg expects Europe, Russia, and China to build their own satellite internet networks, but believes SpaceX's operational efficiency means these competitors are unlikely to meaningfully dent Starlink's business, aside from political/market-access risk — he specifically cited India as a market Musk may have alienated for political reasons.

## SpaceX — Starship
1. **Starbase seeking major launch cadence increase:** SpaceX has applied to significantly increase Starship launch and landing frequency at its Starbase, Texas facility, with a reported target of up to 456 Starship flights per year, up from a current limit the host and guest recalled as somewhere around 25–50 flights annually with restricted days/hours. Goldberg noted logistical uncertainty around beach road access and closures required during launches, given Starbase sits on the only road to a public beach.
