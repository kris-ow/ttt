A California court ruled that Tesla engaged in deceptive marketing by calling its driver-assist software "Full Self-Driving" and "Autopilot," and authorized the California DMV to suspend Tesla's dealer/manufacturing licenses for 30 days if Tesla doesn't fix its marketing; the DMV has given Tesla 60 days to comply before taking action.

## Autonomous Driving / Market & Competition
1. **Court finds FSD name "unambiguously false":** The court ruled the term "Full Self-Driving" is "actually, unambiguously false and counterfactual," rejecting Tesla's argument that no reasonable person would interpret it literally. It found "Autopilot" not unambiguously false but used in "a long but unlawful tradition" of intentional ambiguity, noting a reasonable person could wrongly believe a car on Autopilot doesn't require constant driver attention.
2. **Other marketing language cited:** The court flagged Tesla copy stating its system "is designed to be able to conduct short and long distance trips with no action required by the person in the driver's seat," and suggestions that only "legal reasons" prevent full autonomy; it rejected Tesla's claim this was future-tense intent, citing the present-tense wording used.
3. **Potential 30-day suspension, DMV gives 60 days:** The court said the DMV may suspend or revoke Tesla's license to sell or build cars in California for 30 days if misleading marketing continues; the DMV said it will give Tesla 60 days to comply, focusing on Tesla's dealer license rather than manufacturing license. Tesla's Fremont factory builds roughly half a million vehicles a year and employs about 20,000 people. The court declined to order financial restitution, citing lack of established quantifiable harm. Tesla called the suspension remedy "draconian" and said after the ruling that "sales in California will continue uninterrupted."
4. **Background on the case:** The California DMV began investigating Tesla's FSD/Autopilot marketing claims in 2021 and sent a formal inquiry in 2022; California's legislature separately passed a law banning automakers from overstating autonomous capabilities. The article notes a separate California class-action lawsuit alleging Tesla misled customers about self-driving capabilities, which could be bolstered by this ruling's finding of fact.

## Editorial Stance
The Electrek author (Jameson Dow) argues Tesla has repeatedly deceived customers beyond naming — citing wrong timelines, alleged hardware capability misstatements, price changes, and continued hype around "Robotaxi" despite vehicles being only level-2 capable, asserting Tesla states robotaxis are operating in California when "by any definition, they are not." The author is skeptical the ruling will change Musk's or Tesla's public statements, drawing a comparison to a past SEC action requiring pre-screening of Musk's Tesla-relevant statements that "didn't seem to change his behavior much."
