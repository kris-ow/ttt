This video by Jordan Giesige of The Limiting Factor examines Tesla's fully dry electrode 4680 battery cell following Tesla's announcement that it can now coat electrodes using a dry process instead of a wet process. The central question is whether the 4680 is now obsolete given the rise of sodium ion and solid-state batteries, and the broader decline in lithium-ion battery prices since Battery Day. Giesige argues that the "obsolete" narrative fundamentally misunderstands both Tesla's original goals for the 4680 project and the nature of the dry electrode process itself.

## Electric Vehicles

1. **Tesla's Three Goals for the 4680 Project — In Priority Order:** Giesige frames the 4680 project around three goals ranked by importance: (1) supply chain stability — rebuilding a battery supply chain outside of China, which dominates not just cell production but also cell components and materials processing; (2) cost reduction — vertical integration offered the opportunity to cut battery costs by nearly 60% by eliminating supplier margins at each step of the supply chain; and (3) performance — producing high-performance battery cells. Performance is ranked last because Tesla always has the option to purchase best-in-class cells from third parties; supply chain disruption, by contrast, would halt production entirely.

2. **Progress Against Goals — What Tesla Has Actually Achieved:** Tesla has built an alternative supply chain for the 4680, eliminating the strategic supply chain risk. The 4680 is now described as the cheapest high-nickel battery cell available to Tesla in North America, though the cost savings are estimated at 10–20% rather than the originally projected 50–60%. Performance is described as comparable to Panasonic and LG Chem cells, but not yet best-in-class. Giesige notes it is still early days for Tesla as a cell manufacturer.

3. **Sodium Ion Batteries Cannot Replace the 4680 for Tesla Vehicles:** Sodium ion batteries do not meet the minimum range requirements for Tesla vehicles. Despite adequate gravimetric energy density, their volumetric energy density is lower, meaning EVs using sodium ion batteries can expect roughly one-third less range than those using iron-based lithium-ion batteries. This is acceptable for budget vehicles but not for an entry-level luxury vehicle like Tesla sells. Giesige notes, however, that Tesla could develop its own in-house sodium ion chemistry for grid storage applications and potentially for vehicles like the Cybercab that don't require long range.

4. **Solid-State Batteries Are Not Near-Term Displacement Threats:** Multiple companies claim they will have high-performance solid-state batteries on the market within one to two years. However, Giesige argues none of them are cheap enough or scalable enough for multi-million-unit mass-market vehicles. He does not expect solid-state batteries to significantly displace liquid electrolyte batteries like the 4680 in the next decade, especially given what BYD and CATL are achieving with LFP chemistries. He also notes that solid-state batteries typically require prismatic or pouch formats due to brittle separators that cannot be rolled — making them incompatible with the cylindrical 4680 can.

5. **Chinese Battery Competition Is Geographically Constrained:** The CATL Shenxing and BYD Blade 2.0 batteries are not "4680 killers" in the U.S. market due to import costs and legal, political, and financial barriers to direct Chinese ownership of U.S. factories. For Tesla's China-produced vehicles, Tesla already uses CATL cells and would likely adopt Shenxing batteries when ready if there are no major drawbacks. For European vehicles, Tesla can use a combination of the 4680 and whatever cells are available locally or for import.

6. **What Customers Actually Prioritize:** Giesige argues that the most important factors to customers are safety, price, and reliability, followed by charging network quality, charging speed, and range. Most customers want a top-up in under 30 minutes when fast charging and more than 300 miles of real-world range — thresholds most EV makers can now achieve. Beyond those minimums, further performance improvements yield diminishing returns for unit sales. Only a small minority of customers prioritize cutting-edge specs like solid-state batteries or maximum range.

## Energy

1. **The 4680 Is a Form Factor, Not a Chemistry — Enabling Future Flexibility:** The 4680 is a cylindrical can that can be filled with any chemistry, including LFP or sodium ion. This means Tesla's manufacturing infrastructure is not locked into high-nickel chemistry. The potential exception is solid-state batteries, which require prismatic or pouch packaging due to brittle separators that would crack if rolled.

2. **The Dry Electrode Process Is Chemistry- and Form-Factor-Agnostic:** The dry electrode coating process is compatible with every major battery chemistry and is not tied to any specific form factor. Once Tesla scales the high-nickel 4680, nothing prevents them from applying the dry electrode process to LFP, sodium ion, or solid-state batteries in whatever form factor is optimal. Giesige emphasizes this is the key point that undermines the "obsolete" narrative: the dry electrode process is not a competing chemistry — it is a fundamental manufacturing improvement that the entire battery industry will eventually adopt.

## Market & Competition

1. **The Durability of Tesla's Cost Reduction Estimates:** Giesige breaks down the original Battery Day cost reduction projections by source. He identifies which savings are still durable: 14% from better cell design (durable, as competitors haven't changed cell design significantly); 18% from dry electrode coating and other manufacturing improvements (largely durable until competitors adopt the process); 12% from in-house cathode and lithium production (durable, as it eliminates supplier margins). Items he crosses off as no longer uniquely durable: 5% from adding silicon anode (achievable by any manufacturer) and 7% from cell-to-vehicle integration (competitors have replicated this). His estimate is that up to 44% of the original 56% cost reduction target remains achievable and durable.

2. **What Actually Drove Industry-Wide Battery Price Declines:** Giesige argues that much of the lithium-ion battery cost reduction seen over the six years since Battery Day was driven by market conditions — lower materials costs, overcapacity, intense price competition, and a shift toward cheaper LFP chemistries — rather than fundamental manufacturing improvements. He estimates less than one-third of the industry-wide cost reduction came from genuine manufacturing advances, and that Tesla can benefit from some of those same component cost reductions (e.g., cheaper electrode foils, separators, and electrolyte). He concludes that approximately 44% cost savings from in-house manufacturing remains a sound estimate.

3. **Tesla as a First-Mover in Dry Electrode Manufacturing:** Giesige concludes that the entire battery industry — across all chemistries and form factors — was always heading toward dry electrode coating. Tesla, as a relatively new battery manufacturer, achieved this first, giving it a durable manufacturing advantage that extends beyond any single chemistry or form factor.
