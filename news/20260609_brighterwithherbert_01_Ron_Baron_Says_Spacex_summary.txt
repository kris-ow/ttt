This video from the "brighterwithherbert" channel discusses the SpaceX IPO (scheduled for that Friday, around June 9, 2026) and the extraordinary valuation projections coming from Ron Baron, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley. Host Herbert is joined by guest Jeff Lutz, a former supply chain C-level executive at Motorola, Google, and Lenovo who now runs his own consulting firm serving mega-cap tech companies. The central thesis is that SpaceX's unique combination of reusable rockets, space-based AI data centers, Starlink, and XAI positions it as potentially the most valuable company ever — though both hosts acknowledge the frenzy of IPO-week hype warrants skepticism.

## Market & Competition

1. **Ron Baron's $10–$30 Trillion SpaceX Valuation:** Billionaire investor Ron Baron, speaking ahead of the SpaceX IPO, stated that SpaceX — initially expected to be valued at under $2 trillion at IPO — will ultimately be worth $10 trillion, $20 trillion, or even $30 trillion, and that it will become the largest, most profitable company on the planet. Baron added that people inside SpaceX believe he is lowballing that estimate. Elon Musk responded to Baron's remarks simply by saying "Ron is smart," and separately appeared to suggest the scale could extend beyond the planet — "largest in the sector of the galaxy." Baron also noted he personally cannot imagine ever selling his SpaceX (or Tesla) stock, and is urging friends and executives of portfolio companies to invest at IPO.

2. **Ron Baron's Existing SpaceX Position and IPO Plans:** By some estimates, Baron Capital Group and its various funds hold approximately $15 billion worth of SpaceX. Baron has publicly stated he plans to purchase an additional $1 billion worth at IPO, reflecting his conviction that this is "the greatest idea" he has ever encountered as an investor.

3. **Goldman Sachs Revenue Projections:** Goldman Sachs, one of the lead bookrunners for the SpaceX IPO, projects that SpaceX revenue will grow roughly 100 times — from approximately $3.2 billion in AI-related revenue in 2025 to $322 billion by 2030. Their broader SpaceX revenue projection reaches $474 billion by 2030, with Starlink contributing approximately $144 billion of that and rocket launch revenue contributing approximately $8.8 billion. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley both project SpaceX revenue near $160 billion by 2028. Both hosts noted the timing of these projections — released in the days immediately before the IPO by the very banks earning fees on the deal — warrants caution.

4. **Morgan Stanley Revenue Projections:** Morgan Stanley projects SpaceX revenue reaching $3.4 trillion by 2040, with profits of approximately $2.7 trillion, calling it an unprecedented revenue figure for any company. Jeff Lutz noted that while the AI buildout numbers are extraordinary, the 2040 timeframe makes them harder to dismiss outright, drawing a parallel to how AI infrastructure projections from five years ago would have seemed equally absurd.

5. **XAI Losses and the Google/Anthropic Deals:** Goldman Sachs's model notes that XAI lost $6.4 billion in 2025. However, both hosts noted that the recently announced deals with Google and Anthropic for SpaceX's space-based data center capacity appear to offset those losses, and that the XAI total addressable market is claimed at $26.5 trillion, versus $2 trillion for Starlink and space launch combined — meaning the bull case is fundamentally an XAI/data center story.

6. **SpaceX's Competitive Moat:** Jeff Lutz argued that SpaceX's core moat is its manufacturing capability for reusable rockets — a capability no competitor has yet replicated at scale. He pointed to Blue Origin/Amazon as a potential future competitor but noted the gap remains large. He also observed that many automotive companies attempted in-house autonomy programs and abandoned them, and that companies relying on Nvidia for AI infrastructure may be in a "pretty big bind." The hosts framed SpaceX's moat as: reusable rockets → exclusive pathway to space → space-based AI data centers → access to near-unlimited solar energy → largest possible AI models → compounding intelligence advantage.

7. **Elon Musk Key-Man Risk:** Ron Baron identified Elon Musk as the single biggest risk to the SpaceX investment thesis. Jeff Lutz offered a nuanced assessment: Musk's decade-long master plans, long-tenured direct reports at both SpaceX and Tesla, and well-articulated product portfolios provide meaningful continuity. However, Lutz identified specific initiatives that remain dependent on Musk's direct involvement — particularly Terafab (described as "very early") and SpaceX AI data centers (described as still being figured out) — while characterizing robotaxi as "solved" and Starship reusability as essentially a "done deal." Both hosts agreed the companies would suffer a valuation hit in a key-man scenario but would not collapse.

8. **SpaceX/Tesla Merger Speculation:** Ron Baron was asked directly about a potential SpaceX-Tesla merger. He said there have been no public announcements and that he defers entirely to Elon's judgment — if Elon says merge, Baron will support it and explain why; if Elon says don't merge, Baron will support that too. Jeff Lutz noted that whether the $10–$30 trillion figure refers to SpaceX alone or a combined SpaceX-Tesla entity is a critical and unanswered question — a combined entity including Tesla's physical AI and Optimus would make those numbers "not inconceivable at all." Both hosts also noted what appeared to be a Freudian slip by Ron Baron, who said "I can't imagine I'm ever going to sell my stock in Tesla" when apparently meaning SpaceX, before correcting himself — mirroring a similar reported slip by Elon Musk in a JP Morgan interview.

9. **China Risk and Geopolitical Dynamics:** The hosts identified China as a top-tier risk and unknown for both SpaceX and Tesla. Jeff Lutz argued that China is unlikely to lock Tesla or Optimus out of the Chinese market because doing so is more detrimental to China than to Elon's companies — "China needs Tesla more than Tesla needs China." He also argued that the U.S. government, not China, may be the more likely party to restrict export of physical AI (including Optimus) to China, drawing a parallel to ongoing U.S. government restrictions on Nvidia chips and AI model exports. Both hosts agreed SpaceX would never make any military or national-defense-related deal with China.

10. **National Defense and Government Contract Potential:** Jeff Lutz noted that while SpaceX currently derives only a small single-digit percentage of its contracts from government, the product lines have clear national defense correlaries — Star Shield being the government version of Starlink — and he speculated there could be many undisclosed or future government business units. He also raised the possibility of Tesla and/or SpaceX entering the drone manufacturing space given the U.S. shortage of reusable drone manufacturing capacity, though he noted neither company is obligated to pursue that market.
